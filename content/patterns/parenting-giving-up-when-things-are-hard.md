## 1. A recognizable situation

Your child tries a maths problem, gets stuck, and pushes the page away. “I can’t do it.”

“You haven’t really tried,” you reply. “Just think about it.”

They become upset. You demonstrate the solution, hoping that seeing it will help. They watch, then ask you to do the next one too.

It can be difficult to tell what needs changing. Does the child need encouragement, clearer teaching, a break, more independence, or help with a difficulty that has gone unnoticed?

**The useful goal is effective persistence: recognizing when to try again, change strategy, seek help, pause, or reconsider the task.** Continuing longer is valuable when it creates an opportunity to learn. Repeating an unsuccessful action indefinitely may accomplish very little.

A recent theoretical review frames children’s persistence as a developing judgment about their capabilities and whether effort is worthwhile, shaped through interactions with caregivers. This is a useful organizing idea, rather than a tested formula for responding to every child. [Leonard and Shachnai, 2026](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/09637214251401848)

## 2. What might be happening

“I can’t” can describe several different problems.

| Possible difficulty | Observations that may help distinguish it |
|---|---|
| **A missing skill or unclear instruction** | The child cannot explain the task or identify a first step, even when calm. |
| **Too many demands at once** | They understand individual steps but lose track when reading, remembering, organizing, and writing must happen together. |
| **Fear of being wrong** | They erase repeatedly, avoid unfamiliar tasks, or interpret mistakes as evidence that they are incapable. |
| **Frustration without an alternative strategy** | They repeat the same unsuccessful action, become upset, and stop. |
| **Low confidence that effort will help** | They expect another failure, particularly in an area where they have repeatedly struggled. |
| **A task with little meaning or ownership** | They persist in some activities but disengage quickly from this one. |
| **Fatigue, discomfort, or an unsuitable setting** | The difficulty changes with timing, noise, physical demands, or the length of the session. |

These are hypotheses to investigate. A child’s ability to concentrate on a favorite activity does not establish that they possess all the skills needed for a different one.

Look at where the stopping happens. Is it before beginning, after the first error, when instructions become complicated, or after substantial effort that the adult has not noticed?

Consider variation across days too. An observational study followed toothbrushing in 81 three-year-olds and found day-to-day variation in persistence, with relationships involving mood, sleep, and parent stress differing across children. The study concerned one daily activity and cannot establish what causes a particular child’s difficulty with schoolwork. It does caution against treating persistence as an entirely fixed characteristic. [Leonard and colleagues, 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34904237/)

## 3. How development changes the picture

**Toddlers and preschoolers need help that is visible and immediate.** A demonstration, a gesture, or assistance with one physical step may be more useful than discussing motivation. A young child struggling to connect construction pieces may need to see how the pieces align before another attempt can be productive.

Young children also learn from observing adults. Experiments with 15-month-olds found that watching an adult work through difficulty increased attempts on a subsequent task. This shows an immediate experimental effect; it does not demonstrate a lasting change in character or predict later achievement. [Leonard and colleagues, 2017](https://dspace.mit.edu/entities/publication/20eb68c8-36d9-4b4a-b093-84384ba532b1)

**Primary-school children can increasingly identify obstacles and compare strategies**, but they may still need an adult to organize the process. “Show me the last part that made sense” offers a more concrete starting point than “Be more resilient.”

**Older children and adolescents need increasing ownership of goals and methods.** They can participate more in deciding what preparation is needed, when to seek feedback, and whether an optional activity remains worthwhile. Their greater verbal ability should not be mistaken for mastery of every task they encounter.

These are practical starting expectations, not developmental deadlines. Language, learning differences, motor abilities, stress, and prior experience affect what support is appropriate.

## 4. What needs balancing

The child needs opportunities to act, make mistakes, and experience improvement. They also need adequate teaching, emotional safety, and help when a demand exceeds their current abilities.

The parent may need homework completed, shoes put on, or a family responsibility carried out. There may be little time available for patient experimentation.

Distinguish the purpose of the activity:

- **For necessary tasks**, the goal is an accessible route to participation, with support where needed.

- **For learning tasks**, the goal is understanding and growing capability.

- **For optional interests**, the child’s preferences deserve substantial weight, alongside any commitments already made.

An adult can help with a zipper when the bus is arriving and arrange practice during a quieter moment. Every rushed morning need not become a test of independence.

Likewise, asking for help can be a successful response to difficulty. The question is whether the help enables learning and participation, and whether its form can change as the child becomes more capable.

## 5. Possible responses

The following approaches combine research, professional guidance, and practical synthesis. Their specific examples are illustrations, not independently tested scripts.

### A. Find the obstacle before asking for more effort

When the child is able to engage, ask them to show you what happens:

“Which part stopped making sense?”

“Show me what you tried.”

“Is it working out the answer, remembering the steps, or writing it down?”

For a younger child, observe and offer something concrete: “These pieces keep slipping. Shall I hold this part while you connect them?”

Then adjust the demand while preserving its purpose. That might mean demonstrating an unfamiliar method, clarifying vocabulary, separating a task into stages, or changing an awkward tool.

There is strong educational support for systematic instruction in specific contexts. The US Institute of Education Sciences recommends systematic teaching, mathematical language, and representations for elementary pupils struggling with maths. This supports investigating instructional needs; it does not establish that breaking every task into smaller pieces will solve every persistence difficulty. [IES practice guide](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/PracticeGuide/26/Published)

For schoolwork, coordinate with the teacher. The task may be intended to reveal what the child cannot yet do independently. Completing or heavily correcting it can obscure that information.

### B. Offer enough help for the child to make the next move

Begin with support that addresses the obstacle, then watch what the child can do.

A hint may be enough. Sometimes a full demonstration is necessary. After demonstrating, give the child a related opportunity to act rather than automatically continuing for them.

For example, help a child position the first puzzle piece and let them search for the next. With writing, discuss an opening sentence and have the child decide how to express it. Reduce support as competence grows; increase it when the child is still genuinely stuck.

Experiments provide a reason to be cautious about routinely taking over. Two preregistered studies involving 150 four- and five-year-olds found that children whose task was completed by an adult persisted less on a subsequent task than children in teaching or baseline conditions. This was a short-term laboratory finding. It does not establish that ordinary parental assistance causes a lasting lack of persistence. [Leonard and colleagues, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.13492)

The distinction concerns what the help makes possible. Finishing something for a child and teaching them how to approach it are different experiences.

### C. Demonstrate a workable response to difficulty and give useful feedback

“Keep trying” becomes more informative when it includes something to try.

You might model changing an approach: “Pulling harder isn’t opening this. I’m going to check the catch.” Or identify what the child discovered: “That piece didn’t fit when it faced that way. Now we know something.”

Feedback should make progress or strategy visible. “You checked the instructions after the first attempt” gives the child information about an action they can repeat.

Across four experiments involving 520 four- and five-year-olds, children’s persistence was influenced by adults’ actions, outcomes, and statements. Observing adult success mattered substantially; modeled effort helped when the adult ultimately succeeded. These findings suggest that children attend to evidence that effort can work, rather than simply copying prolonged struggle. They do not prove that parents should stage difficulties or hide their own failures. [Leonard and colleagues, 2020](https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.13305)

In practice, demonstrate a genuine strategy when one is available. If you do not know how to solve something, model seeking information or deciding to return later.

### D. Agree on a manageable attempt and a way to reassess

An open-ended instruction such as “You’re staying here until it’s done” provides no clear route out of being stuck.

Instead, agree on a bounded next step: work through one example together, attempt a related problem, then review what is understood. A younger child may need the adult to organize this. An older child can help choose the sequence, break, and source of assistance.

For homework, Nationwide Children’s recommends clear expectations about work before breaks and helping children communicate with the teacher when they still do not understand. This is professional guidance, not evidence for a universally effective timer or work–break ratio. [Homework guidance](https://www.nationwidechildrens.org/family-resources-education/health-wellness-and-safety-resources/helping-hands/homework-help-tips-for-families)

Keep choices genuine. A break should not secretly become another test, and a review should allow changes when the plan is failing.

Autonomy support is associated with positive learning outcomes, particularly motivation, engagement, and self-beliefs. A large meta-analysis covered elementary school through university, but its correlational evidence cannot establish that offering choices will cause a particular child to persist. [Mammadov and Schroeder, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cedpsych.2023.102235)

## 6. Examples in practice

**A preschool construction project**

A four-year-old wants to build a tall tower. The base repeatedly collapses. Their parent says, “You can do it!” The child throws down a block.

The parent acknowledges the frustration, stops the throwing, and examines the structure with them. They demonstrate a wider base and invite the child to add the next layer.

The tower falls again. The child remains upset and stops for now. The parent leaves the materials available for another attempt. The session has produced some information and a demonstrated strategy, even though it has not produced a finished tower.

**A child who can explain ideas but avoids writing**

An eight-year-old abandons written assignments almost immediately. Their parent initially interprets this as unwillingness to think.

When asked to explain an answer aloud, the child provides a detailed response. Writing it down remains painfully slow. The parent discusses the discrepancy with the teacher and asks about handwriting demands, spelling, and appropriate support.

Reducing the physical writing demand may help reveal what the child understands, but any adaptation should fit the assignment’s purpose. A task assessing handwriting needs a different plan from one assessing knowledge of science.

**An adolescent who wants to leave an activity**

A thirteen-year-old asks to stop music lessons after struggling with a new piece. Rather than deciding immediately that this is avoidance, the parent explores the difficulty.

The child still enjoys music but dislikes the current repertoire and finds the practice schedule exhausting. They agree to discuss a different piece and a more manageable plan with the teacher, then review the arrangement.

The child may continue, change how they participate, or eventually stop. A considered decision is a different outcome from abandoning the activity in the first moment of frustration.

## 7. Common mistakes and limitations

**Turning encouragement into disbelief.** “Of course you can” may contradict the child’s experience. Identify the obstacle before assuring them it is manageable.

**Confusing repetition with learning.** More attempts help when they provide practice or new information. Repeating an unknown procedure can simply rehearse confusion.

**Treating help as failure.** Refusing useful assistance can make the task unnecessarily inaccessible. Independence can develop through supported practice.

**Making effort the only explanation for outcomes.** Instruction, resources, accessibility, time, and opportunity matter. Children should not be held responsible for overcoming every obstacle through determination.

**Treating growth mindset as a complete solution.** The evidence is contested. One 2023 meta-analysis found negligible academic effects after accounting for study quality and potential bias. Another found small benefits in targeted groups under stronger implementation conditions. Neither establishes that adding “yet” to “I can’t do it” will resolve an individual child’s difficulty. These studies evaluated interventions and academic outcomes, not isolated parental phrases or every aspect of persistence. [Macnamara and Burgoyne](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36326645/); [Burnette and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36227318/)

**Overlooking the setting.** An overloaded school schedule, unreliable equipment, a crowded home, or an application that abruptly increases difficulty may need attention. Changing the parent’s language cannot remove those barriers by itself.

## 8. What to observe over time

Look for a broader repertoire of responses to difficulty:

- The child can identify where they are stuck.

- They try a different strategy rather than repeating the same action.

- They ask for more specific help.

- They return after an agreed break.

- They complete a useful part of the task with gradually changing support.

- They retain curiosity, enjoyment, and willingness to attempt unfamiliar things.

Compare similar tasks over time. Longer work sessions are not sufficient evidence of progress if understanding, wellbeing, or the parent–child relationship is deteriorating.

Seek further assessment when difficulties are persistent, concentrated in particular skills, or substantially interfere with learning and daily life. The AAP identifies frustration during learning among possible signs of a learning difficulty and recommends discussing concerns with professionals. Giving up is not itself a diagnosis. [AAP guidance on learning differences](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/health-issues/conditions/learning-disabilities/Pages/Learning-Disabilities-What-Parents-Need-To-Know.aspx)

Parents can also ask for help because homework has become a recurring source of distress. It is useful to bring concrete examples: what the child can do, where they stop, what support changes the outcome, and how much time the task takes.
