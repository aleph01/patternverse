Practice becomes easier when the next problem looks like the previous one. If you spend twenty minutes repeating one dance step, one grammatical construction, or one type of chess position, you rarely have to decide what kind of situation you are facing. The task has already been identified for you.

Interleaving deliberately mixes related kinds of practice. Instead of working through all examples of A before moving to B and C, the learner encounters A, C, B, A, B, C and has to identify what each case requires.

That extra decision matters. A birdwatcher ultimately needs to distinguish a chaffinch from several similar birds, not identify twenty photographs already grouped under the heading “chaffinch.” A language learner has to choose the appropriate tense in conversation, not merely complete a worksheet in which every blank requires the same tense.

This is why interleaving can initially make practice feel worse. Repetition creates rhythm and confidence. Switching breaks that rhythm. The learner hesitates, makes more mistakes, and may feel less fluent even while learning something more useful.

The hidden skill being practiced is discrimination. Before applying a method, movement, concept, or category, the learner first has to recognize what kind of situation this is. In many real settings, that recognition is as important as execution itself.

Interleaving works particularly well when the items are related enough to be confused. Mixing watercolor painting with French vocabulary and tennis serves would mostly create inconvenience. Mixing several related brush techniques, several grammatical constructions, or several species of similar trees can sharpen perception because the learner has to notice which features actually matter.

Blocked practice still has a place. When something is entirely new, a short period of repetition can help establish the basic form. Someone learning a dance step may need to repeat it several times before mixing it with others. Interleaving becomes more valuable once the problem shifts from “Can I do this?” to “Do I know when this applies?”

Real life rarely labels the next situation. Interleaving makes practice a little more honest by removing that label.
