*Preserve the appearance of strength while designing how an object fails.*

A chair splinters during a fight. A vase shatters as it hits the floor. A performer crashes through something that looked solid a moment earlier.

Ordinary objects are built to survive handling and resist damage. That makes their behavior poorly suited to a scene requiring a particular break at a particular moment.

**A breakaway prop is made to resemble an ordinary object while breaking more readily under its intended action.** Materials, weight, construction, and finish are chosen together so that the object looks convincing before, during, and after its destruction.

The audience sees an apparently powerful impact. The production has designed the object’s response.

## The puzzle: how can the same vase be delicate to film and easy to replace?


A vase can have a consistent shape, color, and surface finish across shots. Yet the version used for a detailed handling shot need not be the one that breaks.

A production can use a detailed **hero version** for close views, a matched breakaway for the destructive action, and separately prepared fragments for the aftermath.

A hero prop is the version prioritized for its appearance on camera; it does not necessarily have to be a genuine antique or an ordinary manufactured object.

The important continuity is what the viewer recognizes: silhouette, decoration, proportions, labels, and wear.

**One fictional object can be represented by several physical versions, each built for a different job.**

Manufacturers supply precisely this range. Breakaway Effects describes matching breakaways, real “twins,” and plastic or rubber versions of selected objects. [Its catalogue shows the distinctions.](https://breakawayfx.com/)

## How the object’s behavior changes
There is no single material called “breakaway.”

Glass-like bottles and tableware may use brittle casting materials that reproduce the appearance of glass or ceramic. Furniture can use lightweight wood and construction suited to the intended collapse. Apparent masonry can be represented by lightweight, fragile cast material.

The finish supplies much of the visual identity. An object can look like varnished hardwood, glazed pottery, or heavy masonry while having very different mechanical properties.

Prop maker Ryan Johnson describes this work as providing alternatives to everyday objects for kinetic action. His shop produces matched versions through molding and casting, including lightweight masonry effects. [His interview explains the fabrication approach.](https://behindthelensonline.net/site/interviews/interview-exclusives/ryan-johnson-new-rule-fx-exclusive-interview/)

A soft stunt double has another purpose again. A rubber object that bends and survives contact is not necessarily a breakaway. The required behavior determines which version belongs in the shot.

## Is “sugar glass” actually sugar?

The familiar name can obscure the material.

NewRuleFX explains that its professional breakaway glass uses a plastic resin formulation, although the older expression “sugar glass” remains common. Different suppliers and applications can use different materials, so the nickname does not identify an object’s composition.

[The manufacturer’s explanation distinguishes the term from its current products.](https://www.newrulefx.com/pages/breakaway-glass-props)

For understanding the screen effect, the useful questions concern appearance and behavior: how the object handles, how it fractures, and what remains afterward.

### Example: a wooden chair—how can it look sturdy and then come apart during action?

A convincing chair suggests joints and material capable of supporting weight. A breakaway version needs that visual credibility while serving a different performance requirement.

NewRuleFX’s documented examples use balsa wood to reproduce familiar chair designs. Their appearance suggests ordinary furniture, while their low weight and intended breakage behavior suit stunt work.

The illusion depends partly on handling. If the object seems nearly weightless before the action begins, its destruction may lose force. The performer’s movement and the camera’s framing help establish the expected substance.

This is a reusable version of the pattern: **match the recognizable design, then substitute the physical behavior needed for the scene.**

[The manufacturer’s folding-chair example includes photographs and describes its intended use.](https://www.newrulefx.com/products/newrulefx-brand-smashprops-breakaway-balsa-wood-folding-chair-smashable-stunt-action-prop)

### Example: *Die Another Day* (2002)—how can a car burst through part of an ice palace?

The principle also applies to larger scenic elements.

Breakaway Effects documents the *Die Another Day* ice-palace sequence in which an Aston Martin drove through a breakaway glazed structure. The supplier discusses the production in its account of large breakaway sheets and structures.

Here, the apparent obstacle has to establish enough solidity to make the vehicle’s passage dramatic. Its construction must also accommodate the planned action and the resulting debris.

The underlying task is related to the vase or chair, but the coordination expands: the structure, vehicle, stunt movement, camera position, and cleanup all become part of the event.

[The supplier’s account identifies the film application and illustrates its larger-scale breakaway work.](https://breakawayfx.com/about-us/)

### Example: *John Wick: Chapter 3—Parabellum* (2019)—which glass actually breaks?

*John Wick: Chapter 3 – Parabellum (2019) — Published VFX breakdown image.* The glass-office fight combined physical staging with extensive digital glass. It is a useful boundary case: an apparent breakaway may be substituted debris, a separately photographed element, or simulation rather than a prop visibly breaking on set. [Image source](https://beforesandafters.com/2019/09/16/everything-you-need-to-know-about-the-breaking-glass-in-john-wick-3-part-1/). Credit: Production/VFX imagery published by befores & afters.

The film’s glass-heavy fights demonstrate how practical breakage can be combined with other methods.

Production visual effects supervisor Rob Nederhorst explained that some cabinet breakages were photographed practically, while much of the apparent glass destruction was created digitally. The antiques-store fight used digitally added glass, with performers acting through the intended breaking actions.

For a separate floor-breaking sequence, rubberized debris represented glass already on the ground, while digital fragments completed the falling material.

This example extends the idea of different versions serving different jobs. An intact surface, its moment of fracture, and debris around performers can each receive a separate solution.

It also shows why an apparent glass smash should not automatically be identified as “sugar glass.” The finished image may combine practical objects, substituted debris, and simulated breakage.

[Nederhorst’s interview includes production imagery and explains the different approaches.](https://beforesandafters.com/2019/09/16/everything-you-need-to-know-about-the-breaking-glass-in-john-wick-3-part-1/)

## The break has to tell the right story
Easy breakage alone is insufficient.

A vase that crumbles before reaching the floor reveals its fragility too soon. A chair that separates into conspicuously neat components may look assembled for the trick. A supposedly substantial object that floats through the action can undermine the impact.

The required result depends on the scene. A comic collapse, a destructive outburst, and a fight need different rhythms and amounts of visible damage.

Sound contributes strongly. The recorded action can be supplemented with a crash, splintering, or fragments settling, helping the object acquire the substance suggested by its appearance.

Editing can establish the object clearly, emphasize the decisive moment, and connect it to the aftermath.

## The aftermath is another effects problem
The fragments left after a take are not automatically suitable for every subsequent shot.

A close view may require recognizable pieces of the original decoration. A wide shot may need the broken object to remain legible in the scenery. Continued physical action around the debris may call for different materials again.

Continuity includes where the pieces landed, what the performer holds, and which parts remain attached.

Replacement objects are also essential for repeated takes. Each usable break consumes a version of the prop, and the next attempt must begin with a convincing match.

## What “breakaway” does—and does not—promise
A breakaway reduces resistance or changes failure behavior for an intended action. It still requires testing and coordination for that action. Fragments, remaining rigid sections, and unexpected failure can remain hazards.

Professional prop, effects, and stunt teams therefore treat the object as part of the choreography. Its behavior must suit the performer’s movement and the shot being photographed.

Sometimes digital destruction or a separately filmed effects element offers a better solution. The strongest approach may divide the event among several methods, as the *John Wick* example demonstrates.
