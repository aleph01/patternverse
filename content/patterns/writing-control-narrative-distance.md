A story can tell us exactly what a character feels while keeping us at a considerable distance from the feeling. Another passage can stay so close to each hesitation and sensation that the reader loses the larger situation. Neither problem is solved simply by adding more description or removing every explanatory sentence.

This pattern helps when an important encounter feels remote, routine moments receive exhausting interior attention, or a change from overview to immediate thought feels unprepared. **Choose how closely the prose follows the character's experience, and let changes of distance support the passage's purpose.**

Narrative distance concerns the relationship between the telling and the experience being told. At a greater distance, a narrator can name, summarize, contextualize, or reflect. Closer in, wording and rhythm may take on the character's immediate perceptions and thought. These are available positions, not a ladder on which the closest point is best.

## Distinguish knowing a feeling from encountering it

A narrator can report an inner state without making the prose resemble the character's own experience. That distinction is useful when deciding what a moment needs.

Imagine a fictional writer, Eleni, waiting to give her first public reading. She is one of eight readers at a library event. The host has reached her name:

> Eleni waited beside the platform while the host introduced her. She had spent six years writing alone and was nervous about reading to strangers. She hoped the pages would not shake in her hands.

The narrator identifies the situation, supplies its history, and reports her concern. Readers know how she feels. That degree of explanation may be exactly right if the reading is a brief transition or the narrator's reflective account is important.

A closer version can let the concern enter the language more directly:

> Her name came through the speaker, bigger than it had any right to be. Time to move. If she could just keep the pages from rattling against the microphone.

The description of the amplified name belongs to Eleni's response rather than an objective measurement of its volume. The fragments carry anticipation without repeatedly announcing that she thought or hoped something. The passage lets the reader encounter the moment through a particular pressure on her attention.

The revision also gives up information. It does not supply the six years of private writing or identify the venue. If that context has not been established, a reader may understand the immediate discomfort but miss why the reading matters. Closeness can draw on orientation supplied elsewhere; it cannot make that orientation unnecessary.

## Move through a change of attention

A passage can move from a wider account toward immediate experience without changing its viewpoint holder. What changes is how the telling relates to that person's moment.

In Eleni's scene, a paragraph might begin with the library's arrangement and the order of readers, narrow to the host introducing her, and then reach her response to hearing her name. The introduction provides a local reason for the prose to move closer. The reader can follow the event while the language changes its distance.

There is no requirement to pass through a fixed set of stages. A sudden close thought can be an effective interruption if the context makes it intelligible. A gradual movement can become laborious if every minor event receives a ceremonial approach.

Moving outward also needs a purpose. After the first few lines of the reading, the narrator might report that Eleni settled into a steady pace and finished without looking up. That overview can carry the passage toward the next consequential encounter. Remaining inside every word she pronounces would give the performance much more space and importance.

The useful choice concerns what readers should experience closely and what they need to understand from a broader position.

## Let reflection have its own authority

First-person narration does not erase distance. A person telling a story later can stand apart from the self who lived it, even while using _I_ throughout.

Consider a separate invented first-person account of leaving a hometown at nineteen:

> I thought the ticket proved I could manage alone. Only later did I learn that my aunt had quietly paid the deposit on the room waiting for me.

The narrator connects an earlier belief with information learned afterward. That reflection makes a relationship available which the nineteen-year-old could not yet recognize. Removing it to keep everything immediate would change what the story knows.

A closer account of the departure might stay with the ticket in a pocket, the platform announcement, and the young person's determination not to turn around. That could convey the experience of leaving, but it would not by itself reveal the hidden support. The narrator can choose when the later understanding should enter.

The shift needs a readable position in time. _Only later did I learn_ makes the source of the new knowledge explicit. Without such orientation, the passage might accidentally give the younger self an insight the story says was unavailable then.

## Choose what greater distance makes possible

Overview can do more than hurry through unimportant events. It can compare experiences, preserve irony, establish social context, or let the narrator make a judgment. A restrained account can also leave emotional room that a continuous stream of immediate thought would fill.

Conversely, closeness need not mean maximum sensation or a transcription of every thought. Eleni's worry about rattling pages is selective. The prose does not need to name her pulse, breathing, shoes, and every sound in the audience to follow her immediate concern.

Short sentences do not automatically make narration close, and long sentences do not automatically make it distant. A long, unfolding sentence can follow a character's thought intimately; a short declaration can summarize years. The vocabulary, knowledge, judgments, and temporal position matter along with the syntax.

Nor does moving closer require entering another person's mind. The same Eleni can be described from a contextual overview, through reported feeling, or through language coloured by her immediate experience. Changing the person whose knowledge organizes the passage is a separate decision.

## Keep the telling accountable to its material

In nonfiction, closeness must not become permission to invent a real person's thoughts. A subject's account of what they feared can support attributed interior experience. It does not necessarily provide their exact words of thought at a particular second.

Memoir also involves a distinction between remembered experience and later interpretation. A narrator can acknowledge uncertainty or identify what was learned afterward. Moving outward to make those limits clear may be more faithful than presenting a fully reconstructed inner monologue.

In fiction, the author can invent Eleni's immediate language. The remaining question is whether the movement belongs to the established narrator and whether the information remains coherent. The reader needs to follow who is experiencing, who is telling, and from what position the understanding comes.
