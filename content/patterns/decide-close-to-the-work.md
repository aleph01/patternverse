**Core question:** Which decisions can people nearest the work make well, and which need a wider view or authority?

## What it is for

Imagine a hypothetical equipment-rental company. A customer arrives to collect a machine that has failed its morning check. The depot team knows which replacement is available and what the customer needs. Yet an ordinary substitution requires approval from a regional manager who is traveling between sites.

The team can explain the situation repeatedly while the customer waits. Moving the choice upward adds little knowledge in this case, but the same depot should not independently take equipment already promised by another branch.

Decide Close to the Work helps organizations distinguish those situations. It applies across operational teams, professional groups, departments, and regional units. The aim is to locate authority where relevant knowledge and usable judgment meet, while accounting for consequences beyond the immediate task.

## The core idea

**Place each decision with the nearest capable person or group that can understand its consequences and act within a clear mandate.**

“Close” means close to the information, feedback, and people affected. It need not mean the lowest job grade or the nearest physical location. A specialist supporting several sites may be better placed than either a local novice or a distant executive.

The presumption favors local decisions when the necessary conditions are present. Broader authority remains useful for shared resources, conflicting commitments, or consequences the local mandate does not cover. Sometimes the answer is to bring expertise and information closer, rather than transfer the choice upward.

Explicit Decision Rights identifies who may settle a question. This pattern supplies a principle for choosing where that authority belongs. Delegated Domains gives a continuing remit to a person or group. Mission Command adds shared intent and preparation for adapting execution as circumstances change.

## How it works

![A decision begins near the work. People assess their knowledge, capability, mandate, and effects on others. They decide locally when equipped and authorized, bring in support when expertise is missing, and use wider authorized decision-making when the choice exceeds their remit.](../assets/organizational-design/decide-close-to-the-work.svg)

**Examine an actual decision category.** Start with substitutions, scheduling adjustments, process changes, or another recurring choice. Identify who encounters the relevant facts first, who experiences the result, and where approval currently waits. Avoid announcing decentralization without identifying which decisions will move.

**Match the decision’s reach.** Distinguish a choice affecting one depot’s available stock from a promise that changes several branches’ commitments. Map the affected parties and resources. A local decision can still have substantial consequences: the question is whether those effects are understood and covered by its mandate, not whether the choice feels small.

**Establish capability and context.** Provide training, access to relevant evidence, and an understanding of common priorities. Depot staff need stock availability and customer commitments, not merely permission to improvise. Check whether they can recognize exceptions and obtain specialist help. Being closest to a problem does not automatically provide the skill to resolve it.

**Delegate real authority.** The legitimate authority-holder specifies what people can decide, which resources they may commit, required controls, and reserved choices. Make ordinary substitutions possible within defined conditions. Identify a named person or an agreed team decision rule. Responsibility without usable authority simply preserves the approval queue under a different label.

**Separate advice from authorization.** A local owner can consult a technician or neighboring depot without surrendering the decision. If the issue exceeds their authority, use the designated wider decision-maker. Explain which circumstances require immediate escalation and what to do when the normal authority is unavailable. Silence does not create permission.

**Keep wider effects visible.** Notify affected colleagues, record consequential choices, and share recurring problems. Provide support near the work where practical. A regional specialist might join a difficult customer discussion while leaving an ordinary remedy with the depot’s authorized owner. Leadership still handles conflicts that cannot be resolved within local mandates.

**Review and adjust the allocation.** Examine quality, delay, reversals, transferred costs, and dependence on informal permission. Strengthen capability or revise boundaries where needed. Repeated escalation may indicate missing information; repeated local harm may indicate an unsuitable mandate. Assess decisions using the information available at the time, rather than treating every unfavorable outcome as proof that delegation failed.

## Why it works

Moving facts through an approval chain takes time and requires explanation. Some details are difficult to capture, and conditions may change while the request travels. Where local people are equipped and authorized, deciding there can shorten that path and connect the choice more directly with feedback.

Wider leadership attention can then focus on problems requiring a broader view. This depends on local teams sharing important information rather than treating autonomy as secrecy.

The tradeoff is real. Local judgments can diverge, duplicate effort, or impose costs elsewhere. Common context, clear limits, specialist support, and review make local authority workable. Merely removing a signature supplies none of those conditions.

## Examples

### Toyota’s published stop-and-call arrangements, consulted October 2026

Toyota’s account of its production system describes operators stopping a line with a stop cord when they detect an abnormality. Its virtual plant tour describes a worker using a call button to alert the leader through an Andon board; work does not resume until the problem is resolved. [Toyota, _Toyota Production System_](https://global.toyota/en/company/vision-and-philosophy/production-system/) and [_Virtual Plant Tour_](https://global.toyota/en/company/plant-tours/production-system/)

The arrangement puts the ability to interrupt defective work with someone encountering it, while making the issue visible and drawing in support. The local authority concerns a defined response to an abnormality, rather than unrestricted control over the production system.

This illustrates why consequential decisions need not always move away from the work. A bounded local stop can protect downstream operations. These sources describe the company’s system; they do not establish identical equipment or sequences at every plant, or independently quantify this mechanism’s contribution to performance.

### Timpson’s published shop-level discretion, consulted October 2026

Timpson describes its “Upside Down Management” approach as giving shop colleagues authority to serve customers in their own way. Its culture account assigns managers responsibilities for training, tools, support, and mentoring through major problems, rather than directing every customer interaction. [Timpson, _Our Culture_](https://www.timpson.co.uk/our-story/our-culture)

That combination matters organizationally. Customer-facing colleagues have discretion, while management remains responsible for conditions that make it usable. The account also describes service standards and expectations for handling money; its broad language about freedom should not be read as a complete list of formal powers or exceptions.

The transferable idea is to support judgment where the customer interaction occurs. This is an interpretation of Timpson’s published approach, not independent observation of every shop or proof that autonomy alone produces better service.

## Useful when

- **Approval adds delay without useful knowledge.** Requests travel upward only to repeat facts already understood locally.
- **Conditions change quickly.** The decision loses value while waiting for someone further from events.
- **Relevant expertise is near the work.** Capable people can judge the situation and receive direct feedback.
- **Senior managers are overloaded with routine choices.** Clear delegation would preserve attention for conflicts and commitments requiring a wider view.

## Common mistakes and limits

**Equating proximity with competence.** Build skill and supply context before expecting sound discretion.

**Moving responsibility without powers or resources.** Local owners need authority that others recognize and leaders respect.

**Ignoring effects on neighbors.** Local convenience does not justify committing shared stock, capacity, or reputation beyond the mandate.

**Making escalation a failure.** Seeking help or wider authority can be the correct local judgment.

**Centralizing again after one mistake.** Investigate whether capability, information, boundaries, or behavior caused the problem before withdrawing discretion wholesale.

Some choices need common standards, scarce-resource arbitration, or specialized assurance. Retain those requirements explicitly. Local decisions are most useful when their boundaries fit the work; moving every decision downward can create as much dysfunction as moving every decision upward.
