**Pattern 33 of 49 · Part 6: Bass Movement and Harmonic Color**

**Use when:** The low part follows roots but adds neither direction nor a convincing groove.

A songwriter follows every chord with its root in the bass. The progression is clear, but the low part has little identity of its own. Another draft adds so many passing notes that the harmony becomes harder to follow. A useful bass line balances orientation, motion, and rhythmic character.

Write a Bass Line means giving the low part a purposeful relationship to the harmony and the groove. It can establish roots, connect chords, sustain a center, or become a hook. It does not need to do all those jobs continuously.

## Working choice

Give the bass an intentional rhythm, route between targets, and relationship to the lead and drums. Busy motion can obscure the song; a held root can be the most useful bass decision.

## Start with a clear skeleton

Porch Light's verse gives C, Am, F, and G two bars each. A first bass skeleton can play the root at the beginning of each chord and let it last. That makes the harmonic sequence easy to hear and leaves room for the lyric.

A second version plays each root on beat one of both bars. The additional attacks reinforce the pulse without introducing new pitches. A third adds a shorter fifth late in the second bar of each chord: C then G, A then E, F then C, G then D. Rhythm changes the line's activity while the harmonic role stays simple.

These versions are useful baselines. A more elaborate line can be compared against them to determine what its additional notes contribute rather than assuming busier automatically means better.

## Connect a destination

At the end of two C bars, a short B can lead down to A as Am arrives. The B is a passing connection, not a request to replace C with a B-root chord for an entire bar. Its timing and duration make the role clear.

An original second-bar pattern could be C for two beats, G for one beat, B for one beat, then A on the following downbeat. The line has a clear destination. Holding B through the whole second C bar would create a different bass/harmony relationship that deserves its own analysis.

The chord progression and melody still matter. A passing note can sound exposed if every other part stops there. What works quietly inside a moving line may become a prominent harmonic event in a sparse texture.

## Use inversions for a larger line

A descending bass C–B–A–G can support C–G/B–Am–C/G. The low contour becomes a recognizable feature. The progression is an explicit alternative to the course's base verse, because two upper chords have changed.

A more direct inversion of the base C–Am–F–G might use bass C–C–C–B, giving C, Am/C, F/C, and G/B. The upper chord identities remain while the low line changes. The prolonged C creates its own sustained relationship and may or may not serve the phrase.

These comparisons prevent a common shorthand error: calling any attractive descending line a mere rearrangement of the same progression. The complete notes, not the writer's intention alone, determine the harmonic result.

## Let a stable bass support change elsewhere

Platform Three initially anchors D and uses A as a secondary pitch. A higher figure explores the D Dorian palette while the bass preserves the center. This can create continuity without a new root every bar.

A pedal point is a sustained or recurring pitch across changing harmony. Its effect depends on the chords above it and its duration. A D pedal under Dm, G, and C creates different combined sounds; it does not make those upper chords interchangeable.

A stable bass can also make another change easier to hear. Replacing B natural with B-flat in the higher line compares D Dorian with D natural minor while leaving the low reference intact. That is a controlled compositional comparison.

## I Wish: the bass is an active phrase

Stevie Wonder's **I Wish**, from **Songs in the Key of Life** ([official artist audio](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=c7IYSAUj78g)), makes the bass a recurring source of rhythmic and melodic identity. Its motion can be followed as a phrase before the listener names the harmony. That is a different role from merely striking one low root whenever the chord changes. The voice and brighter instrumental parts have to relate to a bass line that already says something.

Platform Three offers a simpler version of the same arranging problem. Its D on beat one and short A on the second half of beat three establish weight, movement, and a gap. A fill that occupies the gap every time may weaken the line's identity even if all its notes fit the scale. I Wish illustrates the expressive role of an active bass; the D–A timings belong to the original course example, not to Wonder's recording. Begin with a line whose destinations and spaces are clear, then coordinate its attacks with the drums and the singer instead of treating bass activity as something to maximize.

## Coordinate rhythm and duration

A bass attack aligned with a kick can make an event feel unified. An anticipation can lean into the next chord. Long overlapping low notes may obscure a busy progression even when their attacks interlock well. Note endings deserve as much attention as beginnings.

The voice's rhythm also deserves space. A bass fill in a vocal gap can answer the line. The same fill under a crucial new lyric may divide attention. The bass can become more active in an instrumental passage without requiring that activity throughout the song.

This pattern helps when harmony is unclear, when a groove lacks identity, or when a low part sounds disconnected from the rest. It does not reduce bass writing to background support. A distinctive bass hook can be the central idea around which the song is arranged.
