**Pattern 3 of 49 · Part 1: Start Making Songs**

**Use when:** A promising loop becomes static, or constant novelty makes the idea hard to recognize.

A four-bar passage sounds promising the first time. After twenty uninterrupted repetitions, it seems to have lost its charm. The notes have not changed, but the listener's relationship to them has. What was initially a discovery has become an established expectation. A song develops partly by deciding what to do with that expectation.

Repetition gives an idea a recognizable identity. Variation preserves some of that identity while changing another feature. Surprise interrupts or redirects an expectation. These are compositional relationships, not fixed quantities: a tiny alteration can matter after a stable pattern, while a large alteration can pass unnoticed in a constantly changing texture.

## Working choice

Establish a recognizable unit, then preserve selected features while changing one consequential relationship. Repetition can be the point of a groove; vary it when the listening relationship needs to change.

## Establish something the listener can recognize

Porch Light's seed is E4–G4–A4, lasting one, one, and two beats. Played once, it is a gesture. Played again with the same timing, it becomes a recognizable unit. A third appearance gives the listener a basis for anticipating its shape.

Now compare two fourth appearances. Version A repeats the seed exactly. Version B keeps E4 and G4 but ends on C5 for two beats. The rhythm remains familiar while the final interval expands. The changed arrival is easier to notice because two features were preserved: the beginning and the timing.

A writer could instead preserve pitch while changing rhythm: E4 for half a beat, G4 for half a beat, then A4 for three beats. The total remains four beats. This version arrives at the long note sooner. That different rhythmic trajectory may support a word that needs more time without replacing the seed's pitches.

## Change one relationship before changing everything

If the fourth appearance changes the instrument, rhythm, register, harmony, and pitches simultaneously, it may sound like a new section rather than a variation. That can be desirable. It simply answers a different musical question.

Consider an original eight-bar instrumental opening. Bars one and two present a low repeated figure. Bars three and four repeat it with a higher answer. Bars five and six return to the original figure. Bars seven and eight remove the answer and leave a rest before the next section. The design creates continuity without requiring new chords every two bars.

![A repeated motif retains its opening and rhythm while one final note changes.](/diagrams/songwriting/motif-variation.svg 'Preserving selected features makes a change legible as development of an existing idea.')

The same principle applies to words. Repeating a chorus after new information can change its meaning even when the line itself remains untouched. The musical material is identical, but its context is not. Later patterns will use this distinction to develop Porch Light's central request.

## Hear continuity through a changing texture

Four Tet's [Two Thousand and Seventeen](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZNuaLyPMc48), from *New Energy*, gives this distinction a listening context. The repeating plucked figure supplies a reference against which the surrounding texture can be compared. In the listening card, use the opening window to establish that reference, then compare the later window. Attend separately to the recurring figure and to the sounds around it; a new layer is not automatically a new melody.

The songwriting transfer is a choice about where development happens. Platform Three can keep its bass timing while a higher answer enters or disappears. Replacing the bass pattern as well would test a different relationship. ## Make a surprise belong

A sudden silence before a chorus can feel dramatic when an ongoing pulse has made the next entrance predictable. Without that preparation, the same silence may feel like the recording stopped. The listener needs enough context for the interruption to register as a deliberate choice.

Surprise can also come from restraint. If two verses have become progressively fuller, removing the drums at the third entrance may be more noticeable than adding another layer. A quiet final line can redirect a song that previously reserved its longest notes for loud arrivals.

In Platform Three, the bass usually plays D on beat one. Once that pattern is established, omitting the D for a single bar lets the higher figure briefly stand alone. The eventual bass return becomes an event. The omission matters because the part had a stable job before it disappeared.

## Diagnose repetition that feels static

A repeated passage may be boring because nothing changes, but that is only one possibility. The phrase may be too long, the lyric may say the same thing repeatedly, or the arrangement may obscure its most interesting detail. Adding variation without diagnosing the problem can create clutter.

Suppose a chorus already contains a distinctive melodic hook. Rewriting the hook at every return may weaken its role. A more useful variation could happen around it: a changed bass rhythm, a shorter lead-in, or a second voice on the final line. The focal idea stays available to memory while its setting develops.

Exact repetition also has expressive uses. It can sustain a groove, make a statement insistent, or give listeners room to attend to small changes in performance. The question is not whether repetition is allowed. It is what repeated time contributes to the intended experience.

## Use the pattern at the right scale

A note can repeat within a motif; a motif can repeat within a phrase; a phrase can return within a section; a section can return in the whole song. These levels interact. A constantly varied melody may sit over a stable bass loop, while a repeated chorus may arrive after increasingly different verses.

This pattern helps when an idea is attractive but its continuation is unclear, or when a song alternates between unchanging loops and unrelated new material. It cannot replace having an idea worth returning to.
