*Build weather around the action—and make it visible to the camera.*

A character steps into a downpour. Water strikes their shoulders, runs down their face, and splashes beneath their shoes. Behind them, trees bend and windows flash with lightning.

Waiting for a real storm would give filmmakers little control over when those events happen or how long they last. A conversation might require dozens of takes, while the weather changes halfway through.

**Practical rain and storm rigs produce controlled weather effects around the photographed action.** Elevated water systems supply rain; wind machines move it through the frame; wet surfaces, spray, lighting, sound, and visual effects complete the environment.

The result is weather that can participate in a performance.

Standing inside a rain rig may feel convincingly wet. Looking through the camera can reveal surprisingly little falling water.

Raindrops are transparent. Their visibility depends on how they catch light, what lies behind them, their distance from the lens, and how motion is recorded.

Light from behind or the side can make drops stand out against darker surroundings. The same water may become much less conspicuous against a bright background.

That is why screen rain is a photographic effect as well as a water effect.

In his account of *Singin’ in the Rain*, Stanley Donen emphasized backlighting as the way to reveal the drops. [His illustrated explanation shows how the lighting supported the sequence.](https://www.dga.org/craft/dgaq/issues/0903-fall-2009/shot-to-remember-singing-in-the-rain)

## The rain rig supplies only part of the storm
Rain bars and elevated rain towers distribute water above the photographed area. Their placement and coverage determine where the rain falls and which parts of the set receive it.

The intended appearance also matters. Fine spray can suggest mist or airborne moisture; more distinct drops can provide readable streaks and visible impacts.

But the illusion extends beyond the falling water:

- **Wet surfaces** provide reflections, runoff, dripping edges, and splashes.
- **Wind** gives rain a direction and moves clothing, foliage, and other surroundings.
- **Spray and water surges** suggest gusts, breaking waves, or water striking nearby structures.
- **Lighting cues** establish storm darkness, passing illumination, or lightning.
- **Sound** supplies the larger environment beyond the photographed patch.

These elements need to describe the same conditions. A character struggling against a gale looks less convincing if the nearby branches remain still.

### Example: *Singin’ in the Rain* (1952)—how can every splash happen in exactly the right place?

Gene Kelly’s famous dance looks like spontaneous delight in bad weather. Its water effects were closely integrated with choreography.

Donen explained that overhead pipes supplied the rain. The production also created depressions in the pavement where Kelly needed puddles. Because his steps brought him repeatedly to the same positions, the splashes could occur at the intended moments.

The weather was effectively part of the dance partner: it had locations, timing, and repeated interactions.

[Donen describes the pipes, placed puddles, and shooting conditions in his *Fresh Air* interview.](https://www.capradio.org/news/npr/story?storyid=698914065)

He also rejected the often-repeated story that milk made the rain visible, crediting the lighting instead. [The DGA interview records that explanation.](https://www.dga.org/craft/dgaq/issues/0903-fall-2009/shot-to-remember-singing-in-the-rain)

### Example: *The Perfect Storm* (2000)—how can a soundstage feel like an overwhelming sea?

*The Perfect Storm (2000) — Behind the scenes.* A dump tank sends a wave onto the Andrea Gail mockup while a remotely operated camera records it. A hurricane requires more than vertical rainfall. Water comes from several directions, visibility collapses, and the vessel continually moves beneath its occupants.

The production combined rain machines, wind, water cannons, and large dump tanks around the boat set. Those systems supplied immediate water interaction, while the wider ocean was extended through visual effects.

The camera equipment also had to function inside that environment. Cinematographer John Seale and his collaborators developed protective camera coverings and air-curtain arrangements to help keep water from obscuring the lens.

The useful pattern is **layered weather**: falling rain establishes the general conditions, while gusts, spray, and larger water events supply particular dramatic beats.

[American Cinematographer’s production report includes photographs and explains the different systems.](https://theasc.com/article/sound-and-fury-the-perfect-storm/)

### Example: *The Lost World: Jurassic Park* (1997)—what happens when the camera looks straight into the rain rig?

The trailer sequence places the characters inside an increasingly precarious situation during pouring rain.

Cinematographer Janusz Kaminski described a practical complication: some camera angles pointed directly upward into the rain towers. Water reached the lens, fogging became a problem, and rain deflectors failed.

The setup produced the required weather, but the camera still needed a usable view through it.

That is a recurring design problem. The action, rain coverage, camera movement, and lens protection must work together—especially when the viewpoint changes direction during a shot.

[The original production account describes the trailer photography and its rain-related difficulties.](https://theasc.com/article/the-lost-world-jurassic-park/)

## A small patch of weather can suggest a much larger storm
The production does not necessarily need to rain on everything visible.

Water close to the camera establishes a foreground layer. Rain around the performer supplies contact. Additional rain farther back gives the scene depth.

Wet scenery can carry the impression beyond the active rain zone. A dripping awning, puddled road, or soaked window suggests continuing weather even where individual drops are difficult to see.

Framing helps conceal the boundaries. Digital effects can extend rainfall into distant areas, repair gaps, or maintain density across shots.

The practical water provides especially useful details where it touches bodies and surfaces.

## Wind needs something visible to move

Air itself is largely invisible. On screen, viewers read wind through its consequences.

Coats pull sideways. Hair changes direction. Rain slants. Leaves turn, branches bend, and loose material travels through the scene.

Those reactions can be distributed across foreground and background. A large fan affecting only the performer may leave the wider environment looking strangely calm.

Professional effects companies use different wind machines for different coverage and movement requirements. [Dan Oliver’s effects equipment overview illustrates that range.](https://www.danoliversfx.com/wind-and-air)

The dramatic rhythm matters too. A rising gust, a brief lull, and a sudden blast can support different moments in the action.

## Camera settings shape the rain’s texture

A drop moves while each frame is exposed. That movement can register as a streak rather than a distinct droplet.

Changing exposure duration changes that appearance. Faster recording for slow motion also changes how impacts, spray, and falling water unfold during playback.

A close view of droplets on a face therefore presents a different photographic problem from a wide shot of a storm-lashed street.

The choice should support the scene’s scale and rhythm. More visible water is not automatically more convincing water.

## Continuity continues after the rain stops

Water alters costumes, hair, scenery, and props throughout a take.

A coat becomes darker and heavier. A puddle grows. Footsteps disturb water that was still in the preceding shot. A window develops a new pattern of runoff.

Repeated takes need compatible starting conditions. The effects team may maintain wet surfaces, while costume and hair departments manage the performer’s appearance.

Drainage is part of the production design too: the water delivered into the set needs somewhere to go without changing the working area unpredictably.

## Sound completes the weather

Practical rain and wind can be noisy, while the storm the audience hears needs to support dialogue and dramatic emphasis.

The finished soundtrack may combine production sound with separately recorded dialogue, footsteps, splashes, rainfall, gusts, and thunder.

Sound can also enlarge the apparent storm. Water battering a roof or wind moving around a building establishes activity beyond the frame.

A convincing scene therefore coordinates what the weather looks like, what it does to the performer, and what the audience hears.

## Where practical weather reaches its limits
Large rain coverage requires substantial infrastructure and coordination. Real wind can disrupt artificial rainfall; water affects footing, equipment, materials, and working conditions.

Practical effects are particularly valuable for contact and immediate physical behavior. Digital work can supply distant coverage or conditions that would be impractical to create across the entire view.

The strongest combination gives each method a clear role within one coherent storm.
