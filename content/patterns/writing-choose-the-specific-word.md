A passage can communicate its general direction while leaving the important distinction unnamed. Something was damaged, an arrangement was difficult, a person was nice. Readers understand whether the writer approves or objects, but have little basis for imagining the situation, assessing the claim, or deciding what to do.

This pattern helps when a broad label conceals the property that matters, when an approximate synonym changes the claim, or when forceful wording promises more certainty than the writer has. **Choose the word or phrase that identifies the relevant distinction at a useful, supportable level of precision.**

Sometimes the result is shorter. Sometimes naming the difference requires several words. The gain is that the reader can understand what kind of thing the writer means, rather than supplying a version of it from experience.

## Find the distinction the reader needs

Specificity is useful in relation to a purpose. A detailed description of the wrong property can leave the reader just as uncertain as a vague one.

Imagine an independent bookshop receiving copies for a Saturday author signing. Several books arrive with visible defects. An employee drafts a message to the supplier:

> The delivery arrived in poor condition. A number of books were badly affected. We need to resolve this issue before Saturday's signing.

The message conveys urgency but leaves the supplier to discover what happened. Were books missing, were pages misprinted, or was only the outer carton marked? _Poor_, _affected_, and _issue_ register concern without specifying the condition that makes replacement relevant.

Suppose the employee checks the copies and records the defects. The message can become:

> Twelve copies arrived with creased covers; three of those copies also have torn pages. Please send twelve replacement copies before Saturday's signing.

_Creased_ distinguishes folded or wrinkled covers from covers that are stained, detached, or simply scuffed. _Torn_ identifies a different defect inside three of the same books. The count establishes the scope. The request gives _resolve_ an actual meaning in this situation.

This revision required inspection, not just a thesaurus. The writer could not legitimately replace _a number_ with _twelve_ before counting. Nor would changing _badly affected_ to the more dramatic _ruined_ establish what was wrong. A stronger impression of damage is different from a more accurate description of it.

The recipient's task also determines which details belong. The colour of the carton and the pattern of tape across its lid may be observable and exact, but neither necessarily helps the supplier send replacements. Precision directs attention to a consequential difference.

## Keep precision within what is known

A narrow word can smuggle in an explanation. Calling a cover _stained_ describes an appearance; calling it _water-damaged_ also identifies what caused the mark. If the employee has only seen a brown patch, the second description may exceed the evidence.

The same problem occurs with words about people. _Declined_, _ignored_, and _forgot_ can all describe situations in which a request receives no positive response, but they make different claims about the person's action or attention. Replacing an indistinct phrase with one of them requires knowing which event occurred.

In the bookshop message, “Twelve copies arrived with creased covers” leaves the cause open. “Your packers crushed twelve books” assigns responsibility as well as describing damage. The latter may be justified by additional evidence, but it is not a neutral improvement in specificity.

Precision can therefore expose a gap in the writer's knowledge. A qualified phrase such as “what appears to be a stain” may be more accurate than a confident diagnosis. General wording is appropriate when the category itself is all that can be established.

## Choose the meaning, including its attitude

Near synonyms carry different judgments. A word can be factually compatible with a scene while directing the reader toward an interpretation the writer does not intend.

Consider a separate fictional scene. Tessa has repeatedly covered a colleague's missed early shifts. At the colleague's farewell party, she responds to a thank-you:

> Tessa smiled and said, “Of course we'll stay in touch.” On the way home, she turned off the alarm that had been waking her before dawn. She stood for a moment with the silent phone in her hand.

A narrator might call Tessa's response _generous_, _forgiving_, _polite_, or _practised_. Each word changes what the scene asks us to believe. _Forgiving_ suggests that she has released a grievance. _Polite_ describes a social manner without settling her feelings. _Practised_ suggests familiarity or rehearsal and may invite us to notice the effort beneath the response.

There is no single most specific word independent of the story's intention. If the narrator knows that Tessa still resents the colleague, _forgiving_ may misrepresent her. If the scene should leave her feelings open, a label may settle too much. The writer can retain the actions and let their relation remain unresolved.

This is also why a rarer synonym is not automatically better. Replacing an ordinary word with an ornate one can change tone, introduce an unintended association, or make the narrator sound unlike the voice established elsewhere. A word earns its place through its meaning in the passage.

## Stop at the useful level

Greater detail can become a distraction. A story may need _a bird_, _a gull_, or the name of a particular species, depending on who notices it and why. The narrowest available classification can be wrong for a viewpoint that would not make it, or irrelevant to a moment concerned chiefly with a sudden movement overhead.

Abstract language has legitimate work too. Once the bookshop has described the creases and torn pages, _the damaged copies_ can efficiently refer to the group. Repeating every defect would add bulk without improving the request. A general category can gather particulars after they have been established.

Choosing an accurate label also differs from choosing the one revealing detail around which to build a scene. _Creased_ names a condition; deciding to show a bookseller smoothing the cover of a cherished first edition is a choice about attention and significance. Both can help the writing, but they answer different questions.

Specific wording is most useful where the reader needs a distinction. Elsewhere, a broad term can preserve proportion, uncertainty, pace, or voice.
