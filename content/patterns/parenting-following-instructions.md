## 1. A recognizable situation

“Turn the tablet off, put your shoes on, and bring your bag.”

Your seven-year-old says, “Okay.”

Five minutes later, the tablet is still playing. You repeat yourself, louder. They switch it off, wander toward the hallway, and start looking for a toy.

“You never listen.”

“I did hear you!”

Both accounts may be accurate. Hearing an instruction, understanding it, remembering it, stopping another activity, and carrying it out are different tasks.

**The useful starting point is to find where the request breaks down.** Sometimes the child needs a clearer instruction or practical support. Sometimes they understand and disagree. Sometimes the expectation itself needs reconsidering.

## 2. What might be happening

“Not listening” can describe several different situations.

- **The instruction did not receive their attention.** You spoke from another room, several people were talking, or the child was absorbed in something. Does approaching them and reducing competing noise change the response?

- **They did not understand what you meant.** “Get ready” or “Behave yourself” requires interpretation. Can they identify the first action? Do they struggle more with unfamiliar words, classroom language, or lengthy explanations?

- **They understood but lost the sequence.** They complete the first step and forget the rest. Does a picture, written reminder, or one instruction at a time help?

- **They cannot readily start or switch activities.** Knowing that shoes are needed does not organize finding them, stopping a game, and getting moving. Observe where they become stuck.

- **The request involves distress or difficulty.** Brushing teeth may hurt; homework may expose a learning problem; changing clothes may involve sensory discomfort. Does refusal concentrate around a particular task?

- **They object or prefer something else.** The child may consider the request unfair, want to finish, or dislike being directed. Their objection may contain useful information even when the responsibility remains.

- **An interaction pattern has developed.** If instructions are routinely repeated before anything happens, both parent and child may come to expect several rounds. This is a plausible explanation to investigate, not proof of deliberate manipulation.

Do not infer ability from one successful occasion. A child may manage a task when rested and supported but struggle after a demanding school day.

Persistent difficulty understanding directions can be associated with language difficulties. ASHA identifies following directions as one aspect of listening comprehension; the behavior alone does not establish a disorder. [ASHA guidance](https://www.asha.org/practice-portal/clinical-topics/spoken-language-disorders/)

## 3. How development changes the picture

**Toddlers and preschoolers need concrete, manageable requests and substantial adult help.** “Put this cup on the table” asks less than “Clear up and get ready for bed.” Understanding a rule also develops before reliably following it under temptation or distress.

For necessary tasks, expect to demonstrate, work alongside them, and intervene for safety. Repeating a rule from across the room is not an adequate safety arrangement for a toddler.

**Primary-school children can take increasing responsibility**, but everyday tasks may still contain hidden demands. Packing a schoolbag requires knowing what tomorrow involves, locating materials, and checking what is missing. Teach that sequence before expecting independence.

Use the child’s demonstrated abilities to decide how much information to give at once. A long explanation is not necessarily more respectful or useful than a brief instruction followed by time to act.

**Adolescents need more ownership over ordinary responsibilities.** Agreeing what must be completed and by when often makes more sense than directing each movement. A teenager may reasonably want to choose when to shower or how to organize homework, while still having obligations that affect other people.

Research on parenting programs is stronger for younger children than for adolescents. Preschool instruction techniques should not be presented as an established solution to teenage disagreement. [AHRQ systematic review, 2025](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK614678/)

## 4. What needs balancing

Parents are responsible for safety, health, shared spaces, and getting necessary things done. They also have limited time and cannot negotiate every departure indefinitely.

Children need understandable expectations, opportunities to practise, and influence over their own activities. They should be able to say that something hurts, ask for clarification, or explain an obstacle.

Distinguish the purpose of a request:

- **Immediate safety:** action may be necessary before discussion.

- **A shared responsibility:** the task matters, but timing or method may be negotiable.

- **An adult preference:** consider whether an instruction is needed at all.

The goal is a child who can follow important directions, communicate difficulties, and gradually manage responsibilities. Immediate obedience measures only part of that.

## 5. Possible responses

The approaches below combine professional recommendations and research-supported principles with practical synthesis. The particular dialogue and household arrangements have not each been independently tested.

### A. Make the instruction easy to receive and understand

**This fits when attention, wording, or too much information appears to be the obstacle.**

Move close enough to speak normally. Obtain some acknowledgment before giving the instruction; eye contact need not be the test of attention.

State the action clearly:

> “Please put your shoes on.”

Then pause long enough for the child to process and begin. Avoid filling the pause with more instructions, explanations, or warnings.

For a younger child, point to the shoes or show the first action. For an older child, ask, “What’s the first thing you need to do?” when you genuinely need to check understanding—not as an interrogation.

If the child starts incorrectly, clarify before treating it as refusal. If they complete the task while grumbling, recognize that the instruction was followed; a separate demand for cheerful agreement can restart the conflict.

The CDC recommends clear, developmentally appropriate directions, attention before speaking, and one instruction at a time for its **two-to-four-year-old** audience. These are professional recommendations, not a trial proving each step independently. [CDC guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/parenting-toddlers/communication/giving-directions.html)

### B. Teach the task and change the conditions

**This fits when the child understands the request but struggles to remember, organize, or execute it.**

Watch one attempt. “Tidy your room” may fail because the child cannot decide where to begin. Work through a bounded part together:

> “First, clothes go in this basket. I’ll help you find them.”

For repeated routines, create a short reminder with the child: pictures for a younger child, a checklist or chosen reminder for an older one. Put materials where the action happens. A toothbrush, schoolbag, or laundry basket that is difficult to locate adds another task.

Reduce support as the child becomes capable. That may mean moving from doing together, to a visible reminder, to an occasional check.

Consider timing and product design. A video service that starts another episode automatically, or a game without an immediate stopping point, complicates transitions. Plan an achievable stopping point and change available settings where useful.

Alberta Health Services recommends reducing distractions, simplifying directions, using gestures or demonstrations, and allowing processing time. These are communication recommendations; the particular checklist and household changes above are practical applications. [Alberta guidance](https://myhealth.alberta.ca/speech-language-hearing/child-speech-and-language/for-preschool-and-school-aged-children/helping-children-follow-directions-preschool)

### C. Make necessary limits clear and follow through proportionately

**This fits when the expectation is reasonable, the child can manage it with available support, and repeated requests are becoming ineffective.**

State what needs to happen and what you will do:

> “We’re leaving now. You can put your shoes on, or I can help you.”

Explain what help involves. If getting dressed repeatedly becomes a physical struggle, investigate discomfort and obtain a workable plan rather than treating physical control as ordinary follow-through.

For an older child, follow-through might mean completing an agreed shared responsibility before beginning the next optional activity. Keep it related, predictable, and manageable. A missed instruction should not trigger a growing list of unrelated losses.

If safety is immediate, act to prevent harm rather than relying on further verbal directions. Discuss and practise afterward in a safe setting.

Acknowledge useful actions specifically: “You brought your bag when I asked; that helped us leave.” Appreciation need not become a reward negotiation for every ordinary contribution.

Parenting programs that include positive reinforcement and nonviolent consequences can reduce disruptive behavior. A component meta-analysis found these techniques associated with stronger program effects, but programs were not randomly assigned to contain each component. Those associations do not prove that praise or a particular consequence caused the improvement. [Leijten and colleagues, 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30738545/)

### D. Solve recurring disagreements before the next confrontation

**This fits when the same instruction repeatedly produces conflict, particularly with school-age children and adolescents.**

Discuss one specific difficulty while calm:

> “When I ask you to stop gaming for dinner, we keep ending up in an argument. What makes stopping difficult?”

Hear the answer before proposing a solution. Then explain the adult concern: food is ready, other people are waiting, or the family needs a predictable evening.

Develop a plan addressing both concerns. Perhaps the child avoids starting a new round near dinner, receives an agreed warning, and helps serve when the round ends. Specify what happens if the plan proves unrealistic and when you will review it.

Collaboration includes adult responsibilities; it does not require accepting every proposed solution. Younger children may choose between two workable options. Teenagers can contribute substantially to the arrangement.

A community randomized trial compared Collaborative and Proactive Solutions with parent management training in 160 children aged seven to fourteen with diagnosed oppositional defiant disorder. Outcomes were similar, with gains maintained at six months. This supports two complete treatments for a clinical population—not proof that one conversation resolves ordinary disagreements, or a reason to diagnose a disagreeing child. [Murrihy and colleagues, 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36858768/)

## 6. Examples in practice

**A three-year-old keeps playing when asked to leave.**  
You approach, explain that it is time to go, and ask them to put one toy in its box. They shout, “No!”

You acknowledge the interruption: “You wanted to keep building.” Then you help with the necessary transition. They remain unhappy while you help with shoes. The aim is a supported departure; ending the tears is a separate matter.

**An eight-year-old forgets the morning routine.**  
Clearer wording does not solve it. They can repeat “teeth, shoes, bag,” but get distracted between tasks.

Together, you make a three-picture reminder at the relevant location. You initially help them use it, then observe whether they need fewer prompts. Remembering the words was not sufficient evidence that the routine was independently manageable.

**A fourteen-year-old repeatedly ignores a request to clear the kitchen.**  
You discover that “clear the kitchen” means different things to each of you. They also object to being interrupted without notice.

You agree on the actual tasks and a completion time. On the first evening, it still does not happen. Rather than declaring collaboration ineffective, you establish whether the difficulty was forgetting, an unrealistic deadline, or disagreement with the responsibility. The next response depends on that information.

## 7. Common mistakes and limitations

**Interpreting “okay” as evidence of every required ability.**  
Acknowledgment shows little about memory, planning, practical skill, or willingness.

**Repeating an unsuccessful instruction more forcefully without changing anything.**  
Sometimes repetition is necessary. But if the words are unclear or the task is inaccessible, volume does not solve the problem.

**Making every request urgent.**  
Children need uninterrupted activity too. Reserve immediate interruption for situations that require it; give reasonable notice for predictable transitions.

**Treating help as automatically rewarding refusal.**  
Helping a child learn to fasten a coat differs from completing a task they already manage independently. Observe what assistance enables and whether responsibility can gradually transfer.

**Expecting praise to be a reliable compliance switch.**  
An experimental meta-analysis found no clear average improvement in compliance from praise alone, despite stronger findings for some other techniques. Most outcomes were immediate or short-term. That does not make appreciation pointless; it limits claims about what praise alone will accomplish. [Leijten and colleagues, 2018](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0204929)

**Assuming a treatment’s benefits will necessarily last.**  
An older review found average gains maintained after parenting interventions, with follow-up up to three years, but results varied. A newer AHRQ review found longer-term comparative evidence limited for many interventions and outcomes. Different studies and follow-up methods prevent a simple guarantee of durability. [Van Aar and colleagues, 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27930935/), [AHRQ review, 2025](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK614678/)

Trust, independent judgment, and emotional wellbeing are also less well measured than disruptive behavior or immediate compliance.

## 8. What to observe over time

Choose a few recurring situations rather than evaluating the child’s entire personality. Look for:

- Starting after an understandable request with fewer prompts.

- Completing more of a routine independently.

- Asking for clarification or help.

- Identifying a stopping point and managing transitions.

- Expressing disagreement without the interaction escalating.

- Less shouting and fewer prolonged battles for everyone.

Check whether apparent improvement transfers beyond close adult supervision. A child who acts only when threatened may look more compliant without becoming more capable.

Reconsider the explanation if difficulties cluster around particular tasks, noise levels, times of day, or language demands. Compare observations with teachers without assuming that different behavior at school and home means either adult is mistaken.

Seek advice when difficulties are persistent, substantially affect learning or family life, involve frequent unsafe behavior, or continue despite clearer communication and appropriate support. Hearing, language, learning, attention, sleep, and emotional difficulties may need consideration. ASHA recommends appropriate assessment where listening comprehension or communication is a concern. [ASHA guidance](https://www.asha.org/practice-portal/clinical-topics/spoken-language-disorders/)

Evidence-based parenting support can be useful when conflict is entrenched. It provides structured coaching and assessment of the interaction; needing that support is not evidence that the parent caused the difficulty.
