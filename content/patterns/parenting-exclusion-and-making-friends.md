## 1. A recognizable situation

Your eight-year-old gets into the car and says, “Nobody likes me.” At lunch, the children they usually sit with moved to another table. At recess, they walked around alone.

You want to reassure them, find out exactly what happened, and perhaps contact someone immediately. Instead, you suggest inviting a classmate over. Your child snaps, “You don’t understand.”

They may need help finding companionship. They may also need someone to recognize the loss of a particular friendship, investigate repeated exclusion, or change an arrangement that leaves them stranded.

**The aim is to help the child experience relationships in which they feel welcome, safe, and able to participate.** Friend counts and invitations provide only partial information about that experience.

## 2. What might be happening

Several situations can sound like “I have no friends.”

**A painful incident within otherwise workable relationships.** A friend chose someone else, an invitation did not arrive, or an argument disrupted the day. Ask whether this is unusual and whether the child still experiences welcome at other times.

**Too few opportunities to connect.** A recent move, transport difficulties, cost, illness, or an overscheduled school day may leave little repeated contact with compatible peers. Notice whether the child connects comfortably when opportunities actually exist.

**Difficulty entering or sustaining interaction.** The child may approach an established game without understanding its rules, struggle to explain ideas, interrupt when excited, or become overwhelmed by disagreement. Look for the particular point where interaction breaks down rather than assuming a general lack of “social skills.”

**A mismatch between the child and the available setting.** A noisy playground organized around competitive sport offers different possibilities from a small art group. A child may struggle in one environment and connect readily in another.

**Fear, discouragement, or withdrawal.** After unsuccessful attempts, approaching another child may feel increasingly risky. Anxiety or low mood can also affect participation. These are possibilities to explore, not diagnoses to infer from solitary play.

**Deliberate exclusion or bullying.** Repeatedly telling others not to associate with a child, humiliating them publicly, or using group membership to control them can constitute social bullying. Bullying involves unwanted aggression, unequal power, and behavior that is repeated or has the potential to recur. Serious incidents still deserve intervention before a pattern is established. [StopBullying.gov: What Is Bullying?](https://www.stopbullying.gov/bullying/what-is-bullying)

For repeated targeting involving unequal power, see [[parenting-being-teased-or-bullied|Being teased or bullied]].

Also distinguish **being alone by choice** from **wanting connection and being unable to find it**. A child can enjoy solitude and still need friendship; a child surrounded by classmates can feel lonely.

## 3. How development changes the picture

### Preschool: participation often needs adult assistance

Young children’s friendships commonly grow through shared activity. Difficulty joining elaborate pretend play may involve language, understanding the ongoing game, or coordinating ideas.

Help with an immediate point of entry: finding another role, bringing a needed material, or beginning a simpler activity alongside a willing peer. Adults may need to remain nearby and help sustain the exchange.

Do not judge preschool friendship solely by whether a child can independently enter a large group or maintain a stable “best friend.”

### Primary school: reciprocity and group arrangements matter more

Children increasingly care about whether another child seeks them out, keeps agreements, and includes them consistently. Simply placing children together does not guarantee friendship.

Parents can help organize contact, while teachers investigate what happens during lunch, recess, group work, and transitions. A child who manages well in lessons may still have no accessible way into unstructured activities.

### Later childhood and adolescence: trust, belonging, and privacy become especially important

Friendship increasingly involves loyalty, confidence, and emotional support alongside shared activities. Losing a close friend may therefore involve losing someone who knows private parts of the child’s life. Research reviews describe these changing expectations, while also finding considerable instability in childhood and adolescent friendships. Change is common; its emotional significance varies. [Santucci and colleagues, 2025](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/02654075251334226)

With older children, agree on parental involvement whenever possible. Offer transport, help drafting a message, or support speaking to a trusted adult. Quietly arranging their social life without consultation can damage the trust needed for them to disclose difficulties.

Across ages, communication style, disability, cultural background, and previous experiences matter. Assess whether the setting accommodates the child rather than measuring them only against its dominant social style.

## 4. What needs balancing

**Taking distress seriously while investigating carefully.** “Nobody likes me” communicates pain. It does not require either immediate agreement that everyone is hostile or an argument proving the child wrong.

**Supporting agency while providing protection.** Children should influence whom they spend time with and how adults help. They should not carry sole responsibility for stopping intimidation or discriminatory treatment.

**Teaching useful skills while examining other people’s behavior.** A child may benefit from learning to join a game or manage interruptions. That does not make rejection deserved.

**Respecting everyone’s freedom to choose friends while ensuring fair participation.** Schools cannot require affection. They can organize accessible activities, prevent harassment, and stop practices that repeatedly leave the same child publicly unwanted.

**Providing opportunities within family constraints.** Friendship support should not depend on expensive clubs, elaborate parties, or a parent being available every afternoon.

## 5. Possible responses

### A. Understand the particular difficulty before choosing a solution

**Fits:** a new disclosure, an unclear situation, or a child who feels overwhelmed by advice.

Begin with the concrete hurt:

> “You expected to sit with them, and then you were left on your own. That sounds painful.”

Let the child tell the sequence before asking many questions. Later, explore a few details: Who was involved? Does this happen repeatedly? Is there anyone they feel comfortable with? What happens when they try to participate?

Then establish what help would feel useful: listening, thinking together, or involving someone else. With younger children, offer these as simple choices. With older children, explain any safety concern that means you need to involve an adult despite their reluctance.

For repeated school difficulties, request observations from the adults who supervise the relevant settings. The American Academy of Pediatrics specifically recommends gathering information from lunch and recess staff when rejection forms a pattern. This is professional guidance, not a proven script for reducing loneliness. [AAP: Problems With Peers](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/gradeschool/school/Pages/Problems-With-Peers.aspx)

### B. Create manageable opportunities with compatible peers

**Fits:** limited opportunities, a recent transition, or better interactions in smaller settings.

Look for someone your child already finds approachable. Arrange a modest activity with a shared focus: a familiar game, drawing, walking, or working on something together.

For a younger child, help the activity begin and remain available without directing every exchange. For an older child, ask what setting they would choose and what practical assistance they need.

Interest-based activities and supported opportunities are common professional recommendations. Their rationale is that an activity supplies something to do together and reduces the demand to generate conversation from nothing. They are not guarantees of friendship, and evidence does not establish an ideal playdate length or frequency. [Harvard Health: Helping Children Make Friends](https://www.health.harvard.edu/blog/helping-children-make-friends-what-parents-can-do-202303062899)

My practical suggestion is to favor an affordable opportunity the child can return to over a succession of unfamiliar events. School lunchtime activities, a library group, or meeting at a park may be more feasible than another paid commitment.

Afterward, ask about enjoyment and comfort. Avoid treating every encounter as an audition that must produce a friend.

### C. Coach one useful action, with the child’s involvement

**Fits:** an identifiable difficulty during otherwise potentially welcoming interactions.

Choose one situation:

- Joining play: notice what others are doing, then offer a relevant contribution.

- Starting contact: make a specific invitation connected to a shared interest.

- Sustaining interaction: leave room for the other person’s idea or response.

- Handling rejection: acknowledge the answer and approach another person or activity.

- Repairing a disagreement: describe what happened and make a specific proposal.

Rehearse briefly if the child wants to. A preschooler may need an adult to help during the actual interaction. An older child may prefer to plan privately and try independently.

Teach options rather than a social performance. Eye contact, constant sociability, or concealing harmless interests should not become conditions for deserving inclusion.

The evidence requires caution. A systematic review of randomized friendship interventions for children with neurodevelopmental needs found that some programs helped, but the pooled advantage over comparison conditions was statistically uncertain. The included studies concerned autism or ADHD; they cannot establish effectiveness for every child struggling socially. They also evaluated intervention packages, not isolated conversation tips. [Cordier and colleagues, 2023](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0295917)

Improvement during coaching therefore needs checking against real outcomes: does the child actually feel more connected, and do peers respond?

### D. Change an exclusionary or unsafe environment

**Fits:** repeated rejection, bullying, discrimination, or arrangements that consistently block participation.

Bring concrete information to the school or activity leader: what happens, where, how often, and what the child has already tried.

Ask for a specific plan. Depending on the problem, this might include closer supervision at a particular location, adult-organized group allocation, accessible alternatives at recess, or action on discriminatory comments. Agree who will check with the child and when the plan will be reviewed.

For bullying, avoid making reconciliation the immediate goal. Official guidance cautions against forced peer mediation where children do not have equal power. Protection and follow-through come first. [StopBullying.gov: Supporting Children Involved](https://www.stopbullying.gov/prevention/support-the-children-involved)

There is causal evidence for school intervention: a meta-analysis of 69 independent randomized trials, involving children approximately four to seventeen, found small average reductions in bullying and improvements in mental health outcomes. Results varied, and the findings concern complete programs—not proof that one assembly or classroom conversation will resolve an individual case. [Fraguas and colleagues, 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33136156/)

Online exclusion may require parallel action. My practical recommendation is to preserve relevant messages, agree on reporting and contact arrangements, and consider muting distressing notifications. Group chats and visible reactions can extend an exclusionary incident beyond school. Preserve access to supportive contacts while addressing the harmful interaction.

## 6. Examples in practice

**A four-year-old cannot enter an established game.**

Your child repeatedly announces, “I’m playing too,” then changes the rules. The others protest.

With the educator, you identify the difficulty: finding a role within an ongoing story. A willing peer offers a delivery role in their pretend shop, and the educator helps the first exchange happen. Later, your child plays alongside the group rather than participating continuously. That may still be useful progress.

**An eight-year-old’s first attempt fails.**

You suggest a lunchtime club because your child spends recess alone. They attend once and return more upset: places were informally reserved, and established friends worked only together.

You acknowledge that the proposed opportunity was not accessible in practice. The next step is to speak with the organizer about how newcomers enter an activity. Repeating “Keep trying” would leave the barrier untouched.

**A fourteen-year-old is excluded from a group chat.**

Your child asks you not to contact anyone. You agree to discuss possible steps before acting and ask whether there are threats, humiliating posts, or pressure on others to avoid them.

If this is a painful friendship change, you can help them decide whether to contact one person privately and strengthen other connections. If peers are coordinating harassment, adult protection becomes necessary. Explain what you will share and seek the teenager’s input about reducing retaliation.

They may remain devastated even after a sensible plan. Neither a replacement activity nor an adult conversation immediately replaces the relationship they lost.

These examples are practical synthesis, not separately tested interventions.

## 7. Common mistakes and limitations

**“Just go and ask to play.”** This skips the question of whether the group is receptive and whether the child has already tried.

**Turning loneliness into a performance target.** “Make one new friend today” asks a child to control another person’s response. A specific action, such as offering an invitation, is more manageable—but should not become another daily test.

**Assuming a quiet child needs more friends.** Ask whether they feel connected. Equally, do not dismiss loneliness because a child has classmates around them.

**Correcting the child before understanding the setting.** Coaching may help, but leading with their supposed mistakes can discourage disclosure.

**Promising that kindness will earn inclusion.** Children can behave considerately and still encounter incompatibility, prejudice, or cruelty.

**Overselling programs.** The loneliness evidence is mixed. A 2026 review focused on interventions targeting loneliness in ages four to eighteen found only six randomized trials; their pooled effect was small and compatible with no benefit. An earlier review covering a broader population up to age twenty-five found benefits overall, but often studied young people selected for other difficulties rather than loneliness itself. These reviews use different eligibility criteria and do not identify one established remedy for a particular child. [2026 child-focused review](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jad.2026.121445), [Eccles and Qualter, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/camh.12389)

Associations also require care. A meta-analysis linked warmer teacher–student relationships with less loneliness, including reciprocal associations over time. It cannot show that assigning a supportive teacher alone will resolve loneliness. [Teacher–student relationship review, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsp.2024.101380)

## 8. What to observe over time

Progress may include:

- Finding someone who responds with interest and sometimes initiates contact.

- Feeling safer during previously difficult parts of the day.

- Participating with less adult assistance.

- Recovering from a declined invitation without concluding that nobody will ever want them.

- Being able to disagree, decline, or express an interest without fearing loss of the relationship.

- Feeling less lonely, rather than merely appearing busier.

Ask for the child’s account as well as adult observations. Attendance at a club or improved classroom behavior does not establish belonging.

Reconsider the explanation if support produces little change. A presumed confidence problem may be persistent exclusion. A supposed lack of interest may be exhaustion or fear. Increasing withdrawal across previously enjoyable settings may call for assessment of emotional wellbeing.

Seek additional help when loneliness persists, distress grows, friendships repeatedly become coercive, or difficulties affect sleep, school attendance, eating, or usual activities. A school counselor, pediatrician, or appropriate child mental health professional can help assess the pattern. Persistent friendship difficulties warrant discussion even without an obvious diagnosis. [AAP guidance](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/gradeschool/school/Pages/Problems-With-Peers.aspx)

Threats, abuse, or indications that the child cannot stay safe require prompt protective action.
