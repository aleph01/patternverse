Someone speaks about a disappointment while keeping their shoulders raised and their arms close to their body. A helper might encourage them to relax. In Hakomi, another possibility is to become interested in what the effort is doing and whether some of that effort can be supported from outside, with precise permission.

## Core idea

Taking Over the Defense is the established phrase for this move. The practitioner supports an existing way of managing experience rather than opposing it. The intention is to preserve its protective function while reducing the person's need to perform all of the effort themselves, leaving room to notice what else is present.

The word defense can suggest combat or malfunction. Hakomi's nonviolent orientation treats these actions as ways a person manages experience. Holding tension, interrupting an impulse, or repeating an internal warning may have an understandable function.

The defining move is cooperation with a protective effort. Instead of asking the person to stop managing experience, the practitioner explores whether the management action can be supported in a way the person actually welcomes. That support may free attention for another experience, or reveal that the proposed support does not fit.

## What changes in the experience

If support for a protective action fits, the client may notice a wish that the action has been containing. They may also feel more pressured, hear the therapist as critical, or find the experiment unhelpful. Each response changes what should happen next.

A non-touch alternative may sometimes explore the same theme through words, objects, posture, or another collaborative experiment. Such an adaptation should be named accurately rather than assumed equivalent to every clinical form of taking over.

The relevant change is not necessarily relaxation. The person might notice irritation, uncertainty, an unmet wish, or the importance of keeping control of the action themselves. Feedback changes the experiment. A defense that remains in place can still become more understandable; its disappearance is not the criterion that validates the intervention.

## A worked example

In an illustrative verbal version, a client notices the phrase "Don't make a fuss" whenever anger appears. The therapist asks whether hearing that exact warning voiced outside might help them study its effect. The client can change the words, decline the experiment, or stop it. The therapist is not endorsing the warning's truth, and accuracy matters more than theatrical emphasis.

Suppose the client agrees to hear the warning spoken outside them. The therapist gives it a firm tone, and the client immediately says it sounds like an order. They stop. The client explains that the inner phrase usually sounds worried, almost pleading: making a fuss is expected to threaten belonging rather than break a rule.

If the client wants to continue, the wording and tone can be revised. Hearing the worried quality, they notice affection for the effort to preserve the relationship and anger about having so little room within it. The intervention has supported discovery by accepting correction. It has not proven that the warning belongs to a particular historical figure or that the client should now abandon restraint.

## Why the move may help

One clinical rationale is that an action held with constant effort may leave little room to notice what else is present. Welcome support can alter that distribution of effort. Another contribution is relational: the person discovers whether a helper can cooperate with the way they are managing before proposing change.

These are Hakomi's experiential rationales, not guarantees about a bodily mechanism. The observable effects might be reduced effort, clearer objection, or access to a previously overshadowed feeling. An intervention that produces more pressure calls for reconsideration; distress should not automatically be interpreted as evidence that deeper material has been successfully reached.

## When it helps—and what can get in the way

The pattern is relevant when relaxation requests provoke greater bracing, when stopping a protective behavior feels threatening, or when effort itself has become the focus of curiosity. It does not establish that all muscular tension has an emotional cause. Pain, disability, fatigue, and ordinary physical demands remain relevant.

This may fit a protective action the person can recognize and is curious about supporting. It may fit poorly when the therapist has inferred a defense from posture alone. A raised shoulder might concern pain, temperature, habit, or a chair's height. Accurate description and collaboration precede any hypothesis about what a movement manages.

## Boundaries and common misunderstandings

Hakomi descriptions include taking over a bodily effort. These interventions are professional skills involving assessment, precise feedback, and explicit consent. A conceptual article is not sufficient instruction for physical contact or pressure, particularly around breathing, movement restriction, pain, or trauma responses.

The distinction is important because "support the defense" can be misread as holding someone in a posture or increasing a constriction. That would risk overriding the person's agency. The protective function must remain available, and the person must be free to change or end the interaction.

The intervention loses its character if the practitioner supports a defense only to make it disappear. A client may accurately sense this agenda. Taking over should remain a shared experiment, not an indirect method of extracting emotion.

Cooperation also has limits. Supporting the meaning or effort of protection does not require endorsing conduct that harms someone else. Physical interventions require particular competence and ongoing consent; an educational account cannot establish their suitability for an individual. The client should retain a clear way to stop without having to justify the decision.
