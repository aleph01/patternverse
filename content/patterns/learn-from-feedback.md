## Purpose and problem

Tess and Daniel have been arguing less since agreeing to pause difficult conversations. Their therapist is pleased. Tess feels relieved that evenings no longer escalate. Daniel says the change has helped too, then adds that they never return to the subjects they pause.

The same result contains two experiences: welcome relief and accumulating unfinished concerns. If the therapist asks only whether arguments have decreased, the work may look more successful than either partner's fuller account supports.

These fictional people introduce the pattern's central question: what is the work actually doing, for whom, and what follows from finding that out?

## Core idea

**Learn from Feedback means letting information about effects change the understanding or direction of the work.**

Feedback can concern everyday life, the experience of a session, an agreed change, or the therapist's approach. It may come through conversation, an accessible drawing, a questionnaire, an observed difficulty, or a later account that corrects an earlier interpretation.

A useful mental model is to keep three things separate: what was hoped for, what people report experiencing, and what to reconsider. Asking for feedback opens the process. Learning requires a response to what was heard, which can include keeping something helpful, changing direction, or stopping an unhelpful approach.

## How it works

### Review the purpose as well as the result

An arrangement may be carried out faithfully without producing the wanted difference. A weekly conversation can happen without anyone feeling heard. Fewer calls from school may indicate a calmer day, less reporting, or a child becoming less visible. Effects need interpretation with the people concerned.

The UCL systemic competence framework includes discussing outcome measures with clients, using feedback to reflect on the therapist's own work, and revising goals as information emerges. It places the therapist within the work being examined. Feedback is not simply an assessment of how well clients followed instructions. [UCL: Basic systemic competences](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/brain-sciences/sites/brain_sciences/files/migrated-files/Systemic_basic_systemic_competences.pdf)

### Preserve different accounts

One person's relief can coexist with another's increased burden. Disagreement does not automatically mean that somebody is inaccurate. People may be describing different moments, costs, or priorities. A shared summary should not erase those differences.

Dialogical family therapy gives particular attention to creating room for participants to say how the conversation is affecting them. Rober, Van Tricht, and Sundet describe their Dialogical Feedback Tool as a conversational invitation, including room for mixed responses and children's drawings. They explicitly distinguish it from a quantitative outcome measure. [Rober and colleagues: One step up, but not there yet](https://mareld.se/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/Rober.van-Tricht-Sundet-2020.pdf)

### Use measures as evidence to discuss

**Routine outcome monitoring** means repeatedly collecting information about progress, often through standardized questionnaires, to inform treatment. Measures of the **therapeutic alliance** concern the working relationship, including whether the approach and goals fit. A rating can prompt a conversation; it cannot supply its whole meaning.

Research does not justify treating every feedback procedure as reliably beneficial. Anker and colleagues' randomized couple-therapy trial found better outcomes with a structured feedback approach than usual care. Tilden and colleagues' later randomized couple-and-family-therapy trial found no significant outcome advantage from its monitoring system. These were different systems and studies, not a direct comparison between tools. Neither establishes the effectiveness of every practice gathered under this editorial pattern. [Anker et al. (2009)](https://digitalcommons.uri.edu/hdf_facpubs/220/), [Tilden et al. (2020)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31497883/)

### Reconsider something identifiable

The response might concern timing, a task, participation, a goal, the therapist's interpretation, or access to another kind of help. It can also be a decision to continue while clarifying an uncertainty. Constant change is not the objective.

An improvement after an intervention does not establish that the intervention caused it. Medication, changing pain, working hours, housing, or events elsewhere may matter. Likewise, deterioration deserves attention without automatically proving that therapy caused it. The next decision needs both responsiveness and care about what the evidence can show.

## Worked examples and case studies

### Less arguing, but less opportunity to finish

*Fictional teaching scenario. All people, dialogue, circumstances, and outcomes are invented.*

Tess wants difficult evenings to feel less overwhelming. Daniel wants disagreements about household spending addressed without a fight. Both welcomed the possibility of pausing, and the therapist initially treats their report of fewer arguments as progress.

Asked what happens afterward, Daniel describes repeatedly postponing a conversation about a rent increase. Tess explains that she experiences the pause as relief, then dreads restarting. He worries that asking again will count as refusing to respect her need for space.

The therapist asks, “Which concerns have you been able to return to, and what happens to the ones you haven't?” Neither can name a completed discussion. This question changes the review: the issue is not whether pausing is good or bad in general, but whether it supports the particular conversation they wanted to have.

![A fictional couple reports fewer arguments. Tess describes welcome relief, while Daniel describes concerns that remain unfinished. Both accounts are retained alongside the same observation. A green review question asks whether they can pause and later return to a concern; it does not average their experiences into a single success rating.](../assets/systemic-therapy/feedback-different-effects.svg 'The observation stays the same while different experiences change what needs reviewing. Relief can be real and the original purpose still unmet. This is a fictional qualitative account, not measured improvement or evidence about what caused the result.')

The therapist acknowledges having counted reduced conflict without checking what happened to unresolved concerns. Tess is relieved that she is not being asked simply to tolerate more intense discussion. Daniel appreciates having the unfinished issue recognized. They agree that the next review should include whether returning to a concern feels possible and whether one person must repeatedly chase it.

In this invented continuation, they discuss one spending decision after a pause but leave another unresolved. Tess still needs room to recover, and Daniel still wants a more dependable way back. The rent increase remains real. Feedback has changed the criterion used to judge the arrangement; it has not settled their finances or shown that pauses are generally helpful or unhelpful.

### The positive forms that did not mean agreement

*Fictional teaching scenario. All people, circumstances, dialogue, and outcomes are invented.*

Ravi and Nila attend family sessions with their eight-year-old daughter Mila. They want calmer transitions between work, after-school care, and home. A professional interpreter supports Ravi's participation, but the clinic's written feedback form is available only in the local language. Nila usually completes it for the family.

The forms are consistently positive. The therapist assumes the schedule and approach fit. Yet Ravi misses two sessions. Asked about commitment, he explains that the appointment requires losing paid work. Nila has been marking the forms positively because she appreciates the help and fears that criticism could jeopardize it. Mila says the long conversations are boring and that she wants someone to ask about the after-school journey.

The therapist recognizes that one person's completed form was being treated as three people's feedback. Through the interpreter, Ravi is offered space to describe the appointment's effects in his own words. Mila can draw and explain what matters to her. Nila is asked what works and what remains difficult, rather than to supply an overall family verdict.

The therapist explains how feedback will be used and shared, including the limits of the clinic's control over appointments. The family identifies a mixed picture: the sessions have helped Nila understand some arguments, but attendance is costly, and Mila's practical concern has received little attention.

In this fictional continuation, the clinic offers one later appointment and the next session includes the journey. Ravi attends, although future scheduling remains uncertain. Nila still values the existing work. Mila contributes a detail the adults had missed. The important revision is to the feedback process itself: gratitude, language access, age, financial pressure, and fear of losing support had shaped what could be said. None can be inferred from this family's cultural background alone.

### A client's correction of the therapist's explanation

*Published individual-therapy case, adapted from Helleseth, McAleavey, and Moltu (2023). Sonja is the paper's pseudonym. This is an individual-therapy report included for its close examination of client feedback, not a study of systemic family treatment.*

Sonja struggled with distress and trust. Her therapist used an emotion-focused approach alongside repeated reports from Norse Feedback. The paper draws on those reports, clinical records, and the therapist's notes. It also includes Sonja's response to the researchers' account after therapy had ended.

Sonja had wanted more focus on emotions. Later, however, she recalled an intensified intervention as unfamiliar and overwhelming. She interrupted the work and remained glad that she had set that limit. Wanting a general direction had not meant being prepared for the particular intervention delivered.

During a later walkthrough of the study's interpretations, Sonja also challenged part of the therapist's explanation. She agreed that avoiding difficult inner experience had mattered, but disputed the importance assigned to reducing it for symptom relief. She described better quality of life and functioning while continuing to struggle with that avoidance.

Her account was not a wholesale rejection. She valued the feedback process and believed it had helped her communicate distress and develop trust. She also described continuing difficulties and pain around the ending of therapy. The paper preserves this mixture rather than making appreciation evidence that everything fitted. [Helleseth and colleagues: A dual case study](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1199431/full)

For this field guide, the important move is inviting the client to correct the professional account of what happened and what helped. The later conversation could revise the explanation presented in the research; it could not undo an earlier overwhelming encounter. No subsequent therapeutic repair is invented here.

This is a selected case, not causal evidence for a feedback system. The authors acknowledge their involvement in developing that system and the possibility that the therapist's perspective is overrepresented. Those limitations belong beside the insights the account offers.

## Conditions for useful application

People need a credible opportunity to say something unwelcome without being punished, argued out of it, or made responsible for reassuring the therapist. The purpose of feedback, who will see it, and relevant confidentiality limits should be understandable. Accessible communication and appropriate ways for children to contribute affect whose experience becomes available.

Not every account can safely be shared in a joint session. Where there is coercion or abuse, asking someone to criticize an arrangement in front of the person harming them may create risk. NICE recommends private, one-to-one enquiry in a safe setting. Reported relief or disagreement does not redistribute responsibility for harmful actions. [NICE, PH50, recommendation 6](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ph50/chapter/Recommendations#recommendation-6-ensure-trained-staff-ask-people-about-domestic-violence-and-abuse)

## When to use it

Feedback matters throughout therapy, especially after a change, when progress stalls, when people disagree about whether things are better, or when someone seems less able to participate. It can also inform ending, referral, or continuation.

Two brief fictional contrasts show its range. A caregiver reports better coordination but less sleep because the new arrangement gives them more work. Elsewhere, a teenager dislikes a session yet values having finally disagreed openly. A practical gain can carry a cost; immediate satisfaction and therapeutic usefulness need not coincide.

An urgent danger or medical concern requires a response, not another round of ratings before action. Reviewing uncertainty should not become a way of postponing necessary help.

## Tradeoffs

Feedback takes time and can expose needs a service cannot meet. Repeated questionnaires may be tiring, inaccessible, or less useful than a short conversation. Additional detail is worthwhile when it changes understanding or a decision.

Responding also requires judgment. A difficult session is not necessarily harmful, and a pleasant one is not necessarily helpful. The therapist must take distress seriously without asking the client to accept a predetermined explanation of it. Sometimes the honest result is disagreement about what happened, followed by a decision about how to proceed despite that uncertainty.

## Common mistakes and limitations

**Collecting without responding.** Repeatedly requesting an account while never acknowledging it can make participation feel pointless. An explanation of what can and cannot change is itself part of the response.

**Flattening differences into an average.** One overall family rating can conceal whose burden increased or whose priorities were excluded.

**Rewarding praise.** If positive feedback receives warmth and criticism receives a defense, the process teaches people which answer is welcome.

**Treating the measure as the person's meaning.** A score is information obtained under particular conditions. Language, interpretation, privacy, and expectations affect what it represents.

**Protecting a theory from correction.** Explaining every objection as avoidance or resistance makes the therapist's interpretation impossible to question. A useful explanation must remain open to revision.

**Demanding proof from one change.** A better week is worth understanding but does not establish a cause or a durable result. The pattern supports responsive judgment, not certainty on demand.
