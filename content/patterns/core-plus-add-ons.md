## What it is for

Use this pattern when customers share a basic need but differ in the convenience, capabilities, or support they want around it.

Two customers buy the same desk. One collects and assembles it. The other wants delivery and help putting it together. Both want a usable desk; they value the surrounding services differently.

Core plus Add-Ons provides a common offering with separately priced extensions.

The business can keep its entry proposition focused while earning additional revenue from customers who want more. Customers can adapt their purchase without paying for every possible benefit.

A strong version begins with a core that delivers its stated benefit for its intended customer.

## The core idea

Build the offering around a stable center, then let customers extend it through optional purchases.

The core might be a product, software subscription, journey, or service. Add-ons might provide installation, specialized functionality, personalization, extra capacity, or continuing support.

The relationship is asymmetric: the core anchors the purchase, while the extras enhance its use. An add-on may have little value without that core.

This distinguishes the pattern from Unbundling, which describes separating a combined offering into purchasable parts. Core plus Add-Ons describes the resulting commercial structure—and can also be designed from the beginning.

It also differs from fixed tiers. Customers choose particular extensions rather than moving to a larger package containing several benefits.

## How it creates value

**It separates common needs from variable ones.** The business can standardize what most customers need while reserving specialized benefits for those who value them.

**It gives convenience a price.** Some customers willingly perform tasks themselves. Others prefer to pay for delivery, setup, or assistance.

**It allows the relationship to develop.** A customer may begin with the core and add capabilities when a new need appears. Every extension does not have to be sold at the first transaction.

**It makes specialization economical.** A capability useful to a smaller group can have its own revenue stream rather than being funded by every customer.

The boundary between core and extra matters. A feature can be optional for one audience and essential for another. The offer needs to make clear which customers the core can serve on its own.

## Famous example: IKEA

IKEA combines furniture designed for customer assembly with services that help customers transport and assemble their purchases.

Its US services page explicitly describes a choice between doing those tasks yourself and using services such as delivery and assembly through Taskrabbit. [IKEA: Services](https://www.ikea.com/us/en/customer-service/services/)

The furniture anchors the transaction. The service adds convenience for customers who lack the time, transport, tools, or inclination to handle the work themselves.

This lets a common product serve customers with different preferences about effort and spending.

The operating challenge extends beyond offering the option. Availability, scheduling, handoffs, and responsibility must fit together. When a partner provides the service, its performance still shapes the customer’s experience of the overall purchase.

## Famous example: Shopify

Shopify provides core commerce functionality that merchants can extend through apps.

Its guidance asks merchants to identify needs outside the platform’s core features, then consider apps that help with activities such as marketing, selling, and shipping. Apps can carry recurring, one-time, or usage-based charges. [Shopify: Finding and Choosing Apps](https://help.shopify.com/en/manual/apps/finding-choosing-apps)

The same platform can therefore support businesses with different workflows without placing every specialized capability in the default offering.

This also shows how add-ons can support an ecosystem. An extension may be developed and sold by another company, so the additional payment does not necessarily all go to the core provider.

The limitation is cumulative complexity. Several useful apps can create a substantial combined bill, overlapping functions, and compatibility or support dependencies.

## Famous example: Tesla Premium Connectivity

Tesla offers Premium Connectivity as a paid subscription for eligible vehicles.

The service adds connected features such as satellite-view maps, live traffic visualization, and media streaming over cellular connections. Availability depends on the vehicle and its configuration; some media services require separate subscriptions. [Tesla: Connectivity](https://www.tesla.com/support/connectivity)

The vehicle is the core purchase. The subscription extends part of the ownership experience and can be purchased after the vehicle has been delivered.

This illustrates an add-on with continuing value and continuing charges. It also shows why dependencies matter: paying for connectivity and paying for a particular entertainment service can be separate decisions.

The commercial opportunity depends on whether owners continue to value the additional convenience enough to renew.

## The economics

The business earns from the core and from the extras customers select.

An important measure is the **attachment rate**: the proportion of core purchases or customers that also buy a particular add-on.

Consider a hypothetical one-time purchase. The core contributes $20 after its variable costs. An optional service contributes another $12, and one-quarter of customers choose it. Average contribution becomes $23 per core purchase, before customer acquisition costs and shared overhead.

That calculation depends on the add-on’s actual delivery costs. Installation, support, returns, partner payments, and infrastructure can consume much of its selling price.

The core does not necessarily need to recover every cost immediately. However, relying on later add-on purchases makes the model sensitive to attachment rates, customer retention, and the timing of payment.

Recurring extras create another obligation: the business must continue delivering something worth paying for.

## Useful when

This pattern works well when a substantial group can use the same core, while smaller groups value different enhancements.

It fits products with optional services, software with specialized extensions, and offerings where customer needs develop over time.

It is especially useful when an extra is easy to explain, practical to deliver separately, and valuable enough to justify its price.

The fit weakens when nearly everyone needs the same extensions or when the number of choices makes the purchase difficult to understand.

## Common mistakes and limits

**Making the core’s promise depend on an undisclosed extra.** Customers need to know whether the base purchase will accomplish what they expect.

**Using the entry price as the whole story.** The likely total, including recurring charges and dependencies, should be understandable during selection.

**Assuming add-ons are high margin.** A service can command a substantial price and still be expensive to fulfill.

**Creating too many combinations.** Every extension adds potential interactions, training needs, and support questions. Optionality has an operating cost.

**Selling before the need appears.** Repeated upgrade prompts can interrupt the core experience. An extension is easier to understand when its benefit is relevant.

**Leaving cancellation consequences unclear.** Customers need to understand which capabilities end, which remain, and what happens to their work or data when a recurring add-on stops.
