A person can know many individual facts while still lacking a sense of how they fit together. The result is knowledge that feels more like a drawer full of objects than a navigable landscape.

Reconstruct the Map means putting the source aside and rebuilding the structure of a subject from memory. Someone studying jazz history might sketch the relationships among blues, swing, bebop, cool jazz, modal jazz, free jazz, major musicians, and historical periods.

The point is not to produce a beautiful diagram. The missing connections, misplaced branches, and uncertain relationships reveal which parts of the subject have not yet formed a coherent structure.

The map can take many forms. A historian might draw a timeline with causal arrows. A biologist might sketch relationships among systems. A reader of philosophy might arrange thinkers by disagreements rather than chronology. A cook might organize dozens of recipes into broader families such as braises, emulsions, doughs, and stocks.

As the structure becomes clearer, compression often follows naturally. Several individual facts become instances of one broader principle, and dozens of details acquire places inside a smaller number of meaningful categories.

Good compression does not simply make the notes shorter. It preserves relationships. A subway map omits almost everything about a city, but it keeps what matters for navigating the rail system. A good knowledge map does something similar.

There is also a danger in making the map too tidy. Real subjects contain overlap, exceptions, feedback loops, and competing interpretations. The purpose is not to force everything into a perfect tree.

The aim is to create enough structure that the details stop floating independently. Facts answer local questions; a map lets you move through the territory.
