A person has described an old friendship many times. They can explain why it ended and understand the other person's limitations. Yet one sentence remains difficult to say: "You mattered to me, and I was hurt that you disappeared." Speaking about the friend has not been the same as addressing them.

## Core idea

Empty-Chair Dialogue brings an imagined significant other into an experiential encounter. The person may express feelings, needs, questions, or unfinished messages directly to that representation. Gestalt and Emotion-Focused Therapy use this form in different ways; the common move is from a report about a relationship to an enacted address.

An empty chair can help give the encounter a location, but the furniture is not essential to every adaptation. What matters is whether the person can engage with the emotionally relevant other while retaining awareness of the actual session.

The defining move is direct address to a representation of a significant person. A sentence about them becomes a sentence to them, which can change emotional contact and clarify what has remained unfinished. The representation is held as an imaginative therapeutic construction, not a channel to the other person's actual thoughts.

## What changes in the experience

In the friendship example, the person may imagine the friend's familiar manner and find words that were previously softened or omitted. The therapist helps keep contact with the person's experience rather than demand a complete speech. The difficulty in saying the sentence may itself be important.

The imagined friend's reactions are not evidence of the actual friend's motives. If the person imagines a dismissive reply, that can reveal an expectation or a representation. It does not establish how the real person would respond today.

Direct address can change pronouns, voice, posture, and emotional contact. The person encounters what it is like to make a request or protest rather than merely describe having one. The therapist can attend to barriers, expectations, and unmet needs as they appear.

A person may discover that an explanation conceals a request, that a protest has never been stated plainly, or that they no longer want to address the image. The imagined other's response can also reveal an expectation. The therapist follows these developments without supplying the absent person's motives or requiring a reparative answer from them.

## A worked example

Another person remains angry about a parent's repeated criticism. Their account includes detailed arguments about who was right. Addressing the imagined parent, they discover a simpler message: "I wanted you to be interested before you corrected me."

That statement may bring grief, anger, relief, or more uncertainty. The task need not end in forgiveness, understanding the parent, or a benevolent imagined response. A clearer recognition of what was missing can be meaningful without reconciliation.

The person might also discover that speaking to the image feels artificial or unwanted. They can remain in ordinary conversation. A therapist should not interpret reluctance as proof that an unresolved relationship must be confronted more forcefully.

Suppose the client starts by listing every unfair criticism. The therapist wonders about a wish for approval, but the person says that approval would still leave the old arrangement intact. What they wanted was interest in how they themselves understood their work. Addressing the image, they find a sentence about wanting questions rather than evaluation.

The imagined parent remains dismissive. Instead of forcing a kinder reply, the work turns toward the grief of not having received that interest. The client can recognize what was missing and consider where reciprocal interest exists now. The dialogue ends unfinished in a literal sense, yet with a clearer need. It has not required forgiveness or created evidence about what the real parent might say today.

## Why the move may help

Direct address can make the stakes of a relationship more immediate than third-person explanation. A person encounters the vulnerability of asking, the force of a protest, or the sadness of an unanswered need. These experiences may help move a repetitive account toward a more specific understanding.

Gestalt and EFT use empty-chair work with differing formulations. A vivid or emotionally powerful enactment does not establish historical accuracy, reveal the absent person's unconscious motives, or guarantee durable change. The meaningful observations concern the client's experience and what becomes more possible or clearer through it.

## When it helps—and what can get in the way

The pattern is useful when a particular relationship remains emotionally unfinished, when explanations repeatedly circle the same ground, or when important words have no available real-world recipient. It can involve someone who is absent, deceased, estranged, or unsuitable to contact.

An imagined encounter does not require later real-world contact. Deciding whether to speak to the actual person is a separate judgment involving consent, safety, purpose, and likely consequences.

The pattern may fit recurring unfinished communication with a particular person, including someone absent or deceased. It may fit poorly when the client finds imagined dialogue artificial or primarily needs assistance with an actual current negotiation. A well-facilitated conversation about the relationship can be useful without enacting it.

## Boundaries and common misunderstandings

Chair work can evoke more than the person expected. The practitioner needs to monitor engagement, pacing, and the ability to stop. Ending an unresolved dialogue still requires attention to present orientation and what the person needs before leaving.

Common mistakes include demanding a dramatic breakthrough, supplying the other person's supposed motives, and treating a forgiving ending as the only successful one. Sometimes a firm boundary, acknowledgment of loss, or refusal to continue the imagined conversation fits better.

The therapist should not confuse acceptance of an imagined exchange with readiness for real contact. Reaching out can involve risks and obligations that a session cannot simulate fully. Ending may include acknowledging what remains unresolved and leaving the representation behind, rather than insisting on emotional completion before returning to the present.
