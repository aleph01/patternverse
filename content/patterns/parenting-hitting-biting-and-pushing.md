## 1. A recognizable situation

Your three-year-old is building on the floor when another child reaches for a piece. Before you can intervene, your child pushes them over. The other child is crying. Yours is shouting, “It was mine!”

You want your child to know that anger is allowed. You also need to protect the child who was hurt, and you may feel angry or embarrassed yourself.

These responsibilities require different actions at different moments. **First stop further harm and care for the injured person. Then work out what happened and what would help prevent another incident.** Understanding the behavior should make your response more effective; the injured child should not have to wait for that understanding before receiving protection.

## 2. What might be happening

Hitting, biting, and pushing describe actions, rather than a single underlying problem. Several explanations can fit apparently similar incidents.

- **An immediate response to frustration.** A toy disappears, a sibling knocks something down, or an adult ends an activity. Notice whether aggression happens within seconds of disappointment, before the child attempts another response.

- **An attempt to obtain something or create distance.** Pushing may get a child to the front of a queue; biting may make someone stop grabbing them. Observe what changes immediately afterward. This can help explain repetition without assuming calculated manipulation.

- **Communication that exceeds the child’s available abilities.** A child may lack a usable way to say “Stop touching me,” ask for help, or negotiate a turn. Even a child who speaks fluently when calm may struggle to use those abilities during conflict.

- **Mouthing, teething, or exploration in a very young child.** A baby biting a parent’s shoulder is not necessarily experiencing the same problem as a school-age child biting during a fight. Early-childhood guidance recognizes these different possibilities. [NAEYC: Understanding and Responding to Children Who Bite](https://www.naeyc.org/our-work/families/understanding-and-responding-children-who-bite)

- **A difficult or threatening situation.** Crowding, repeated unwanted touching, bullying, painful care routines, exhaustion, or overwhelming demands deserve investigation. Aggression may also occur during rough play that has exceeded someone’s tolerance.

For repeated incidents, briefly record **what happened immediately before, the exact action, and what happened afterward**. “Bit during the crowded wait for lunch; other child moved away” is more useful than “Aggressive again.”

These observations generate hypotheses. They do not establish a diagnosis or prove that the child acted for one particular reason.

## 3. How development changes the picture

**Babies and young toddlers need adults to do much of the prevention.** Move a biting mouth away from skin, interrupt hitting, and provide an appropriate alternative when mouthing or teething seems relevant. A lengthy explanation about another person’s feelings asks more than the child can currently use.

**During the toddler years, knowing a rule and reliably following it are different achievements.** A child may say “No hitting” afterward and still need an adult close enough to interrupt the next conflict. Responses should be brief and concrete, with practice outside the incident.

Physical aggression commonly increases during early childhood before declining. In a longitudinal Quebec cohort of 2,223 children, average aggression increased between approximately 18 months and three-and-a-half years, then decreased. This was an **observational developmental pattern**, not evidence that every child will outgrow aggression without assistance. The predominantly White sample, changing informants, and loss of participants over time also limit generalization. [Teymoori and colleagues, 2018](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2719575)

**Preschoolers can increasingly rehearse alternatives**, such as stepping back, calling an adult, or asking for a turn. Nevertheless, expecting them to manage every contested toy or crowded transition independently is unrealistic. Adults should gradually reduce assistance as the child demonstrates the relevant ability.

**School-age children can participate more actively in investigating incidents and planning repairs.** Ask about peer relationships, classroom demands, and what happened before the visible aggression. Repeated or escalating physical aggression at this stage warrants closer assessment rather than simply reusing toddler advice.

Chronological age is only part of the picture. Communication, attention, learning, and sensory differences may change the support a child needs. Expectations should reflect those abilities while maintaining protection for everyone involved.

## 4. What needs balancing

Several legitimate needs can pull against one another:

- **The injured child needs care, protection, and control over further contact.** They should not have to accept a hug, apology, or immediate return to shared play.

- **The child who acted needs help developing a usable alternative.** Shame or a lengthy reprimand does not show them how to handle the next crowded doorway or disputed possession.

- **Anger and bodily boundaries deserve respect.** Children need ways to refuse unwanted contact and defend their space without routinely injuring others.

- **Adults have practical limits.** A parent cooking dinner or an educator supervising a group cannot provide uninterrupted individual coaching.

A workable plan must therefore address supervision and the environment, alongside the child’s skills. “Watch more carefully” is inadequate unless someone identifies when, where, and how that supervision can happen.

## 5. Possible responses

The approaches below combine professional guidance with practical synthesis. The research is stronger for **complete interventions for persistent disruptive behavior** than for any particular sentence used immediately after a bite or push.

### A. Interrupt harm and organize immediate care

**This fits every incident where someone may be hurt.**

Move between the children or separate them, using only the physical intervention necessary to prevent immediate injury. Remove a dangerous object or move the other child out of reach. Avoid turning the intervention into a struggle.

Use a short statement:

> “I won’t let you hit. I’m moving you apart.”

Once further contact is prevented, attend to the injured child. If two adults are available, divide responsibilities. If you are alone, keep both children safely supervised while providing care. Separating children and comforting the child who was hurt are established professional recommendations; they are not techniques supported by trials comparing exact wording. [AACAP: Fighting and Biting](https://aacap.org/AACAP/Families_and_Youth/Facts_for_Families/FFF-Guide/Fighting-And-Biting-081.aspx)

The child who hit may continue crying or protesting. You can acknowledge that without allowing renewed contact:

> “You wanted your building left alone. I’m staying here so nobody gets pushed.”

If a **human bite breaks the skin**, wash it with soap and water, cover it with a clean dressing, and obtain prompt medical advice. Large or deep wounds, or bleeding that will not stop, need emergency assessment. [NHS: Animal and Human Bites](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/animal-and-human-bites/)

### B. Change the conditions around a recurring incident

**This fits behavior that clusters around particular situations.**

Choose one recurring situation and make a specific change:

- If pushing occurs while everyone puts on shoes, stagger departures or provide separate spaces.

- If biting happens when peers crowd a toddler, arrange an adult nearby during that part of the day.

- If a child hits when a task becomes inaccessible, simplify the task and teach a way to request assistance or a short break.

- If rough play repeatedly ends in injury, stop it earlier and establish a clear signal that ends contact immediately.

Child Australia’s professional resource emphasizes observing incidents, crowding, competition for materials, and coordination between families and educators. It is practice guidance rather than experimental proof that each environmental adjustment independently reduces biting. [*When Children Bite*](https://www.acecqa.gov.au/sites/default/files/2021-01/WhenChildrenBite.PDF)

My practical recommendation is to test changes against the observed pattern. If incidents happen during one noisy transition, reorganizing that transition is a more informative first step than buying a product advertised to promote calm.

For childcare or school, ask for a prevention plan with named responsibilities: **Who will be nearby? What early signs will they respond to? What will they help the child do?** Incident reports alone document injury; they do not provide prevention.

### C. Teach an alternative that can actually work

**This fits once everyone is safe and the child can participate.**

Match the alternative to the situation:

- For unwanted contact: say or gesture “Stop,” move away, and approach an adult.

- For a contested object: ask for adult help arranging a turn.

- For overwhelming frustration: put the object down and request help before returning.

- For school conflict: leave the immediate confrontation and use an agreed route to a responsible adult.

With a toddler, demonstrate one action in a brief, calm practice. With an older child, reconstruct the sequence: “When did you first notice you wanted to push? What could you do at that point?”

The alternative must receive an effective adult response. “Use your words” becomes hollow if saying “Stop” repeatedly leaves the child unprotected.

Notice the replacement behavior specifically:

> “You moved back and called me when he grabbed you. I’ll help keep your space clear.”

Afterward, support repair: returning an object, helping rebuild something, or checking whether assistance is wanted. Offer options rather than requiring physical affection. NAEYC recommends teaching alternatives and avoiding labels such as “biter”; these are professional recommendations, not evidence that a particular repair ritual prevents recurrence. [NAEYC guidance](https://www.naeyc.org/our-work/families/understanding-and-responding-children-who-bite)

The dialogue here illustrates the approach. Its usefulness depends on timing, adult follow-through, and the child’s abilities.

### D. Use structured support when aggression persists or causes significant harm

**This fits repeated injury, escalating incidents, disrupted relationships, or a family struggling to maintain safety.**

Ask a pediatrician, primary-care clinician, or appropriate child professional for an assessment and an evidence-based intervention suited to the child’s age and needs. Bring observations from home and school, including situations where the child manages successfully.

A 2026 meta-analysis pooled **64 randomized trials involving preschool and school-age children**. Parent-focused and multicomponent interventions reduced parent-reported disruptive behavior immediately after treatment compared with usual care or waiting lists. Longer-term findings were less consistent; adolescent evidence was limited and could not be pooled. These were broad disruptive-behavior outcomes, not studies exclusively about biting or pushing. [Selph and colleagues, 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41525996/)

This provides causal evidence for the evaluated **programs as packages**. It does not establish that praise, emotion naming, or any single consequence produces the same result alone.

For children with significant clinical difficulties, guidelines also distinguish age-appropriate interventions: parent training has an important role in younger children, while older children may receive additional social and problem-solving work. These recommendations concern assessed clinical needs, rather than every child who occasionally hits. [NICE recommendations](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg158/chapter/Recommendations)

## 6. Examples in practice

**A two-year-old bites during crowded play.**

You separate the children and care for the child who was bitten. Your child remains furious and reaches toward them again. You maintain distance and stay nearby rather than repeating a lecture.

Later, you practice stepping back and raising a hand for help. The following day, an adult stays close during the crowded activity and intervenes earlier. Success initially means fewer opportunities for injury and more adult-assisted alternatives; the child may still become upset.

**A four-year-old’s first alternative fails.**

Your child pushes a peer after being repeatedly grabbed. You had previously taught, “Say stop,” and discover that they did—but nobody responded.

You still interrupt the pushing. Afterward, you address the failed protection: the educator agrees to respond to a raised hand, and the children receive more space during that activity. Teaching another phrase without changing adult responsiveness would leave the original problem largely intact.

**An eight-year-old repeatedly hits a younger sibling during games.**

You stop the game and separate them. The younger child chooses whether to play again later.

When calm, you investigate the sequence. Is the older child losing, being provoked, struggling with complicated rules, or using force to control the game? The next attempt might involve a shorter game, nearby supervision, and a practiced response to frustration. Repeatedly targeting a smaller sibling also requires assessment of the power imbalance; both children need individual opportunities to describe what happens.

## 7. Common mistakes and limitations

**Treating developmental commonness as permission to wait.** Toddler aggression can be common and still require active prevention. Other children should not absorb repeated injury while adults wait for maturation.

**Expecting understanding to produce immediate control.** A preschooler’s accurate explanation afterward does not establish that they can independently stop the action next time.

**Using injury to teach about injury.** Biting back, hitting, or intimidating the child introduces further harm. Professional guidance advises against physical retaliation. [AACAP guidance](https://aacap.org/AACAP/Families_and_Youth/Facts_for_Families/FFF-Guide/Fighting-And-Biting-081.aspx)

**Making the apology the main outcome.** A prompted “sorry” provides little information about whether the child can manage the next conflict. Repair matters, but the injured child retains the right to distance.

**Assuming everyone involved has equal responsibility.** Stop the immediate harm, then investigate grabbing, threats, bullying, and repeated targeting. The last visible push may not reveal the full situation.

**Presenting one consequence as universally necessary—or universally harmful.** Brief, structured time-out appears in CDC guidance for children aged two to four. That does not justify prolonged isolation or establish that every child needs the technique. Whole-program evidence cannot settle every question about an isolated component. [CDC guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/parenting-toddlers/responding-to-behavior/time-out.html)

Finally, the strongest treatment evidence here concerns reductions in reported disruptive behavior. It provides less certainty about lasting friendship quality, trust, independence, or emotional wellbeing. Those outcomes deserve attention even when incident counts improve.

## 8. What to observe over time

Track more than whether the child apologizes or appears calm afterward:

- Are incidents less frequent or less injurious?

- Is the child beginning to step back, signal, or seek help before contact?

- Can adults gradually reduce supervision without renewed harm?

- Does the child who was hurt feel protected?

- Can children participate safely in ordinary play and family life?

Interpret the numbers in context. Fewer incidents because a child has lost all opportunities to play with others is different from safer participation.

Reconsider the explanation if the plan produces little improvement. A supposed sharing problem may involve unwanted touching. Apparent defiance may cluster around an inaccessible school task. Sudden aggression may call for attention to pain, sleep, frightening experiences, or changes in relationships.

Seek additional support when aggression causes injuries, escalates, leads to exclusion, repeatedly targets someone vulnerable, or makes family members afraid. Frequent aggression in an older child deserves assessment even if it has become familiar at home. The American Academy of Pediatrics identifies injury, attacks on adults, exclusion, and concern for safety as reasons to consult a pediatrician. [AAP: Preventing Aggressive Behavior](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/toddler/pages/Aggressive-Behavior.aspx)

Serious injury or an immediate inability to keep people safe requires urgent help.
