**Core question:** How can an organization respond to different customers while developing products and capabilities that many markets can share?

## What it is for

Imagine a hypothetical industrial technology company serving food producers and logistics operators. Both use its sensors, controls, and monitoring software, but their operating problems differ. Separate industry divisions would duplicate product development. Organizing entirely around products leaves customers assembling an answer from several sales teams.

Front-Back Organization creates two complementary kinds of unit: a front organized around customers or markets, and a back organized around products or capabilities. They work together to shape and deliver offerings. The pattern applies across businesses, regions, or customer segments where market understanding and reusable capabilities both warrant substantial organizational attention.

## The core idea

**Organize the front around the customers to be served and the back around shared offerings, then give both a workable way to make interdependent decisions.**

The front understands a customer’s situation and brings together a relevant offer. The back develops and sustains products, technologies, or delivery capabilities used across those relationships. Neither can succeed independently.

Jay Galbraith describes the front-back model as a modification of matrix design, combining customer, country, or segment orientation with product orientation. Its purpose is to accommodate market differences while retaining scale in the product organization. [Galbraith, _The Evolution of Enterprise Organization Designs_, 2012, figure 4](https://jaygalbraith.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/EvolEnterpriseOrgDesign.pdf)

The defining feature is this division of responsibilities, rather than every employee having two managers. It also goes beyond a sales department passing orders to production: the customer side needs meaningful influence over plans, and the product side needs authority over what can be delivered reliably.

## How it works

![Two customer-facing units serve food producers and logistics operators. Both connect through joint planning to shared sensors, controls, and monitoring software units. Customer needs inform product priorities, and product capabilities constrain customer commitments.](../assets/organizational-design/front-back-organization.svg)

**Choose the front’s organizing basis.** Use customer segments, named accounts, channels, or territories where needs and relationships differ. Give each unit responsibility for understanding demand, developing customer plans, and coordinating the offer. Avoid several front units making competing promises to the same customer.

**Define the back’s reusable contribution.** Product or capability units own development, quality, lifecycle decisions, and relevant delivery capacity. They maintain offerings that multiple front units can use. Specialist staff can still meet customers directly; the structure should improve understanding rather than create an information barrier.

**Make the joint decisions explicit.** Specify who approves prices, delivery dates, configuration changes, and investment in new features. The front cannot promise unavailable capacity; the back cannot ignore important market requirements. Commercial responsibility and profit accountability can be distributed differently, but internal prices and targets need to support viable customer offers.

**Connect planning and information.** Bring demand forecasts, customer evidence, roadmaps, capacity, and delivery results into joint reviews. Assign an owner for each combined customer offer. Product leaders need to see recurring needs across markets; account leaders need early warning of product changes and constraints.

**Resolve exceptions at the right level.** A request outside the standard offering needs a decision about feasibility, funding, ongoing support, and displaced work. Cross-unit leaders resolve conflicts that exceed local authority. Repeated escalation signals a missing rule, inadequate capacity, or an unsuitable boundary.

In the industrial technology illustration, the food-sector front proposes a monitoring package. Product specialists check whether existing sensors and software meet the requirement. A genuinely new capability enters a joint investment decision instead of becoming an uncosted promise to one customer.

## Why it works

The front can build knowledge across a customer’s needs instead of concentrating on one product sale. The back can invest in capabilities that serve several markets, spreading development effort and concentrating specialist expertise.

Connecting them creates a route for local information to influence common offerings. A need first identified in one account may become a reusable feature; an existing product may solve problems in several segments without separate development teams.

The benefit depends on integration work. Galbraith’s November 2005 account emphasizes collective leadership, transparent information, and management processes linking the two sides. Reporting lines alone cannot settle their competing priorities. [Galbraith, _The Front-Back Model: How Does It Work?_, pages 1–2](https://jaygalbraith.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/FrontBackHowitWorks.pdf)

## Examples

### P&G in 2006: global brands and local market plans

P&G’s 2006 annual report describes Global Business Units responsible for brand strategy and innovation, alongside a Market Development Organization with retail-customer, channel, and country teams. The MDO translated innovations into local business plans. Its Global Operations group contained both the MDO and the separate Global Business Services organization; the business-unit and Global Operations heads reported to the CEO. [P&G, _2006 Annual Report_, organizational structure](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/80424/000095015206007291/l21828aexv13.htm)

This historical arrangement separated developing a brand’s offering from organizing its route to different markets. Local teams could draw on several businesses while product leaders maintained a broader innovation agenda. The resulting dependency was substantial: launches needed both a product pipeline and workable local execution plans.

Shared business services supported that arrangement but were a distinct part of it. The example therefore illustrates why the product-oriented back should not be confused with administrative support. P&G attributed benefits to its structure; the report alone does not isolate the structure’s causal effect on performance.

### Ericsson: market areas connected to business areas

Ericsson’s organization page, consulted in October 2026, describes market areas responsible for selling and delivering customer solutions. Business areas develop and maintain product and service offerings and secure delivery capability. Its executive team includes leaders from both kinds of area, alongside group functions. [Ericsson, _Our organization_](https://www.ericsson.com/en/about-us/company-facts/our-organization)

Read through the front-back lens, market areas provide the customer-facing organization while business areas sustain capabilities that those markets draw upon. Delivery is not simply assigned to a remote back office: the company explicitly gives market areas a delivery role too.

The practical consequence is a continuing dependency between the offer made in a market and the products, services, and capacity maintained across markets. This interpretation illustrates the pattern’s allocation of responsibilities. Ericsson’s description establishes those roles, without documenting every pricing decision, escalation rule, or individual reporting relationship.

## Useful when

- **Customers need several offerings together.** Product-by-product relationships make them coordinate the organization themselves.
- **Markets differ, but capabilities overlap.** Local requirements matter while substantial development can be reused.
- **Separate divisions duplicate expensive expertise.** Similar products or technologies are repeatedly built for different segments.
- **Both sides can sustain joint planning.** Customer and product leaders have the authority and information to make credible commitments.

## Common mistakes and limits

**Giving only one side real authority.** A powerless front becomes an order taker; a powerless back accumulates bespoke commitments it cannot sustain. Name the decisions each owns and the ones requiring agreement.

**Rewarding incompatible results.** Sales targets can encourage promises that destroy product margins or delay other customers. Product efficiency targets can discourage worthwhile adaptations. Assess the economics and customer outcome across the boundary.

**Treating customer knowledge as the front’s exclusive property.** Filtered requirements can hide the problem a customer actually faces. Direct contact and shared evidence help specialists judge what to build.

**Adding coordination without enough reuse.** Joint reviews, negotiations, and overlapping accountability consume time. A smaller organization or a business with little overlap between markets may be better served by a simpler functional or divisional structure.

**Leaving the top leader to settle everything.** Frequent cross-unit disputes require usable delegated authority and collective leadership capacity. Otherwise, the organization’s responsiveness depends on one person’s availability.

## Related patterns

- [[divisional-organization|Divisional Organization]] groups capabilities inside business units; front-back shares selected product capabilities across customer-facing units.
- [[matrix-organization|Matrix Organization]] distributes authority across intersecting dimensions. Front-back specifies a particular division between customer and product responsibilities and may use matrix reporting to connect them.
- [[shared-services|Shared Services]] pools defined services across units. It can support both sides, while the back here owns capabilities central to the customer offering.
