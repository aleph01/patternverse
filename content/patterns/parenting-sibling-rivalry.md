## 1. A recognizable situation

Your seven-year-old has spent half an hour building something when their younger sibling pulls it apart. The older child shouts and grabs their arm. You intervene: “You’re older. You should know better.”

Now both children are crying. One says, “You always take their side.” The other wants you to make their sibling play with them.

You need to stop the grabbing, protect the unfinished project, and help a younger child handle being excluded. Meanwhile, dinner is burning.

Sibling rivalry often presents this way: several legitimate needs collide, and the parent’s quickest solution leaves someone feeling overlooked. **The aim is to make living together safer and fairer while helping children gradually manage more of their disagreements.** Friendship cannot be required.

## 2. What might be happening

“Rivalry” can describe several different situations. Understanding which one you are seeing changes the response.

**Competition for access to you.** Does conflict reliably begin when you feed the baby, help another child with homework, or listen to their news? A child may be protesting a real change in your availability. That does not mean every disagreement is a disguised request for attention.

**Competition for possessions, space, or control.** One child wants company; another wants an uninterrupted game. A shared bedroom, one device, or no protected place for unfinished work creates repeated practical disputes. Notice whether the same resource is involved.

**A mismatch in abilities and expectations.** A toddler may dismantle an elaborate construction without understanding its significance. The older child may understand the toddler’s limitations and still feel furious about the loss. Neither child can solve that mismatch through goodwill alone.

**A concern about family standing.** Comparisons, uneven responsibilities, and different privileges can become questions about belonging: “Are they more important?” Ask what “You always favor them” refers to before treating it as an exaggeration.

**A recurring pattern that now runs almost automatically.** One child provokes, another retaliates, and an adult responds to the most visible action. Observe the sequence, including what happens before the shouting. Avoid deciding in advance that one child is always the instigator.

**Intimidation or abuse.** Repeated humiliation, threats, deliberate destruction, or physical harm require a different response from an ordinary disagreement. Ask privately whether either child feels afraid, can refuse contact, or worries about retaliation. A child fighting back does not establish equal power or responsibility. [University of New Hampshire: Sibling Aggression and Abuse](https://www.unh.edu/saara/our-work/sibling-aggression-abuse)

These explanations are working hypotheses. More than one may apply.

## 3. How development changes the picture

### When a new baby arrives

An older child may enjoy the baby and resent the disruption. They may seek more help, protest during feeds, or wish the baby would leave. Those reactions do not establish how the relationship will develop.

Offer concrete expectations: babies cannot wait reliably, understand ownership, or reciprocate play. Let the older child choose whether to help with small caregiving tasks; involvement should not become a requirement to demonstrate love. Never leave a baby alone with a toddler. These are professional recommendations rather than findings from trials of particular scripts. [Nationwide Children’s Hospital: Sibling Rivalry](https://www.nationwidechildrens.org/family-resources-education/health-wellness-and-safety-resources/helping-hands/sibling-rivalry)

### Toddlers and preschoolers

Adults need to organize much of the sharing, separation, and protection. “Work it out” may ask children to coordinate language, waiting, and impulse control they cannot yet use together.

Make the problem smaller: “This is her drawing. Here is paper for you.” Offer two workable options rather than requiring a lengthy discussion about fairness.

### School-age children

Children can increasingly explain competing wishes, generate solutions, and try agreements. However, the younger or less verbally confident sibling may need help expressing a position. An articulate explanation is not automatically the most accurate account.

A small randomized mediation study involved children aged **five to ten**. Its results support trying structured parental help at these ages; they do not establish that toddlers or adolescents need the same procedure. [Smith and Ross, 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17517005/)

### Adolescence

Disputes may concern privacy, borrowing possessions, friends, responsibilities, and different freedoms. An older child’s wish for separate activities can be reasonable. Parents can protect that independence while requiring respectful treatment.

Research describes changing sibling roles and differentiation across childhood and adolescence, but much of it is observational. It does not support one inevitable trajectory toward either closeness or estrangement. [McHale and colleagues’ research review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3956653/)

Across stages, consider communication, disability, temperament, and the actual power relationship—not age order alone.

## 4. What needs balancing

**Fair treatment and different needs.** Children may need different bedtimes, assistance, or supervision. Those differences still deserve scrutiny: is the justification meaningful, and does each child receive consideration?

**Connection and autonomy.** A younger child may desperately want to join in. An older sibling still needs permission to play alone, protect possessions, and have separate friends.

**Learning and protection.** Manageable disagreement offers practice. Repeated intimidation is not an educational opportunity the harmed child must endure.

**Family cooperation and adult responsibility.** Siblings can contribute to family life, but one child should not become the permanent entertainer, mediator, or substitute parent because they are more capable.

Parents also need solutions that work under pressure. A predictable borrowing rule may accomplish more than repeatedly conducting elaborate negotiations while trying to leave for school.

## 5. Possible responses

The following approaches combine research-supported principles with practical examples. The examples are my synthesis; the exact arrangements have not each been independently tested.

### A. Decide whether this needs space, coaching, or protection

**Fits:** the immediate moment of conflict.

For a disagreement without threats or intimidation, pause briefly and observe. Are both children able to speak, refuse, and leave? If they are making progress, they may not need intervention.

If the argument is stuck, offer help:

> “You both want the controller. Would you like help making a plan?”

If someone is being hurt, threatened, or prevented from leaving, intervene directly. Separate children as needed, attend to the harmed child, and arrange supervision before discussing solutions.

Professional guidance supports mediation for ordinary disputes but warns against using it for a persistent pattern of harm involving unequal power. Asking a frightened child to negotiate can make matters worse. [UNH recommendations for parents](https://www.unh.edu/ccrc/sites/default/files/media/2023-04/saara_bulletin2_recommendations_for_parents_managing_sibling_conflict_aggression.pdf)

Even after an ordinary conflict, children can choose separate activities. Immediate reconciliation is not required.

### B. Reduce recurring competition and examine fairness

**Fits:** repeated arguments about attention, possessions, privileges, or responsibilities.

Choose one recurring problem and make the arrangement explicit:

- Distinguish personal possessions from shared materials. Borrowing personal belongings requires permission.

- Give unfinished work a protected place—even a designated tray or shelf.

- Arrange access to shared devices before play begins, including how saved progress will be protected.

- Explain different privileges using relevant reasons and review points, rather than “Because they’re older.”

- Identify when each child can count on your attention within routines you already have.

When a child complains about favoritism, ask for a recent example. They may identify something you missed: their sibling’s appointments repeatedly displace their activities, or they are always expected to yield.

A 2024 meta-analysis involving more than 26,000 participants, approximately ages three to nineteen, linked less-favored treatment and greater differences in parenting with more emotional and behavioral difficulties. These are **associations**: children’s needs and behavior can also influence parenting. The predominantly White samples limit generalization. The findings do not mean every difference in treatment is harmful. [Jensen and Thomsen, 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38439142/)

My practical conclusion is to examine differences rather than defend or eliminate them automatically.

Brief individual attention is a reasonable professional recommendation. Evidence does not establish that a prescribed number of minutes will resolve rivalry. Avoid making an exhausted parent responsible for producing identical quantities of attention.

### C. Mediate a specific disagreement

**Fits:** children who can participate without fear, once they are sufficiently settled.

Help them solve the actual problem:

1. Establish the immediate rule: no insults, threats, or interruptions.

2. Let each child describe what they want. Help the less fluent child express a position.

3. Name the competing needs: “You want to finish. You want a turn before dinner.”

4. Ask for possible arrangements and check whether both can genuinely accept one.

5. Try the agreement and review what happened.

With younger children, supply a couple of realistic options. With older children, ask them to generate proposals. If they cannot agree, make a temporary arrangement and return to it later.

The randomized study of 48 families with five- to ten-year-olds found improvements in reported conflict strategies and observed negotiations after parents received mediation training. This is **causal evidence for the training package**, not proof that every conversation works or that the procedure prevents serious aggression. The small sample leaves uncertainty about wider application. [Smith and Ross](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17517005/)

### D. Make room for enjoyable contact—and use structured help when needed

**Fits:** siblings whose interactions have become dominated by conflict.

Offer brief, optional activities with a reasonable chance of success: making a snack, sharing a familiar joke, or building something with separate materials. Avoid turning every shared activity into a competition or requiring extended togetherness.

Notice a specific cooperative act without ranking children:

> “You checked before moving his pieces. That helped him keep playing.”

The broader intervention evidence is promising but limited. A 2021 meta-analysis found eight randomized evaluations of parenting programs, mainly involving primary-school-age children. Programs improved sibling interactions overall, but estimates were imprecise and varied considerably. Neither behavior-management approaches nor mediation emerged as clearly superior. These were **whole programs**, so their findings do not establish that praise or shared activities alone produce the same benefits. [Leijten and colleagues, 2021](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10117692/)

Results are not uniformly positive. A 2023 trial involving 74 parents of children aged three to ten found improvement in both intervention and waiting-list groups, with little additional benefit on most outcomes from a brief parenting discussion intervention. [Pickering and colleagues, 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10826-023-02539-7)

For entrenched conflict, seek support that assesses sibling interactions and family circumstances, rather than relying on generic advice to spend more time together.

## 6. Examples in practice

**A preschooler resents the new baby.**

During a feed, your child says, “Put him away. I hate him.”

You acknowledge the specific loss: “You wanted me to play, and feeding is taking a long time.” You can allow the statement while preventing rough contact. Offer something workable now and a believable next opportunity for attention: choosing a book beside you, followed by reading together after the feed.

Do not require affection toward the baby. The immediate aim is safe behavior and continued access to you.

**A first attempt at sharing makes things worse.**

Your six- and nine-year-olds fight over craft supplies. You introduce equal turns, but arguments continue.

On closer inspection, the older child is upset because the younger one uses a personally owned set and damages completed work. Equal turns addressed access while missing ownership and protection.

You separate shared supplies from personal ones and create a place for unfinished projects. The younger child remains disappointed about needing permission. You can help with that disappointment without reopening access to someone else’s belongings.

**A teenager wants privacy; a younger sibling wants company.**

The younger child repeatedly enters the teenager’s room and is met with insults.

You address both actions: knocking and waiting for permission becomes a household expectation; insults remain unacceptable. The teenager can choose whether to offer some shared time, but you arrange the younger child’s alternatives. Access to a sibling’s room is not the price of family membership.

## 7. Common mistakes and limitations

**Treating equality as the answer to every dispute.** Identical treatment can ignore age, ownership, health, and prior agreements. Conversely, “Everyone gets what they need” should not end discussion when one child’s needs repeatedly disappear.

**Always asking the older child to yield.** Greater ability may justify expecting a more developed response. It does not remove their claim to protection or possessions.

**Giving equal blame to appear impartial.** Listen to both children, but assess responsibility from what happened. A defensive reaction does not automatically make intimidation mutual.

**Dismissing reports as tattling.** Children need to know they can report injury, threats, or persistent boundary violations. Otherwise, a quieter household may conceal a less safe one.

**Comparing children to motivate cooperation.** “Why can’t you be helpful like your sister?” turns cooperation into a contest over parental approval. Describe the behavior you need directly.

**Forcing closeness.** Required hugs, shared secrets, or constant joint activities can override the boundaries you want children to respect. Help with repair without demanding forgiveness.

Research also leaves substantial gaps. Studies are often small, use parent reports, and follow families for limited periods. Evidence is thinner for adolescents and many family circumstances, including complex blended-family arrangements. Short-term cooperation does not establish lasting affection, independence, or trust.

## 8. What to observe over time

Look beyond the number of arguments:

- Can each child refuse, leave, and request adult help safely?

- Are disputes less threatening and easier to resolve?

- Do agreements protect both children’s interests?

- Does either child repeatedly give in to avoid retaliation?

- Is there room for voluntary enjoyment as well as separate lives?

- Can parents gradually provide less assistance without leaving someone unprotected?

Check privately with each child. Ask concrete questions: “What happens when you say no?” and “Are there times you avoid being alone together?”

Take persistent victimization seriously. A UK cohort following young people from ages eleven to fourteen linked sibling victimization with poorer mental health, including depressive symptoms and lower self-esteem. Persistent victimization showed the strongest associations. The longitudinal design establishes timing better than a one-time survey, but self-report and possible unmeasured influences prevent a simple causal conclusion. [Sharpe and colleagues](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9142430/)

Seek professional support for repeated injuries, serious threats, coercion, destructive targeting, fear at home, or suspected sexual harm. A severe single incident also warrants action. Professional guidance recommends assessment and safety planning when sibling abuse is suspected; forced mediation is inappropriate. Immediate danger or serious injury requires urgent help. [UNH guidance on sibling aggression and abuse](https://www.unh.edu/saara/our-work/sibling-aggression-abuse)

Support can also be useful earlier, when conflict consumes family life or parents cannot maintain a workable plan.
