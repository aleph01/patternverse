**Pattern 21 of 49 · Part 4: Melody and Hooks**

**Use when:** Every line reaches the same high point, so an important word or chorus has no melodic contrast left.

A tune can be drawn as a rising line without anyone knowing its exact notes. That drawing preserves its contour, the broad direction of motion. Move the whole tune into a higher octave and the contour stays the same while its register changes. The distinction gives a songwriter two separate ways to reshape a phrase.

## The move

Reserve a contour change or register peak for the phrase that deserves the listener's attention.

Sketch the verse's rises and falls, then mark the word that should carry the next section. Compare a chorus that continues the verse's D4–A4 region with an alternative that saves C5 for that word. Keep the earlier notes lower so the destination registers. Sing the whole passage, not just the peak. If C5 strains the voice, a longer note, a descent, or a deliberately level phrase may give the word a clearer focal role without a higher ceiling.

Higher is not automatically more expressive; the intended singer's comfortable region matters as much as their extreme notes.

## Compare shapes before selecting every note

Contour is the pattern of rises, falls, and repeated levels in a melody. Register is where the melody sits within the available pitch range. Neither tells us every interval, duration, or harmonic relationship, but each can reveal a useful part of the design.

Porch Light's seed rises from E4 through G4 to A4. A descending answer, G4–E4–D4, creates a different directional gesture. An arching version might rise E4–G4–A4, then return G4–E4. A level version can repeat G4 with a changing rhythm.

These shapes can support different phrase functions. A rising opening may suggest continuation in its context. A descent toward a stable note can support a close. A repeated note can foreground changing words or rhythm. None carries one fixed emotion outside its surrounding music.

![Three original melodic shapes compare a rise, an arch, and a repeated level with a final descent.](/diagrams/songwriting/melodic-contour.svg 'Contour describes direction. The same shape can be sung at different pitch levels and against different harmony.')

A contour sketch is especially useful before notation is comfortable. It should not be mistaken for an exact score. A small rise of one semitone and a leap of an octave can share the same direction while sounding and feeling very different.

## Give the high point a job

An original verse can stay between D4 and A4, with its highest A appearing near the end of a line. A chorus can reach C5 on an important word. The wider register creates a contrast, but the phrase must justify and support the arrival.

If every line reaches its highest note immediately, the song may have little registral contrast left for later. If the one high note falls on an incidental connecting word, it can direct attention away from the intended statement. Pitch height is a form of emphasis alongside duration, accent, and placement.

A high point also need not be the loudest moment. A soft sustained high note may be exposed and fragile; a lower phrase may be delivered forcefully. Performance changes the relationship between pitch and apparent intensity.

## Work with a real voice

Vocal range is not only the highest and lowest possible note. Tessitura describes the region where a melody spends much of its time. A singer may touch C5 comfortably once but struggle if every line hovers there. Repeated phrases, sustained vowels, consonants, and breath all affect the practical demand.

For Porch Light, transposing the whole song may improve the chorus while making the verse too low. Another option is revising one peak or changing the verse's octave placement. The melody should be evaluated in the intended voice rather than against an abstract range chart alone.

Instrumental melodies also have register-specific qualities. A bass instrument's upper register can sound exposed; a keyboard's low chords can become dense. Moving material changes its presentation even when pitch relationships remain intact.

## Keep rhythm in the picture

Two melodies with identical contour can differ strongly because one uses short notes and the other long ones. Porch Light's seed gives its last note two beats. Shortening that A to half a beat and leaving space afterward makes the gesture more abrupt despite the unchanged rise.

Similarly, a small upward movement after a long repeated level may stand out more than a large leap within a constantly angular phrase. Contrast determines how much a change registers.

The main misuse is treating the contour drawing as a recipe that guarantees a hook or emotional arc. It is a diagnostic representation. If a chorus feels flat, the drawing may reveal that its range and shape duplicate the verse, but the needed change could still be lyrical or rhythmic rather than higher pitches.

For contour as a compositional representation, see Dennis DeSantis's [Creating Melodies: Contour](https://makingmusic.ableton.com/creating-melodies-1-contour). ## Wuthering Heights: register makes a destination consequential

Kate Bush's [Wuthering Heights, official video, version 1](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-1pMMIe4hb4), makes register central to the vocal character. The verse moves through a changing contour, while the refrain places its calls in a striking high setting. The effect comes from the journey and delivery as well as the absolute height of any one note. A contour sketch could show rise, peak, and retreat without trying to reproduce every ornament.

For a beginner's song, that relationship is more useful than imitating Bush's range. Porch Light can make its chorus peak consequential by holding most of the verse lower, then approaching one comfortable higher note at a meaningful word. Raising the whole melody indiscriminately would increase effort while weakening the difference between ordinary motion and destination. This listening example concerns expressive register in this performance. It does not prescribe a particular vocal technique, make high notes inherently more emotional, or require a novice singer to match the original pitch level. Choose the destination in relation to the voice that will actually sing it.
