## Purpose and problem

Nora and Ian say they cannot discuss household arrangements without arguing. By the time they sit down, both are tired, several decisions are overdue, and each expects criticism. Asked about a recent conversation that went differently, they remember planning the week together one Sunday afternoon.

That afternoon does not cancel the arguments. It raises a useful question: what was available then that is missing on other occasions?

These fictional people introduce a shift in attention. Alongside understanding a difficulty, systemic therapy can investigate moments, relationships, and conditions that already make living with it easier. The discovery may be a skill, a contribution from someone else, or relief from an unreasonable demand.

## Core idea

**Find What Already Helps means investigating actual sources of support and useful variation, including the conditions that make them possible.**

Think of a better moment as a scene with a surrounding context. Who was there? What did people do? What pressures were absent? Who supplied time, money, access, or reassurance? Which parts mattered to the people involved?

The purpose is to make something usable more clearly understood. “You are resilient” identifies less than “you kept the appointment because your friend provided transport and you asked for a quieter waiting space.” Capability and support can operate together. Neither should disappear from the account.

## How it works

### Look for differences that matter

In solution-focused brief therapy, developed by Steve de Shazer, Insoo Kim Berg, and colleagues in Milwaukee, an **exception** is an occasion when a difficulty did not occur or was less severe. Exploring its details can identify actions and circumstances relevant to what the person wants. The approach also includes conversations about coping when improvement is hard to identify. These practices belong within a collaborative therapeutic process, rather than a requirement to answer one optimistic question. [SFBTA treatment manual, third edition](https://www.sfbta.org/Solution%20Focused%20Therapy%20Treatment%20Manual%20for%20Working%20with%20Individuals%20-3rd%20Edition-%20final.pdf)

A quieter evening matters only in relation to what made it quieter. People may have listened differently; an exhausting journey may have been avoided; someone may have stopped mentioning their needs. These are different findings. Fewer arguments alone cannot distinguish them.

### Include resources beyond the individual

The broader systemic competence framework from UCL includes identifying individual and family strengths, observing helpful patterns over time, and attending to past actions that supported change. This overlaps with solution-focused work without making all systemic traditions versions of the same method. [UCL: Specific systemic techniques](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/brain-sciences/sites/brain_sciences/files/migrated-files/Systemic_specific_systemic_techniques.pdf)

For this field guide, resources include capabilities, supportive relationships, and practical conditions. Knowing how to ask for assistance differs from having someone available to provide it. A supportive friend differs from a funded service, and neither can automatically replace the other. A resource has to be accessible, acceptable, and sufficiently dependable for the purpose being considered.

### Separate a promising clue from a conclusion

Several things usually differ between occasions. A calmer discussion after lunch might reflect timing, preparation, less pain, fewer interruptions, or something nobody has identified. Recalling the episode generates possibilities; it does not establish which difference caused the result.

Participants may also disagree about what helped. One remembers giving space; another remembers being left alone. Exploring that discrepancy is more informative than awarding credit prematurely.

### Leave room for “nothing helped”

The search should be capable of ending without a discovery. The SFBTA manual explicitly advises accepting a person's report that nothing is better or things are worse. Coping can be explored while acknowledging the difficulty, rather than disputing that report. Recognition of endurance must not become a reason to leave someone enduring the same conditions.

## Worked examples and case studies

### A conversation that stayed manageable

*Fictional teaching scenario. All people, dialogue, circumstances, and outcomes are invented.*

Nora wants household planning to stop defaulting to her. Ian wants requests to arrive before they become emergencies. Their usual interpretation is that she nags and he avoids responsibility. Both want necessary decisions made without another exhausting argument.

The therapist asks about the Sunday conversation: “What happened before either of you started talking about the week?” Ian remembers that they had eaten and neither was leaving for work. Nora remembers writing down three decisions beforehand. They looked at the list together instead of introducing new requests while doing other things.

Asked what each contributed, Nora describes keeping to the three items. Ian says he checked his shifts before agreeing to anything. Nora adds that his checking mattered more than his agreeable tone: she could trust the answer. Ian says the limited list helped him attend without wondering what else was coming.

The conversation becomes more precise than “we communicate better when relaxed.” They identify preparation, a bounded task, and checking actual availability as possibilities worth retaining. Ian initially suggests that Nora make a list every Sunday. She objects: this would preserve the very planning burden she wants to share.

In the invented continuation, Ian prepares the next list and Nora adds an item. They settle two decisions, then disagree about cleaning. Nora appreciates the preparation but still feels she notices more of the work. Ian finds starting the list harder than expected.

The exception reveals something they can already do together. It also exposes an unresolved question about who organizes the conditions that make cooperation possible. Their shift schedules and unequal workload still need attention.

### Support that worked, but could not simply be repeated

*Fictional teaching scenario. All people, circumstances, dialogue, and outcomes are invented.*

Alex lives with fluctuating pain and fatigue. Their friend Jo, part of their chosen family, often helps with groceries. Alex wants reliable access to food without feeling permanently indebted. Jo wants to remain involved without becoming the only person responsible. They seek help after missed deliveries and last-minute requests strain the friendship.

Asked about a week that worked, Alex describes a shared shopping trip with Jo and another friend, Mina. Jo drove; Mina carried bags; Alex chose what to buy. Initially, everyone treats the outing as evidence that doing errands together is the answer.

They try again. Mina cannot come, Jo loses paid work to provide the lift, and Alex is too fatigued to enter the shop. Jo feels unappreciated; Alex feels that needing a different arrangement has disappointed everyone.

The therapist returns to the earlier episode: “What did that trip require from each of you, and what was different this time?” Mina had been on leave. Jo had a free afternoon. Alex's pain was lower, and the shop was quiet. Their helpful relationship was real; its availability had been overestimated.

Alex explains that choosing groceries mattered, but being physically present was not essential every time. Jo says an agreed delivery window would be easier than unpredictable lifts. Mina offers occasional help while declining a weekly commitment. Her boundary makes the support more accurately understood.

In this fictional continuation, they distinguish dependable contributions from occasional possibilities and investigate delivery assistance. One delivery goes well; another substitution leaves Alex without suitable food. The arrangement remains incomplete and may require formal support they have not secured.

The useful revision is recognizing the conditions and limits of existing help. Alex's illness has not become a friendship problem to solve, and friends' goodwill has not become an unlimited service. Their contributions affect one another without making responsibility for access equal.

### When searching for exceptions initially fails

*Published clinical vignette, summarized from Chris Iveson (2002), case example 1. The source calls the patient Mrs Brown.*

During a home visit, Mrs Brown initially reported not leaving through her front door for months. Searching for exceptions elicited increasingly severe descriptions of her difficulty.

The therapist noticed the stairs ended beside the feared doorway and asked about coming downstairs. Mrs Brown described sometimes trembling at the top but continuing because she felt she had to. Iveson reports that acknowledging this effort changed the conversation: her voice strengthened, and she recalled bringing in milk and attending an aunt's funeral.

The author interprets these actions as courage and reports renewed activity outside over subsequent weeks, with two further clinic sessions. The brief account supplies neither causal evidence nor detailed long-term follow-up. [Iveson: Solution-focused brief therapy](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/advances-in-psychiatric-treatment/article/solutionfocused-brief-therapy/B8198B18DDEE77F9D39A09FDBCC0CE15)

The revision is from seeking an absent difficulty to noticing effort within distress. This is not a recommendation to push through fear.

## Conditions for useful application

The people concerned need some shared understanding of what would count as helpful, while retaining room to disagree. Quiet compliance, reduced reporting, and actual relief should not be confused. Children, people using communication aids, and participants speaking through interpreters need ways to contribute their own assessment.

Safety also changes the inquiry. Someone avoiding a partner's intimidation may have found protective strategies, but that does not make them responsible for preventing the intimidation. Calm periods do not establish safety or remove the other person's accountability. NICE recommends private, one-to-one enquiry about domestic abuse in a safe setting; discovering support must not require unsafe joint disclosure. [NICE, PH50, recommendation 6](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ph50/chapter/Recommendations#recommendation-6-ensure-trained-staff-ask-people-about-domestic-violence-and-abuse)

## When to use it

This pattern fits when difficulties dominate the available description, when helpful contributions go unnoticed, or when people want to understand why some occasions are more manageable. It can also clarify what must be protected during change.

Two brief fictional contrasts show its range. A child finds a school day easier when a familiar adult welcomes them through a quiet entrance. The useful resource includes an accessible arrangement, not just the child's coping skill. Elsewhere, an adult grieving a partner says nothing feels better but a friend's undemanding messages make evenings less lonely. Support matters without becoming evidence that grief is resolving.

Urgent medical assessment, protection from harm, or securing food and housing may need priority. No exception has to be found before those needs deserve a response.

## Tradeoffs

Detailed attention to helpful moments takes time that could otherwise address grievances or immediate decisions. Moving there too quickly can feel like changing the subject. Staying there too long can leave a clear need unaddressed.

A discovered resource can also expose competing needs. Alex values flexibility; Jo needs notice. Nora values preparation but does not want sole responsibility for producing it. Preserving what helps may require negotiating who supplies it, at what cost, and with what right to stop. Some helpful conditions cannot currently be reproduced.

## Common mistakes and limitations

**Turning an exception into an accusation.** “You managed last week” can imply that present difficulty is a choice. Capacity, pain, demands, and available help vary.

**Praising a trait instead of investigating an action.** “You are strong” may be welcome, but it leaves unclear what the person did, who helped, and whether they want to continue carrying that burden.

**Treating one person's relief as everyone's benefit.** A household may run smoothly because one member has stopped expressing disagreement. Ask whose experience improved and what others paid for it.

**Assuming a resource remains available.** Relationships change, services close, and money runs out. Past access is information, not a present guarantee.

**Overstating the evidence.** This pattern is an editorial synthesis of clinical practices. A vignette can illustrate a conversation; it cannot establish effectiveness. Observing improvement after noticing a resource does not show that the noticing produced it.
