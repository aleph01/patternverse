A conversation ends awkwardly. Hours later, you are still asking, “Why am I always like this?” Each answer produces another accusation. You have thought a great deal, but know little more about what happened or what would help.

Shift from ‘Why’ to ‘How’ means changing the **style of processing** when reflection becomes repetitive and unproductive. Bring attention back to one event, its circumstances, and how it unfolded. A useful replacement might be: **“What happened in this specific situation, what do I know, and what can I do next?”**

The endpoint can be a practical step, a clearer understanding, or a decision that no action is needed and attention can return elsewhere.

## Two ways of thinking about the same difficulty

Rumination-focused cognitive behavioral therapy (RF-CBT), developed by Edward Watkins, distinguishes abstract, evaluative processing from concrete, specific, process-oriented processing. The difference concerns how an experience is approached, rather than simply whether the thought is negative. [Watkins's account of RF-CBT](https://www.guilford.com/excerpts/watkins.pdf)

| Abstract / evaluative / general | Concrete / specific / contextual |
| --- | --- |
| Searches for broad meanings, causes, or implications: “What does this say about me?” | Describes one event: “What did I say, and what happened next?” |
| Generalizes across time: “Why does this always happen?” | Includes circumstances: “What was happening in yesterday's meeting?” |
| Judges the person or outcome against an ideal: “How could I be so useless?” | Examines the process: “Where did I get stuck, and what could help at that point?” |

Abstract thinking is useful for values, theories, and learning across situations. The warning sign is a loop that repeatedly produces global conclusions without new information, a workable response, or a meaningful stopping point.

## The mnemonic is not a word ban

“Why?” can support concrete inquiry: “Why did I miss Tuesday's appointment?” might reveal a calendar error that can be corrected. “How?” can preserve the same abstract judgment: “How can I be such a failure?” changes the opening word without changing the processing style.

Look at the question's job. Does it help describe an event and understand a process, or invite another verdict about your whole self? Useful questions often begin with “what,” “when,” or “how,” but their specificity matters more than their first word.

## Why specificity may help

A global judgment supplies few handles for change. One missed appointment becomes a judgment of character; the circumstances disappear. Concrete processing restores details such as a conflicting commitment, an unclear instruction, or the moment a conversation changed direction. Those details can reveal something adjustable while leaving room for feelings and uncertainty.

An experiment with depressed participants found that concrete self-focus improved social problem solving relative to abstract self-focus. This supports a role for processing style; it does not show that rewriting one question treats depression. [Watkins and Moulds, 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16187867/)

## What the shift looks like

**After a meeting.** Leila keeps asking, “Why am I always like this?” She narrows the question to this morning's meeting: “What happened when I tried to explain the delay, and what could I do differently next time?” She knows she spoke quickly, was interrupted, and left out the revised date. She does not know that everyone judged her incompetent. Her next step is one factual update giving the date. The disappointment can remain without requiring a complete explanation of her personality.

**An unanswered message.** Arun asks, “Why does nobody care about me?” A more specific question is, “What do I know about the message I sent yesterday, and is there anything useful to do now?” He knows it has not been answered; he does not know the reason. Since there is no deadline and nothing to clarify, he chooses “no action needed now” and returns to cooking. Repeatedly checking for reassurance would continue the loop rather than resolve the uncertainty.

## When to use it

Use this pattern when replaying, self-comparison, or broad questions keep returning without producing anything new. Words such as “always,” “never,” and “what is wrong with me?” can be clues, though no phrase proves that thinking is unhelpful.

Choose one manageable everyday episode. Name what you observed, relevant circumstances, and what remains unknown. Then ask one situation-focused question that could yield useful information or a response. You do not need a complete account of the event before stopping.

## Common mistakes and limits

**Changing vocabulary while keeping the verdict.** “How do I stop being fundamentally broken?” remains global and evaluative. Narrow the subject to a particular difficulty.

**Turning description into endless replay.** Concrete processing does not require reconstructing every detail or proving what someone else thought. If nothing useful is emerging, stop the analysis and return attention to a chosen activity or your surroundings.

**Forcing positivity or productivity.** An accurate account can include a real mistake, sadness, unfairness, or a need for rest. Repair, support, and no action are all possible responses. The exercise is not a requirement to solve every feeling.

**Making uncertainty another problem to eliminate.** One useful question is enough for this practice. Rewriting repeatedly until you feel certain or calm can become another checking ritual. Stop or seek qualified support if reflection becomes overwhelming or feeds compulsive analysis.

## Evidence and scope

RF-CBT combines processing-style training with other elements, including functional analysis and changes in behavior. A small randomized trial supported the broader treatment for residual depression, but lacked an attention-matched control and did not isolate this mnemonic. [Watkins and colleagues, 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21778171/)

The article and short practice are original educational adaptations, not diagnosis, a full RF-CBT program, or a clinically validated standalone intervention. Use manageable material and stop when needed. Persistent or disabling rumination may warrant professional support; this practice does not provide trauma processing or treatment for OCD.
