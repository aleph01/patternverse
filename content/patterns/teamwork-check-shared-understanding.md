The meeting ends with a familiar agreement: “Let’s have the guide ready for Friday.”

Everyone nods. One person expects an internal draft for discussion. Another starts preparing a finished document for external distribution. Both believe they are following the plan.

The difference may remain invisible until someone shares the wrong version or misses an expected review. Repeating the original instruction more firmly would not necessarily help. The team needs to discover what people understood it to mean.

## The core move

**Make the interpretation visible, compare it with the intended meaning, and resolve important differences before proceeding.**

A practical foundation is **closed-loop communication**: someone communicates a message, the recipient provides feedback about what they received, and the original speaker confirms or corrects it.

In TeamSTEPPS, a healthcare teamwork program, this includes **check-back**, also called repeat-back. The recipient acknowledges the information and the source verifies its accuracy. Our broader title adapts that principle to everyday requests, handoffs, and decisions. [AHRQ: Check-Back](https://www.ahrq.gov/teamstepps-program/curriculum/communication/tools/checkback.html)

The useful addition is evidence of interpretation. “Got it” acknowledges the exchange. “I’ll share an internal draft by noon, with external distribution awaiting approval” exposes a plan that someone can confirm or correct.

## Why familiar words conceal different plans

Words such as “ready,” “approved,” “urgent,” and “finished” depend on context. Colleagues may bring different assumptions about audience, timing, authority, and quality.

People also coordinate through abbreviated language. That is often efficient: established colleagues do not need to define every routine request. The difficulty arises when they assume more common ground than they have established.

Communication researchers Herbert Clark and Susan Brennan describe **grounding** as the collaborative process of establishing understanding sufficient for the current purpose. Their account emphasizes that the evidence people need varies with the task and communication medium. [Grounding in Communication](https://web.stanford.edu/~clark/1990s/Clark%2C%20H.H.%20_%20Brennan%2C%20S.E.%20_Grounding%20in%20communication_%201991.pdf)

A brief acknowledgment may be enough for a familiar arrangement. An ambiguous release instruction needs stronger evidence.

The check is therefore proportionate to the uncertainty and consequences. Its purpose is to make coordination more dependable without turning every exchange into a formal procedure.

## “Ready for Friday”: a misunderstanding repaired

Consider this **hypothetical** exchange between Maya, who coordinates a partner guide, and Leon, who prepares it.

**Maya:** “Can you get the guide ready for Friday?”

**Leon:** “I’ll send the final guide to all partners on Friday morning.”

Leon’s response exposes assumptions about both completion and distribution. Maya now has something specific to correct.

**Maya:** “My request was too vague. I need an internal draft by noon Friday. Please wait for separate approval before external distribution.”

**Leon:** “Internal draft for our team by noon Friday; external distribution waits for your approval. That timing works for me.”

**Maya:** “Yes. I’ll review it Friday afternoon and confirm the next step.”

The exchange clarifies the deliverable, audience, deadline, and approval boundary. Leon also confirms that he can meet the timing.

There is a small interruption to the conversation. Maya must acknowledge that her wording left room for interpretation, and Leon must make his assumptions visible rather than simply signal cooperation.

They put the corrected plan in the shared work item. If later messages change it, they update that record and check the affected commitments again.

This example is invented. It illustrates how a misunderstanding can be discovered and repaired; it does not establish how much time the practice will save.

## Check meaning, not just wording

Different information calls for different checks.

When precision matters, repeat the exact detail: a date, time zone, document version, quantity, or recipient list. A paraphrase that changes a number defeats the purpose.

When the issue is meaning, ask for the intended action or an example. Someone can repeat “ready for review” while still imagining a different level of completeness.

The related healthcare practice of **teach-back** asks people to explain important information in their own words. AHRQ advises clarifying misunderstandings using a different explanation and checking again, rather than accepting repetition as sufficient evidence. [AHRQ: Teach-Back Method](https://www.ahrq.gov/health-literacy/improve/precautions/tool5.html)

For teamwork, this might mean comparing what a person will actually produce, what happens next, and what would cause them to pause or ask for help. The application here is an editorial adaptation of those communication principles. The Institute for Healthcare Improvement also describes teach-back tools as applicable to important information in clinical and non-clinical settings. [IHI: Always Use Teach-Back](https://www.ihi.org/library/tools/always-use-teach-back)

For a practical skill, a demonstration or worked example can reveal something an accurate verbal summary misses.

## Make the exchange mutual

The tone matters. “Repeat what I just told you” can turn a coordination check into a test of obedience or memory.

A more useful approach shares responsibility for clarity: “Before we separate, let’s compare what each of us expects to happen next.” A team leader can model this by summarizing a colleague’s proposal and inviting correction.

AHRQ’s teach-back guidance explicitly recommends non-shaming questions and places responsibility on the person explaining to make the information understandable. [Checking the explanation](https://www.ahrq.gov/health-literacy/improve/precautions/tool5.html)

A reasonable first step is to use a brief check at one consequential handoff: a changed priority, an unfamiliar task, or a decision involving several people. Focus on the details that could change action.

Individuals can summarize their own understanding without waiting for a new team policy. Consistent expectations around handoffs and decision records require team agreement. Leaders should make it acceptable to reveal uncertainty and correct earlier statements.

In a group, one person’s summary establishes only part of the picture. Invite the relevant contributors to describe their commitments or assumptions, particularly where their responsibilities differ.

## Written communication needs a closed loop too

In another **hypothetical** example, a designer posts, “The layout is frozen.”

A colleague replies: “My reading is that the layout stays fixed, but factual copy corrections can continue. Is that right?”

The designer confirms the distinction and identifies where corrections should be recorded. The team now has a more usable boundary than the original phrase supplied.

Written exchanges preserve a record, but a record can preserve ambiguity. Where explicit confirmation is needed, agree who will respond and how unresolved questions will be followed up. AHRQ’s check-back guidance specifically discusses messages that are overlooked or misunderstood in email and text communication. [Check-back across communication channels](https://www.ahrq.gov/teamstepps-program/curriculum/communication/tools/checkback.html)

Keep the corrected interpretation easy to find. A decision scattered across several messages can become difficult to reconstruct.

## Understanding, agreement, and commitment are different

A colleague can accurately understand a request while believing it is the wrong decision. They can support the decision while lacking capacity to deliver it.

Keep three questions distinct:

- **Understanding:** What does this mean in practice?
- **Agreement:** Is this an acceptable course of action?
- **Commitment:** Can I deliver my contribution under these conditions?

If checking reveals disagreement, the next step is a decision conversation. If it reveals overload, the team needs to renegotiate commitments. Repeating the explanation will not resolve either problem.

Likewise, a shared interpretation does not establish that the underlying information is true, the plan is sound, or the task has been completed. Those require their own evidence.

Use additional support where appropriate: accessible written instructions, a shared sketch, translation, or time to consider the request. Fluent or immediate repetition should not become the standard by which people’s competence is judged.

## Failure modes and signs of progress

One failure is **parroting**: people repeat the words without exposing what they will do.

Another is stopping after a correction. The speaker explains the difference, assumes it is resolved, and never checks the revised interpretation.

A third is ceremonial checking. Everyone confirms the plan publicly while unresolved concerns remain unsafe to raise.

Look for practical changes:

- Are important differences discovered before work proceeds?
- Do summaries identify actual actions and boundaries?
- Are corrections reflected in the shared record?
- Can people say they disagree or cannot commit?
- Are avoidable handoff errors and expectation-driven rework declining?
- Does the checking effort remain proportionate?

The strongest sources here describe communication theory and healthcare practices. Their application to ordinary teamwork supports a useful approach, rather than a guaranteed improvement or a requirement to formalize every conversation.
