**Pattern 4 of 49 · Part 1: Start Making Songs**

**Use when:** A reference impresses you, but imitating its surface gives little direction for your own song.

A listener says a chorus feels huge. Asked what changed, they can only answer “everything.” A songwriter needs a more inspectable account. Did the melody rise? Did the drums enter? Did the bass begin moving faster? Did a voice become doubled? Different answers suggest different things to try in another song.

Listening like a songwriter means turning a response into a question about audible choices. It keeps pleasure and attention together: enjoy the song, then investigate a relationship that made the experience distinctive. The aim is not to reduce a favorite recording to a formula.

## Working choice

Listen for a specific relationship, describe its effect, and compare an original example that uses the principle. A listening interpretation does not establish the artist’s intention, and a transferable relationship does not license copying a melody.

## Separate observation from explanation

“The bass drops out before the vocal returns” is an observation that can be checked against a recording. “The silence makes the return feel exposed” is an interpretation of the effect. “The artist wanted to express abandonment” is a claim about intention and needs other evidence.

Keeping those statements separate improves analysis. Two people may agree about the missing bass and experience its effect differently. A listener may also misidentify a sound: a low keyboard note can be mistaken for bass guitar. When the instrument is uncertain, describe the role or sound rather than inventing a credit.

In Porch Light, an observable feature of the worked arrangement is that the drums remain absent during the four-bar intro. An interpretation is that this leaves attention on the three-note seed. Another listener might find the opening too slow. Both responses can inform a revision without changing the observation itself.

## Choose a listening question

Repeatedly listening for every detail can produce a long inventory with little insight. A narrower question creates a useful comparison: what changes between verse and chorus if the chord vocabulary remains familiar? How does the second verse differ from the first? Which sound signals an approaching return?

For the official [Let It Be video](https://www.thebeatles.com/let-it-be-3), a suitable question concerns how vocal phrasing and accompaniment distinguish recurring passages. This is our proposed listening focus, not a claim that the song demonstrates one exclusive rule. Different recorded versions may contain different details, so an analysis should identify its source.

For Radiohead's official [No Surprises video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=u5CVsCnxyXg), compare the recurring instrumental pattern with the changing sung phrases. The useful task is describing what stays recognizable and where attention moves. No lyric quotation is necessary to discuss that relationship.

## Make a modest listening map

A practical map records a few events in order. For an original example it might read: short instrumental opening; voice enters over sparse chords; bass joins; chorus opens the melodic range; second verse keeps the bass but removes the higher keyboard part. The map need not contain exact notation to reveal a design.

Timing can help, but timestamps belong to a particular recording. A live performance, remaster, or music video may have a different opening. In this edition, our listening studies use clearly identified sources and section landmarks rather than unverified second-by-second annotations.

A map should also preserve uncertainty. If the boundary between a refrain and a chorus is ambiguous, record the reason: the title line repeats, but the accompaniment and phrase do not establish a separate section. A useful analysis explains its hearing rather than pretending names are self-evident.

## Three passes through Everything in Its Right Place

Radiohead's **Everything in Its Right Place**, the opening track on [Kid A](https://radiohead.bandcamp.com/album/kid-a), rewards separating musical jobs. The repeating keyboard pattern offers a reference against which the voice can phrase; processed vocal fragments become another texture. Hearing those together can produce an impression of complexity even when one recurring layer remains comparatively stable.

An observation might be: a keyboard figure returns while the vocal surface changes. An interpretation might be: that stability makes the changing voice easier to follow. The second statement is a useful explanation to test, rather than something the recording proves about every listener or the band's intention. In a new Platform Three version, keep the bass pattern fixed and alter only the upper figure's entrances. If the result stays coherent, there is evidence for that relationship in your own draft. Borrow the arrangement question, rather than the keyboard figure, recording, or vocal treatment itself. This study concerns listening method; it does not infer the order in which Radiohead composed the parts.

## Translate the relationship into original material

Suppose the observation is that a chorus becomes more open without changing the drum pattern. Copying the source melody would not explain the general choice. Instead, Porch Light can keep its drums steady while moving the vocal into a higher register and sustaining the title phrase longer. That creates an original test of the relationship.

The result may not resemble the reference's effect, because harmony, performance, and context differ. That is informative. A technique's contribution is conditional on its setting. The transferable knowledge is a way of making and comparing choices, not a guarantee of a particular emotion.

A second reference might solve the same problem through subtraction: a focal section becomes intimate because several parts disappear. Placing contrasting solutions side by side prevents one favorite artist from becoming the sole model of good songwriting.

## Keep analysis accountable

A confident label can hide weak listening. Calling every unfamiliar chord “jazzy” or every uneven phrase “odd time” closes inquiry too early. A phrase can begin off the beat in ordinary meter. A colorful chord may require only one added note. A useful account identifies what is actually present before reaching for a broad category.

This pattern helps when you admire songs but cannot explain what to learn from them, or when references lead to vague instructions such as “make it more like that.” It is less helpful during a creative moment that needs uninterrupted flow. Listening analysis and drafting can take turns.
