*Change the actor’s physical features while preserving the performance beneath them.*

A character turns into the light. Their nose has an unfamiliar silhouette, their neck carries decades of age, and their cheeks belong to someone we barely recognize. Then they smile—and the face moves with the timing and individuality of a living person.

That is the central challenge of prosthetic makeup. The filmmaker needs a different appearance, sometimes radically different, while retaining an actor who can speak, react, touch other performers, and inhabit the scene.

Paint can suggest shadows, wrinkles, and changes in complexion. But some transformations require actual changes in shape. Prosthetic makeup supplies those changes through sculpted, flexible additions called **appliances**, integrated with the performer’s skin.

The answer lies in treating the transformation as part of the actor’s moving anatomy.

A convincing appliance must do several things together: establish the right form, move appropriately, blend into adjacent skin, and respond convincingly to light. Hair, teeth, costume, and performance complete the impression.

The reusable principle is to **build the character onto the performer, designing the additions around what the performer must still be able to express**.

The Penguin photographs show application in progress and a completed makeup test. A still image demonstrates appearance; the real test also includes speech, expressions, head turns, and the intended camera lighting.

## The face is the starting structure
Prosthetic artists design around an actor’s existing proportions. A common approach begins with a lifecast—a physical reproduction of the performer’s features—on which the proposed changes can be sculpted.

Flexible appliances are attached to the performer’s skin, so a smile or head turn can move the added material with the face. Thin edges help the pieces meet the surrounding skin without a visible ridge.

The sculpture establishes both broad forms and surface detail. It can then be divided into appliances, with moulds used to reproduce the pieces in suitable materials. Bruce Spaulding Fuller’s teaching demonstrations show this progression from character sculpture to a makeup assembled from multiple sections. [References: character sculpting](https://www.stanwinstonschool.com/tutorials/prosthetic-makeup-sculpting-character-creation-tutorial) and [sculpture breakdown and mould-making](https://www.stanwinstonschool.com/tutorials/character-makeup-sculpture-breakdown-mold-making).

There is a basic physical limitation: adding material readily makes something larger. Making the underlying face smaller is harder. Consequently, a successful design works with the actor’s structure and uses neighbouring shapes, colour, hair, and costume to influence how we perceive it.

### Example: *Darkest Hour*—how does Gary Oldman become Winston Churchill without losing his expressions?

For *Darkest Hour* (2017), Kazu Hiro, credited at the time as Kazuhiro Tsuji, designed the transformation, with David Malinowski and Lucy Sibbick applying and maintaining it.

The challenge extended beyond a recognizable likeness. Oldman needed to deliver sustained dialogue and subtle reactions through the makeup.

The production’s featurette records five individual prosthetics applied daily, alongside extensive testing and approximately three hours and twenty minutes of daily hair and makeup work. That preparation made the transformation a repeatable part of filming. In the featurette, compare the application footage with Oldman speaking in the finished makeup: look for how the added shapes move with his expression. [Reference and footage: Focus Features’ makeup featurette](https://www.focusfeatures.com/video/darkesthour-makeup-featurette).

Oldman emphasized that the character’s emotion and intention had to remain visible through the added material. The practical lesson is that likeness and movement must be judged together: an impressive sculpture is only the beginning of a successful acting makeup. [Reference: Oldman discussing the performance with Focus Features](https://www.focusfeatures.com/article/darkest-hour-gary-oldman).

Every attached piece has a boundary. If that boundary catches light differently, forms a ridge, or changes texture abruptly, the audience may see the construction.

Artists therefore design delicate transitions between the appliance and the performer’s skin. The transition includes surface texture as well as thickness and colour. Prosthetic artist Kenneth Calhoun demonstrates how even the direction of sculpted pores contributes to that continuity. [Reference and close-up photographs: Stan Winston School’s explanation of prosthetic surface texture](https://www.stanwinstonschool.com/blog/how-to-create-directional-pores-in-prosthetics).

Colour matching is similarly more demanding than choosing one skin-coloured paint. Real skin contains variation, and its apparent colour changes with lighting. A convincing finish needs to belong to the performer’s surrounding skin under the conditions of the shot.

This is why camera tests matter. They reveal how the combined sculpture, material, paint, and lighting actually photograph.

### Example: *Batman Returns*—how do you create the Penguin while keeping Danny DeVito present?

For *Batman Returns* (1992), Stan Winston’s team developed a stylized transformation around Danny DeVito’s features.

The designers explored alternative nose shapes on reproductions of DeVito’s face and considered them alongside the developing costume. The final makeup included a combined nose, upper-lip, and brow appliance, supported by dental pieces, pale colouring, and dark eyes. Ve Neill applied the makeup during production.

The objective was to create Tim Burton’s character without entirely obscuring DeVito. The face also had to belong to the whole silhouette: head, body, costume, and posture.

The example shows why designing an isolated feature is insufficient. A nose that looks compelling on a workbench might have the wrong scale or personality once the complete character is assembled. [Reference and extensive development photographs: Stan Winston School’s Penguin production history](https://www.stanwinstonschool.com/blog/batman-returns-the-penguin-danny-devito-makeup).

## Materials serve different demands

There is no single ideal prosthetic material for every character. The design may prioritize low weight, softness, translucency, durability, or freedom of movement.

Different areas can also need different solutions. A broad body covering, a delicate facial appliance, and a moving creature feature do different jobs.

### Example: *The Shape of Water*—how can a full-body creature remain an intimate romantic lead?

The Amphibian Man in *The Shape of Water* (2017) had to sustain close attention and emotional interaction with Elisa, played by Sally Hawkins.

Legacy Effects built the physical character around Doug Jones. Shane Mahan’s production account describes a suit made mostly from foam rubber, with silicone elements contributing translucency. Film tests also prompted a substantial repaint when an early colour scheme looked too pale.

The finished creature combined techniques. Mechanical gills could move with Jones’s performance, while planned digital work supplied eye movement, blinking, and additional facial subtleties that could not be accommodated mechanically beneath the makeup.

The practical character gave the scene a physical presence. Other techniques extended its expressive range. The general lesson is to divide the transformation according to what each method can contribute. [Reference and application photographs: Shane Mahan’s account for Stan Winston School](https://www.stanwinstonschool.com/blog/revealing-creative-process-behind-the-shape-of-water-creature-legacy-effects).

## The hidden puzzle: how does the same face return every morning?
A film is shot across many days, often out of story order. The character’s appearance must remain consistent.

Moulds preserve the sculpted design, but each application still requires careful placement, blending, colouring, and hair work. Maintaining the makeup through filming is another skilled task.

For *Darkest Hour*, Tsuji explained that removal damaged the appliances’ fine edges, requiring fresh sets for subsequent filming days. The lasting production asset was therefore the design and its reproducible manufacture, rather than one endlessly reusable face. [Reference and photographs: LAist’s visit to Tsuji’s studio](https://laist.com/shows/the-frame/the-frame-for-february-15-2018).

## Where the illusion becomes difficult
A transformation can look convincing at rest yet suppress the expressions needed for the role. Another may move well but reveal its edges or inconsistent sheen in close-up.

The performer also has to inhabit it for extended periods. Comfort, visibility, speech, movement, application time, and removal all influence what is practical. Wētā Workshop’s prosthetics leads describe both repeated development tests and a close working relationship with the actor as central to the job. [Reference: interview with Kim and Jason Docherty](https://www.backstage.com/magazine/article/prosthetics-special-effects-makeup-career-advice-weta-workshop-70709/).

Prosthetic makeup connects with **costume design**, which completes the character’s proportions; **[animatronics](/pattern/film-animatronics)**, which adds mechanical movement; and **digital enhancement**, which can refine selected features.

Its distinctive contribution is a transformation physically present during the performance: a sculpted character that moves through the actor wearing it.
