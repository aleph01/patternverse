**Pattern 17 of 49 · Part 3: Notes, Keys, and Chords**

**Use when:** A progression works in one key but becomes difficult to compare, explain, or move when only chord letters are recorded.

Two musicians describe the same progression differently. One says C–Am–F–G. Another says I–vi–IV–V in C. The first names the chords directly. The second also preserves their relationships, which makes it possible to move the progression to another key without reinventing its structure.

## The move

Describe roots and chord qualities relative to a tonal center while keeping durations explicit.

Write Porch Light's C–Am–F–G verse as I–vi–IV–V in C, with each chord lasting two bars. Rebuild those same numerals in G as G–Em–C–D and retain their durations. If the melody moves too, transpose it by the same interval. In the editor, switch the key while watching the numerals stay fixed and letter names change. The relative description lets collaborators discuss the progression's design without silently discarding its timing or assuming one set of instrument shapes.

Roman numerals preserve harmonic relationships, not rhythm, voicing, or a guarantee about the next chord.

## Build the major-key family

The previous patterns established five connected ideas: a note has a pitch; an interval measures a distance; a scale arranges a collection; a tonal home orients the passage; and a chord combines notes. Here those ideas meet. **C** can name a single pitch or, on a chord chart, a C-major chord. **C major** can name a scale or a key depending on context. Spell the notes and identify the home whenever the short label is unclear.

A chord family is the collection of chords built from a scale's notes. Chord numbers describe their roots relative to the tonic. They are a practical language for comparison and transposition, not a ranking of which chord deserves to come next.

Stack alternate scale notes above each degree of C major, continuing into the next octave when needed. C–E–G gives C major; D–F–A gives D minor; E–G–B gives E minor. Repeating the process produces the whole triad family.

| Degree | Roman numeral | C-major chord | G-major chord |
| ------ | ------------- | ------------- | ------------- |
| 1      | I             | C             | G             |
| 2      | ii            | Dm            | Am            |
| 3      | iii           | Em            | Bm            |
| 4      | IV            | F             | C             |
| 5      | V             | G             | D             |
| 6      | vi            | Am            | Em            |
| 7      | vii dim       | Bdim          | F-sharp dim   |

Uppercase denotes major, lowercase minor, and “dim” marks the diminished triad in this table. The pattern comes from the scale's interval structure. It remains the same in every major key even though the note names and instrument shapes change.

The editor above uses **vii°** for the same diminished triad. Add chords by Roman numeral, select one to change its duration, then switch the key from C to G. The numerals stay the same while the chord names and sounding pitches change. Your sketch is saved on this device; the Undo control lets you compare revisions.

## Move the relationship

Porch Light's verse uses I–vi–IV–V, each chord lasting two bars. In C, that is C–Am–F–G. In G, it becomes G–Em–C–D. The melody must move by the same interval if the whole song is being transposed.

The number system describes root relationships, not timing. A chart that lists only four numerals leaves the reader unsure whether each lasts a beat, a bar, or two bars. A usable chord sheet supplies meter and duration as well.

Numbers also do not specify voicing. C in root position and C/E both remain I in C major, but their bass notes differ. Inversion notation can add that information. A concise draft may simply retain slash chord symbols beside the broader numerical analysis.

## Recognize two kinds of numbers

For example, in C major, **degree 3** is the single note E. **iii** is the E-minor chord, E–G–B, built on that degree. **Three beats** measures how long something lasts. The editor labels chord duration separately so the three uses cannot silently replace one another.

A single melody note can be called scale degree 3; a chord built on that degree can be called iii in major. These are related descriptions of different objects. The first identifies a note, the second an entire chord.

Plain Arabic numbers appear in several charting systems, sometimes with explicit quality signs. This course uses Roman numerals for harmonic relationships and labeled duration numbers for beats. Where an example uses both, the labels keep them separate.

Similarly, the slash in C/E means C chord with E in the bass. A slash in V/vi, introduced later, means “dominant of vi.” The symbol's meaning comes from the notation system around it, not from its shape alone.

## Treat the family as a starting palette

A chord outside the family is not automatically wrong. An E-major chord in C introduces G-sharp and can point toward Am. An F-minor chord introduces A-flat and can provide a different path back to C. Later patterns explain those choices specifically.

Minor keys have different chord families and several commonly varied scale degrees. Roman-numeral conventions for minor also differ between analytical traditions. Rather than silently mix systems, our advanced examples name the key and spell the actual chord notes whenever an accidental or quality could be ambiguous.

The editor's natural-minor palette uses **i–ii°–♭III–iv–v–♭VI–♭VII**, taking the major scale as its reference for accidentals. In A minor, ♭III is C and ♭VI is F. Some analyses write those as III and VI relative to the minor scale itself. The labels describe the same roots under different conventions. The editor names its convention explicitly and displays the actual chord tones. Its natural-minor v is minor; the major V used in many minor-key songs raises the seventh scale degree and goes beyond that starting palette.

The family is most useful for understanding, transposing, and comparing material. It cannot tell whether a melody is memorable, whether a lyric is clear, or whether the current chord should last longer. Those musical decisions depend on context.

## Let It Be: numbers name relationships, not a whole song

The Beatles' **Let It Be** offers a familiar contrast to Porch Light's progression. Hear the opening verse setting in the band's [official performance](https://www.thebeatles.com/let-it-be-3). Its opening harmonic path can be reduced to C–G–Am–F: in C, **I–V–vi–IV**. This is a reduction of the opening changes, not the complete verse, every bass movement, or the rhythm of the performance.

Porch Light's verse instead uses C–Am–F–G, or **I–vi–IV–V**, with each chord lasting two bars. The two sequences draw on the same four chords but travel through them differently. Calling both a “four-chord song” loses that distinction. If the Let It Be opening relationship is moved to G, the reduced chord names become G–D–Em–C while the numbers remain I–V–vi–IV. That last line is a transposition illustration, not a second Beatles recording. Number notation lets a collaborator preserve harmonic order while a separate chart communicates duration, bass choices, and phrase placement.
