*Disclose something while pretending to omit it: paralipsis.*

A speaker announces that something will be withheld, then supplies enough of it to make the supposed omission impossible. The refusal becomes a conspicuous route to disclosure. The rhetorical name is paralipsis.

## Example 1 · Ricky Gervais

Listing the subjects he is supposedly forbidden to discuss

Gervais presents rules for what he must avoid during the Golden Globes. In listing the supposedly forbidden material, he names Mel Gibson and Jodie Foster’s film The Beaver, introducing the very targets the rules would appear to keep out of the monologue.

— Ricky Gervais

## Example 2 · Joe Lycett

Declining to endorse an insult after spelling it out

Playing an exaggeratedly right-wing commentator, Lycett describes the criticism that years of Conservative rule have left the party choosing from the bottom of its available talent. He attributes that judgement to the haters, then insists he would not say it himself because of his political allegiance.

## Example 3 · Hasan Minhaj

Promising to stop Trump jokes while borrowing Trump’s verbal mannerisms

After mocking the absent president, Minhaj promises that he has finished his Trump jokes and claims he was told to avoid the administration. He reinforces the assurance with the familiar presidential catchphrase “Believe me.” The promise becomes another verbal allusion to its target.

— Hasan Minhaj

## What makes the structure work

The refusal must itself carry the disclosure, insult or imitation. Saying that a topic is forbidden can introduce it; rejecting somebody else’s wording can repeat it; a promise to stop mocking someone can continue the impression. Merely changing one’s mind later is a weaker, neighbouring form. The television satire and dinner-roast settings below are labelled explicitly.
