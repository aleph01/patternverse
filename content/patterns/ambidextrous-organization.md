**Core question:** How can an organization improve today’s operations while developing opportunities that may require a different way of working?

## What it is for

Imagine a hypothetical equipment manufacturer exploring a monitoring service. Its established business sells machines through distributors and manages production against predictable orders. The service team must discover whether customers will share operating data, pay recurring fees, and use its advice.

Judging both activities by this quarter’s equipment margin would discourage the experiments. Letting the service team operate without commercial scrutiny would create a different problem.

Ambidextrous Organization accommodates both kinds of work within one enterprise. It supports dependable execution and improvement while giving uncertain opportunities an appropriate operating environment. It applies across an organization or within a business unit large enough to sustain distinct arrangements and shared oversight.

## The core idea

**Give established operations and exploratory ventures arrangements suited to their different tasks, while keeping leadership responsible for their combined direction, resources, and connections.**

Established work refines capabilities whose customers, processes, and economics are reasonably understood. Exploratory work tests uncertain technologies, markets, or business models. Both can involve innovation; the difference is the uncertainty being managed.

This article focuses on structural ambidexterity: distinct units or arrangements operating concurrently. The broader literature also discusses alternating between modes and enabling people to balance them within their work. These approaches need not require the same organization chart. [O’Reilly and Tushman, _Organizational Ambidexterity: Past, Present and Future_, 2013 manuscript](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/O%27Reilly%20and%20Tushman%20AMP%20Ms%20051413_c66b0c53-5fcd-46d5-aa16-943eab6aa4a1.pdf)

The arrangement differs from an ordinary divisional split because it deliberately accommodates different operating assumptions. It also differs from an isolated innovation lab: shared leadership must secure useful connections and decide how opportunities develop.

## How it works

![Shared senior leadership connects established operations and an exploratory venture. Each uses different operating measures, while selected expertise, customer access, and delivery capabilities are shared through explicit agreements. Leaders decide whether to continue, scale, stop, or transfer the venture.](../assets/organizational-design/ambidextrous-organization.svg)

**Separate where the work requires it.** Give exploratory activity dedicated ownership, people, and enough control over its methods. Separation may involve different processes, incentives, or reporting relationships. A separate office alone changes little if routine operating demands still consume the team’s time.

**Use appropriate measures.** Established operations may emphasize reliability, quality, cost, and incremental improvement. An early venture needs evidence about demand, feasibility, and a workable delivery model. Learning milestones should specify what decision the evidence will inform; counting experiments or prototypes is insufficient.

**Commit resources with review points.** Senior leaders agree funding, staffing, and conditions for further investment. Protection means that routine pressures cannot silently withdraw promised resources. It does not mean indefinite support regardless of evidence.

**Connect selected capabilities.** Name the expertise, customer access, data, manufacturing, or distribution the venture can use. Agree capacity, responsibilities, and constraints with the owners. Share findings in both directions without requiring identical routines everywhere.

**Give integration an accountable owner.** A senior leader or leadership team oversees both activities, resolves resource conflicts, and makes tradeoffs visible. Established-unit managers should understand their contribution rather than discover that support is an unfunded obligation.

**Decide what follows discovery.** Reviews can authorize further testing, expansion, closure, or transfer into another operating unit. Scaling needs an owner, resources, and a delivery arrangement; a successful pilot does not supply these automatically.

In the manufacturer illustration, the venture can test service demand with a dedicated budget. Access to equipment specialists is scheduled explicitly. A positive trial triggers a decision about ongoing support and channel responsibilities, rather than simply adding the service to the existing sales target.

## Why it works

Different tasks can require incompatible routines. Predictable delivery benefits from repeatable processes; discovery requires changing assumptions when evidence contradicts them. Applying one set of expectations indiscriminately can either suppress useful exploration or weaken operational discipline.

Distinct arrangements reduce that interference. Selective connections preserve advantages the venture would lose if it operated entirely independently, such as expertise, trusted relationships, or delivery infrastructure.

Shared leadership makes the tension an explicit organizational responsibility. The mechanism depends on leaders protecting commitments, judging evidence, and resolving conflicts. Creating units with different labels cannot substitute for those decisions. Nor does separation remove uncertainty about whether a particular opportunity will work.

## Examples

### IBM’s emerging businesses in the early 2000s

O’Reilly, Harreld, and Tushman’s 2009 study describes IBM’s Emerging Business Opportunities initiative, launched under senior leadership in July 2000. Selected ventures remained associated with business units while receiving corporate sponsorship, protected funding, and regular reviews. The model distinguished emerging opportunities from growing and mature businesses, with different measures for each. [O’Reilly, Harreld, and Tushman, _Organizational Ambidexterity: IBM and Emerging Business Opportunities_, 2009, pages 86–90](https://digicoll.lib.berkeley.edu/record/284768/files/991056723509706532_C094277598_51_4.pdf)

The strategy group met venture leaders monthly to review progress, refine strategy, and address staffing and organizational alignment. It supported the ventures without taking over their operation from business units.

The important arrangement was therefore differentiated oversight with access to the wider company, rather than complete independence. The study reports that normal processes and funding pressures had obstructed emerging businesses. Its historical account illustrates the response; it does not establish that every protected venture succeeded or that IBM uses the same system today.

### USA Today’s online business, 1995–2001

Tushman and colleagues’ field research followed USA Today’s online business from 1995 through 2001. Initially, it had distinct staffing, routines, and culture but weak senior-team support and few connections to the newspaper. By 2000, funding constraints were contributing to staff losses despite the online operation’s profitability. [Tushman and colleagues, _Organizational Designs and Innovation Streams_, September 2006 working paper, pages 15–16](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/07-087.pdf)

After a leadership change in February 2000, the online organization remained distinct. New editorial meetings connected the platforms, including weekly meetings below the senior-team level. Senior managers’ bonuses reflected both print and online growth.

The change addressed what separation alone had left unresolved: access to shared editorial capabilities and leadership commitment to both businesses. The researchers classified the revised arrangement as ambidextrous. This is a dated case of changing relationships and incentives, rather than a claim that an organizational structure explains the subsequent history of news publishing.

## Useful when

- **Current targets repeatedly crowd out uncertain opportunities.** Promising work loses people or funding to routine demands.
- **The opportunity needs different methods.** Its customers, economics, or delivery model cannot yet support ordinary operating forecasts.
- **Existing capabilities remain valuable.** Exploration can benefit from specific expertise, relationships, or infrastructure.
- **Leadership can support both commitments.** There is enough capacity to fund exploration and resolve conflicts without abandoning current operations.

## Common mistakes and limits

**Applying mature-business targets too early.** Immediate margin requirements can eliminate ventures before their assumptions are tested. Replace premature precision with explicit evidence and investment decisions.

**Making exploration immune to judgment.** Protected resources still need review. Stop or redirect work when evidence weakens its rationale.

**Separating without connecting.** A detached lab may produce demonstrations that nobody can sell, support, or adopt. Give useful dependencies owners and capacity.

**Treating established staff as an obstacle.** They hold knowledge and delivery responsibilities the organization still needs. Shared goals and credible commitments help avoid a privileged venture competing with an unsupported core.

**Forcing every venture into the existing business.** Some opportunities require a different home; others should close. Decide the destination using evidence and strategic fit.

Distinct units add overhead and coordination work. A smaller organization, a modest improvement, or an opportunity with little connection to existing assets may call for a simpler arrangement or an independent venture.
