**Pattern 35 of 49 · Part 6: Bass Movement and Harmonic Color**

**Use when:** A working major-key passage needs a particular color outside its basic chord family.

A familiar progression returns home through an F-minor chord instead of F major. Only A has moved to A-flat inside that chord, yet the path back to C has a different character. The change is useful because it happens at a particular moment, not because a chord outside the basic key is automatically more interesting.

Borrow a Chord means using material from the parallel mode while retaining the larger tonal reference. In our central example, a C-major song borrows from C minor. The tonic stays C; the borrowed collection supplies a different note or chord quality.

## Working choice

Borrow a chord from the parallel palette and check its altered tones against the fixed melody. A borrowed chord need not imply a new key, and its color depends on voicing, duration, and context.

## Keep parallel and relative distinct

C major and A minor share a key signature and are relative keys. C major and C minor share a tonic and are parallel. Borrowing an F-minor chord into C major is a parallel-mode example: F–A-flat–C belongs to C natural minor's collection.

The technique does not require the whole song to become C minor. A single Fm can appear between F and C while the surrounding phrases continue to establish C major. A sustained new center would raise a different question about modulation.

The Roman numeral iv describes minor quality on the fourth degree. A-flat major in C can be labeled flat-VI when the root is measured against the parallel major scale. This course spells the actual chord notes when accidental conventions could otherwise be ambiguous.

## Change one moment in Porch Light

The canonical verse remains C–Am–F–G, each for two bars. A separate ending alternative uses C–Am–F–Fm–C with revised durations. One clear eight-bar plan gives C two bars, Am two, F one, Fm one, and C two. The change includes both a new chord and an altered harmonic schedule.

That plan should not be described as simply replacing F with Fm in the original eight bars; it also removes the original G ending and creates a firmer C return. A controlled comparison can instead keep all durations and substitute Fm for F. The result then moves C–Am–Fm–G. These two experiments answer different compositional questions.

The first creates a major-to-minor subdominant motion before C. The second changes the color of an existing position while preserving the progression's timing. A listener may prefer either, depending on the melody and intended section function.

## Follow the changed note

In F–Fm–C, an upper A can move to A-flat and then G. That semitone descent makes the connection audible. Other voices may hold C while F eventually moves to E. The effect comes through individual lines as well as chord labels.

![A moves to A-flat and then G above a C-centered return through F and F minor.](/diagrams/songwriting/borrowed-chord.svg 'Borrowing changes a specific pitch relationship. Check the fixed melody as well as the chord symbol.')

If Porch Light's singer holds A natural during Fm, the accompaniment's A-flat creates a close clash. The writer can move the melody, delay the chord change until the line has ended, omit a conflicting accompaniment note where appropriate, or deliberately retain the friction. The new chord is not a free decorative layer independent of the song.

## Explore a second color

Another original C-centered passage uses C–B-flat–F–C. B-flat major introduces B-flat and can be understood as flat-VII from the parallel minor palette in this context. The progression need not behave like a classical dominant-to-tonic cadence.

A different passage uses A-flat major before G, bringing A-flat–C–E-flat into the setting. That supplies more changed pitches and requires a fresh check of the melody. “Borrowed” names a source relationship; it does not guarantee a subtle or a dramatic effect.

The ear, genre, and surrounding material determine how ordinary the chord sounds. A sonority that is surprising in one passage may be routine in another. Avoid presenting a borrowed chord as a universal button for sadness, nostalgia, or sophistication.

## No Surprises: hear the borrowed third

Radiohead's **No Surprises** gives the guided player a compact F-major example. The upper figure introduces B♭–D♭–F: a B♭-minor shape, or minor iv in F. D♭ lies outside the F-major scale. The [Faber/Josh Cohen score sample](https://www.fabermusic.com/shop/no-surprises-d38661) provides a published reference for the opening setting and altered pitch; the local guitar reduction has its own register and timing.

Isolating the upper figure and bass makes the changed third easier to follow. A separate, original chord comparison then spaces out F–B♭–F and F–B♭m–F. Only D changes to D♭ in the middle chord. Its root, other chord tones and duration stay fixed. This controlled frame explains the relationship without requiring the reader to untangle every layer of the recording.

The final player comparison returns to the phrase. One version retains the source D♭; the authored alternative raises it to D while preserving every other note and entrance. The difference is local chord colour. It does not establish B♭ minor as a new home. A melody that holds D natural through that borrowed chord would introduce another relationship to check, so the same alteration cannot simply be pasted into any working song.

## Space Oddity: a changed third changes the color

David Bowie's **Space Oddity** ([official video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iYYRH4apXDo)) offers a major-to-minor subdominant contrast in a C-centered setting. The F-to-F-minor movement around the description of Earth changes A to A-flat while retaining F and C. As a teaching reduction, **IV–iv** names that specific contrast; it is not a chart of the whole song. The [licensed Faber piano/voice/guitar edition](https://www.fabermusic.com/shop/space-oddity-d49340) identifies its setting in C and provides a notation reference.

The changed note is what connects this example to Porch Light's original F–Fm–C alternative. The roots alone would conceal the move from A to A-flat. A held A natural in the vocal also cannot be assumed to fit the replacement unchanged: the melody and accompaniment must be considered together. This is why “borrow a chord” should mean choosing a particular relationship, rather than sprinkling chromatic symbols over a working progression. The example illustrates borrowing the minor iv within a major setting; it does not establish that every chromatic chord in Bowie's song comes from the parallel minor.

## Choose the moment rather than the technique

This pattern is useful when a particular return needs a different harmonic inflection, when a line's meaning changes late in a song, or when a repeated section benefits from one marked alteration. It is less useful as a reflex applied to every chord in a progression.

A simpler harmony may leave room for a new lyric or performance detail. A borrowed chord may be most effective at the final return because earlier appearances established a clear reference. Without that reference, the listener may simply hear another chord rather than a changed expectation.

Brian Jarvis's [Modal Mixture](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/modal-mixture/) supplies the technical reference.
