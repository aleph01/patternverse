**Pattern 34 of 49 · Part 6: Bass Movement and Harmonic Color**

**Use when:** The basic progression works, but a sustained moment needs a different color or a little inner movement.

A chart says Cmaj7, another says C7, and a third says Cadd9. Treating them as interchangeable “fancier C chords” hides important differences. Each symbol asks for particular notes, and those notes can affect a melody already in place.

## The move

Add or replace one chord tone under a fixed phrase, keeping the color only when its relationship helps.

Keep the Porch Light verse's roots and durations. Compare its opening C triad with Cadd9, then restore C and compare Cmaj7. Hear the D and B additions separately against the melody. At the final G, compare Gsus4 resolving to G: C moves to B while the bass stays put. Use the particular color or motion the phrase benefits from, and spell it clearly for another player. If it obscures the lead or muddies the low register, change the spacing or return to the triad.

More extensions do not guarantee more expression; Cmaj7, C7, and Cadd9 request different notes.

## A triad refresher before adding notes

The foundation patterns begin with major and minor triads. Two further qualities change the fifth: C diminished is C–E-flat–G-flat, while C augmented is C–E–G-sharp. Compared with C minor, diminished lowers the fifth by a semitone; compared with C major, augmented raises it. The symbols Cdim and Caug name these collections. They change existing chord members rather than adding a seventh.

Minor-key harmony also sometimes alters the starting scale. In A minor, E major or E7 uses G-sharp to lead toward A. Harmonic minor names the natural-minor collection with that raised seventh; classical ascending melodic minor raises both sixth and seventh. These are ways to describe particular relationships, not requirements to keep a minor-key song inside one fixed list. [[songwriting-point-to-a-temporary-home|Point to a Temporary Home]] develops directed chromatic approaches.

## Distinguish the sevenths

Chord colors extend or alter a basic triad. The symbol is a performance instruction as well as an analytical label. It should communicate the intended pitch collection clearly, while allowing performers appropriate freedom over spacing and octave placement.

Cmaj7 contains C–E–G–B: a major triad with a major seventh above its root. C7 contains C–E–G–B-flat: the same triad with a minor seventh. Cm7 contains C–E-flat–G–B-flat. The “7” in an ordinary dominant-seventh symbol does not mean use the seventh note of whichever key happens to surround it.

C7 can function as the dominant of F, but the chord quality and the function are separate descriptions. In another style or context, the same sonority may not be moving toward F. A useful chart names the notes without assuming every occurrence serves the same harmonic grammar.

Likewise, Am7 contains A–C–E–G. If the singer holds G above an Am accompaniment, that combined moment has the same added pitch. Whether the accompaniment itself should play G depends on the arrangement.

## Read added notes and suspensions

Cadd9 requests C–E–G with D added. C9 conventionally includes the dominant seventh B-flat as well as D; performers may omit some chord members in a particular voicing. Cmaj9 instead includes B natural. The difference matters when a fixed melody uses B.

Csus4 normally replaces the third E with F, giving C–F–G. Csus2 replaces the third with D. An added fourth alongside the third is a different collection from a suspended chord that omits the third. Real charts sometimes use inconsistent shorthand, so spelling a critical voicing can prevent misunderstanding.

A chord labeled “sus” does not have to resolve immediately. The symbol names a sound. A prepared melodic suspension is a specific voice-leading event described in [[songwriting-melody-and-harmony]].

## Compare colors under a fixed phrase

Porch Light's seed E4–G4–A4 fits above Cmaj7 because its E and G remain triad members and A adds another relationship. Above C7, B-flat in the backing creates a different harmonic setting even if the seed remains unchanged. Calling both versions simply more emotional would miss what actually changed.

An original quieter accompaniment can use Cadd9–Am7–Fmaj7–Gsus4, preserving the base progression's roots and basic qualities except for the suspended dominant. The final Gsus4 can become G by moving C down to B. That inner resolution provides movement while the bass stays on G.

This is a labeled alternative, not a correction to the simpler verse. The triads may serve the lyric more clearly in another arrangement.

## Clair de lune: a color belongs to a particular spacing

Debussy's **Clair de lune**, from **Suite bergamasque**, gives chord color a setting beyond pop shorthand. The [score and MIDI edition](https://www.mutopiaproject.org/cgibin/piece-info.cgi?id=1778) lets a reader compare the quiet, spacious opening with the more flowing accompaniment at the passage marked **Un poco mosso**, bar 27. Sustained upper sounds and changing lower arpeggios distribute a harmony through time instead of presenting every note as one block. Register and duration are part of the result.

The useful transfer is a careful addition, not a demand to imitate Debussy's harmonic vocabulary. Porch Light's Cadd9 adds D to C–E–G; Cmaj7 adds B instead. Spacing either added note clearly under a fixed vocal phrase makes its particular relationship easier to judge. In Clair de lune, a note's role depends on its line and its surroundings; every note beyond a triad is not automatically a jazz extension. The linked score is the reference for the two textures, while the chord substitutions and fixed-phrase comparison remain original teaching material.

## Let spacing clarify the sound

A D close beside E can create a different texture from a D placed an octave higher. Added notes in a crowded low register can obscure a chord's identity. A lighter upper voicing can retain the color while leaving room for bass and voice.

The extra note should earn its place. Adding sevenths and ninths to every chord may reduce the contrast of a single important colored moment. It can also make the song harder to perform without adding a useful relationship.

This vocabulary helps when interpreting charts, reharmonizing a melody, or specifying an important accompaniment sound.
