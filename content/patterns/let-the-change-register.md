Nora has found a sentence that fits what she wants to say when a colleague changes their shared plans: “Wait. I haven't decided yet.” Speaking it in an imagined exchange brings a little relief. For a moment, she does not have to choose between immediate agreement and a fully prepared refusal.

It would be easy to move straight to rehearsing the conversation. Instead, there is something else to notice. Nora is surprised that she can have time before knowing what she wants. The significance of that possibility is just beginning to reach her.

The sentence was one development. What it is like to have found and spoken it is another. Let the Change Register gives this second movement a place, without requiring Nora to call it a breakthrough or turn it into a promise about how she will behave tomorrow.

## The difficulty this pattern addresses

A meaningful difference can pass through an encounter without becoming part of what the person recognizes has happened. Someone speaks clearly for the first time in a difficult exchange, feels less alone with grief, or discovers a more compassionate response to a mistake. Attention quickly returns to the next problem.

This can happen for ordinary reasons. Time is limited, practical decisions matter, and both people are used to looking for what still needs work. A therapist may also assume that the benefit is self-evident. If the person has already felt something different, why spend more time on it?

Yet experiencing a difference and recognizing its impact are not identical. Someone may feel more at ease without noticing what made the encounter different. They may regard a helpful moment as something the therapist produced, with little awareness of their own contribution. They may remember only the difficult material because that is where most of the attention went.

A person can also move away from a useful development almost immediately. Relief becomes a demand to remain relieved. A clearer wish is dismissed as impractical before it can be understood. A moment of pride is followed by embarrassment about feeling proud. What has just happened is recruited into an older task of evaluating or controlling oneself.

The response to improvement can itself be difficult. Receiving company may bring grief about earlier loneliness. Recognizing a capacity may expose the cost of having doubted it. Feeling pleased can make someone fear that the therapist will now expect faster progress. The new development and the reaction to it both belong to the encounter.

A different problem arises when the therapist sees change that the person does not recognize. The therapist celebrates a firm voice, while the person feels that they were acting a role. The therapist interprets quieter breathing as relief, while the person describes fatigue. Inviting reflection is useful only if it can revise the therapist's account.

Let the Change Register addresses this easily missed period after, or within, a meaningful movement. It asks what has actually become different, how it is experienced, and what it means to the person. The answer can be modest, mixed, uncertain, or unwelcome.

## The core move

**Pause with a difference the person recognizes, and explore the experience of having experienced it.**

The shift can be small. Someone notices that they asked for a pause, that a sad memory contained affection as well as pain, or that they could disagree without losing the thread of the conversation. There is something specific to return to, rather than a general invitation to feel positive about therapy.

Attention then includes a second layer. What is it like to know that the pause could be requested? What happens when the person recognizes that they stayed connected while disagreeing? What does the difference bring into view about their own capacity, the relationship, or the conditions that helped?

AEDP gives this movement a particularly explicit form through metaprocessing, or metatherapeutic processing. The focus includes the experience of change itself and the affective, bodily, relational, and reflective responses that accompany it. The broader editorial pattern here also includes related forms of receiving, savoring, and integration while retaining their differences. [Fosha's introduction to AEDP 2.0](https://www.apa.org/pubs/books/undoing-aloneness-transformation-suffering-flourishing-sample-chapter.pdf).

Registering can involve several dimensions, and not every encounter needs all of them.

| Dimension | What becomes more recognizable | An important distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Felt quality | The particular experience of ease, strength, clarity, connection, or another difference. | Naming a quality does not require intensifying it. |
| Agency | What the person noticed, chose, expressed, or allowed. | Recognizing participation does not make them responsible for producing every future change. |
| Relationship and conditions | What the other person or situation contributed. | A supported capacity is still a capacity, while the support remains relevant. |
| Meaning | What having the experience suggests about the person or their situation. | A new meaning can remain tentative and local. |
| Response to change | Relief, pride, grief, doubt, pressure, or other reactions to the difference. | Mixed feelings do not automatically cancel the helpful part. |

The core move does not require making the change permanent in the moment. Trying to hold a pleasant state still can become another form of effort. The point is to give a development room to become recognizable before deciding what follows.

Nor is this simply an evaluation of whether the session was successful. Practical feedback and outcome assessment have their own uses. This pattern stays closer to a particular event and its present impact. Someone can find the encounter worthwhile while remaining unsure whether their broader difficulty has changed.

## How the experience unfolds

These movements describe possible ways of attending, rather than a required sequence. Sometimes a short acknowledgment or a quiet pause is sufficient.

### Identify the difference with the person

An observation can be offered tentatively: something seemed different when the person said a particular sentence, changed position, or received a response. The person can recognize it, qualify it, or say that the observation does not fit.

Specificity helps. “You sounded more confident” may miss what mattered. The person might say that confidence was not present, but they had more room to be uncertain. The useful development concerns the right to take time, not the appearance of certainty.

If no difference is recognized, there need not be a search for one. The person's account may instead call for clarification of the task, a change in focus, or acknowledgment that the work was not helpful. A reflective invitation should not make improvement a condition of being understood.

### Give the experience enough room to be encountered again

A development can be revisited through the words, image, movement, relational exchange, or quiet recognition in which it was experienced. This does not require a full replay of the preceding work. The person may need only to return to a phrase or notice what remains of its effect.

The available quality may have changed. Relief might now be mixed with tiredness. A clear position may feel less vivid. That is part of the current account. Reproducing the original intensity is not the measure of whether the moment was real.

The form of attention also matters. Some people welcome bodily detail; others find it distracting or intrusive. Ordinary language can carry a precise experiential account. A person need not locate a bodily sensation to establish that a change matters.

### Explore what it is like to have had the experience

Attention can move from the event to its impact. Someone who has voiced a need notices a little pride. Someone who has received company feels surprised that the other person did not become impatient. Someone who has stopped attacking themselves recognizes how exhausting that attack has been.

The therapist follows the response that appears rather than supplying the meaning. Pride, gratitude, and relief are possible; so are sadness, irritation, and uncertainty. A question about the experience should have room for an answer that changes the direction of the work.

There can be movement between feeling and reflection. A person senses a difference, finds words for it, and returns to the experience to see whether those words fit. An explanation that remains connected to the event can deepen understanding. More elaborate analysis is not automatically more integration.

### Include what makes receiving the difference difficult

A useful experience can immediately become a new expectation. “I can do this” becomes “Now I must do it every time.” Being helped becomes evidence that the person should soon be independent of help. Feeling better brings apprehension that the therapist will lose interest in what remains painful.

These responses deserve attention in their own right. The work need not defeat them in order to preserve the original development. It may be possible to recognize a capacity while also acknowledging the conditions it needs, or to value a moment without making a forecast from it.

A refusal to dwell can also be straightforward. Someone may be tired, satisfied, private, or ready to finish. Not wanting further reflection does not necessarily indicate a barrier to receiving change.

### Make participation and context visible together

A new capacity may have depended on support. The person found words because the therapist slowed down, a group made room, or a physical arrangement became more comfortable. Recognizing those conditions does not make the person's participation less real.

Equally, the therapist should not become the sole author of the change. The person may have corrected an interpretation, chosen a limit, or remained interested in an uncertain experience. The account can include both what they did and what made it possible.

This matters for later use. Someone may discover that a particular kind of support, rather than greater willpower, makes a response accessible. That knowledge can inform future choices without promising that the same conditions will always be available.

### Leave room for the next movement without turning it into a test

A difference may suggest a phrase to remember, a practical choice, a conversation, or rest. It may also need no immediate continuation. The person can retain a sense of what mattered without an assignment to reproduce it.

The current state can end. Feeling sad again later does not by itself erase an earlier experience of company. A familiar habit can return while a new distinction remains useful. Whether the broader pattern changes needs to be learned over time.

The session's ending is part of this process. Enough time should remain to acknowledge what is open and help the person return to ordinary life. A last-minute demand for a positive conclusion can undo the room for honest reflection the encounter has created.

### How different approaches implement the move

AEDP makes the experience of change an explicit therapeutic focus. The other approaches below give related attention to receiving, integration, or meaning while pursuing different immediate aims. Their inclusion is an editorial comparison. All encounters are fictional illustrations, not reports from the cited sources or complete demonstrations of the methods.

#### AEDP: process the experience of change itself

AEDP distinguishes work with a new experience from further processing of what it is like to have experienced change. Its intervention framework includes both metatherapeutic and relational metaprocessing, attending to bodily, emotional, reflective, and relational aspects. The framework also describes responses such as pride, gratitude, and mourning. These are clinical concepts and possible developments, rather than emotions a person must produce. [AEDP affective change processes](https://aedpinstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/AEDP-91-for-INTERVENTIONS-with-DESCRIPTIONS.pdf).

Imagine someone who tells the therapist that they felt overlooked. The therapist acknowledges what was missed, and the exchange becomes easier. The person is ready to return to the original topic. Before doing so, they are interested in a short pause to notice the difference.

At first they describe relief. When they consider what it was like to have the complaint taken seriously, they notice surprise: they had expected to end up comforting the therapist. The significance of the exchange becomes clearer. Their disappointment had remained part of the conversation rather than being displaced by the helper's feelings.

There is warmth, but also sadness about how unfamiliar this is. The therapist can stay with both. The person does not have to end in gratitude or conclude that the relationship will never disappoint them. What becomes more available is the experience of their correction having counted.

Imming's clinical account of relational repair includes metatherapeutic processing of the encounter. It helps illustrate why this is more than asking for a satisfaction rating. The reflection remains connected to what happened between the people and what it is like to recognize its impact. [AEDP account of repair](https://aedpinstitute.org/journal/the-work-of-aedp/).

#### Focusing: receive a small step before immediately overruling it

Gendlin describes receiving as a movement in which a newly emerging step is given room before familiar objections crowd it out. The person can return to the words or felt quality of the shift without having to decide everything at once. This concerns the development of a felt process, rather than a demand to preserve a positive state. [The Client's Client](https://focusing.org/gendlin/docs/gol_2149.html).

In a fictional encounter, someone has been unable to choose between two projects. A phrase emerges that fits: “I want a smaller beginning.” There is a little easing. Almost immediately, they argue that small beginnings never lead anywhere.

The objection may be important. But before accepting it as the final word, the person wants to notice what smaller beginning actually means. It is not a decision to abandon the larger ambition. It is relief at being able to begin without promising the whole outcome.

Giving that step some room changes the conversation. The person can recognize both the wish for a manageable start and the fear of endless preparation. Neither has to be settled immediately. The phrase remains a possible reference rather than disappearing under the first objection.

This synthesis does not turn receiving into a rule that all doubt must step aside. The person can find an objection accurate or decline further attention. The family resemblance lies in allowing a fresh development to become known before it is automatically evaluated through an older demand.

#### Hakomi: take time to study and integrate a missing experience

Kurtz describes giving time to a missing experience once it becomes available: taking it in, studying its aspects, and finding access to it again. Integration also includes its use in later situations. This is broader than a momentary pleasant feeling and does not require an immediate declaration that a core model has changed everywhere. [Kurtz's account of integration](https://hakomi.com/a-little-history/).

Imagine someone who usually feels that attention must be earned through usefulness. In an agreed encounter, they have a brief experience of another person remaining interested while they are quiet. They first describe it as relaxing, then begin explaining how they will become less demanding in future.

The therapist wonders whether the new experience is being turned into another obligation. The person recognizes the shift: a moment without performance has already become a task of performing independence. They would like to return to the simpler quality of having company without offering anything impressive.

After a pause, they notice that quiet is not empty in the same way. They are still uncertain about receiving attention elsewhere. That uncertainty can remain while this particular experience becomes more recognizable.

The useful focus is the texture and significance of the available experience, including what makes taking it in difficult. Staying longer is not automatically better. The person may want to speak, notice grief, or end the exploration. Integration follows their response rather than a prescribed duration of nourishment.

#### Sensorimotor Psychotherapy: savor the particular qualities of an available resource

Sensorimotor work can attend to the bodily qualities of support, pleasure, strength, or another resource. A group-treatment study describes participants savoring the significance of new experiences within a broader intervention. Ogden's teaching material also attends to bodily well-being and to difficulties with positive experience. These sources situate savoring in a clinical context rather than establish it as an independently tested treatment. [Group-treatment study](https://sensorimotorpsychotherapy.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/10/A-Pilot-RCT-of-A-Body-Oriented-Group-Therapy-For-Complex-Trauma-Survivors-An-Adaptation-of-Sensorimotor-Psychotherapy.pdf), [Ogden's teaching material](https://sensorimotorpsychotherapy.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/Trauma-Dissociation-Body-Handout.pdf).

In a fictional example, someone notices a sense of steadiness after a small adjustment in how they sit. They habitually register such a moment only as being less uncomfortable and move on. Given a little time, they recognize what is different: their back is supported while they remain able to move.

The therapist initially calls the experience calm. The person says that steadiness is closer. Calm sounds passive; this quality includes readiness. The correction helps identify why it is useful.

The person also notices that prolonged attention becomes uncomfortable. They prefer to let the sensation move into the background while continuing the conversation. The resource has become more particular without having to occupy all attention or increase in intensity.

Here, registering concerns an available supportive quality. Its conditions can become easier to recognize later. That does not demonstrate that a state has been installed in the nervous system or made permanently accessible by a particular period of attention.

#### Raja Selvam's emotional embodiment: recognize integration without assuming the whole process is resolved

Selvam's account of emotional embodiment includes a turn toward integration, attending to changes such as greater physical ease while the situation and emotion recede into the background. The purpose and form differ from AEDP metaprocessing, though both make room for what follows emotional work. [Selvam's protocol account](https://integralsomaticpsychology.com/protocol-embodying-emotion).

Imagine someone working with a difficult feeling who notices that breathing has become easier. They and the therapist attend to that change rather than immediately returning to the evocative situation. The person reports a little more room to think and remain connected to the conversation.

That report matters more than the appearance of a larger breath. On another occasion, easier breathing might have followed complete disengagement from the topic. The person can help distinguish the experiences without being required to name one a successful integration.

The current development may be physiological ease, more capacity to remain with emotion, or simply enough respite to stop. Its meaning can be described at that level. There is no need to infer a complete emotional resolution or adopt every physiological or energetic explanation of the source model.

Selvam's workshop material explicitly allows variation in the amount and intensity of embodiment and in the use of integration. The relevant shared move is giving attention to a difference rather than treating it as an unexamined absence of distress. [Workshop presentation](https://congress.eabp.org/2023/site/wp-content/uploads/Embodying-Emotions-EABP-Workshop-September-8-2023.pdf).

#### Individual Emotion-Focused Therapy: consolidate what the work means

EFT therapist-competency guidance includes processing completed work, consolidating change, creating a meaning perspective, and relating it to a developing formulation. This gives the present pattern a reflective and narrative expression. The emphasis is on what the experiential work makes available, rather than an abstract explanation added from outside. [EFT therapist competencies](https://emotionfocusedtherapy.eu/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/FC-EFT-Competence-v1.03-2.pdf).

In a fictional encounter, someone has found an accessible caring response toward their distress after a mistake. They can recognize that repair is needed without continuing a global attack on themselves. When the active task ends, they initially summarize the outcome as needing to be kinder.

The therapist checks whether that captures the actual difference. The person revises it: the important part was discovering that care could help them take responsibility rather than excuse what happened. The new meaning is more specific than a moral instruction to practice kindness.

They notice that they feel less alone with the repair, and also nervous about the conversation ahead. Both can enter the account. The meaning is connected to the felt development, while practical uncertainty remains.

The same guidance includes realistic expectations about gains and setbacks. Consolidation need not turn a useful event into a claim that the person will respond differently every time. Nor is every reflective summary AEDP metaprocessing. The methods overlap in giving experience a continuation while organizing that continuation differently.

#### Comparing the implementations

| Approach | What receives further attention | Distinction to preserve |
| --- | --- | --- |
| AEDP | The experience of having undergone change, including relational and emotional impact. | Metaprocessing is more specific than a general check on session satisfaction. |
| Focusing | A newly emerging step and the room to receive it. | A step can be welcomed without deciding its full implications. |
| Hakomi | The qualities and implications of a missing experience that has become available. | Present access and integration across life are different observations. |
| Sensorimotor Psychotherapy | The particular bodily qualities of an actual resource or new experience. | Savoring does not require prolonged focus or escalating positivity. |
| Emotional embodiment | Changes attended to during integration after work with emotion. | Greater ease does not by itself establish complete emotional resolution. |
| Individual Emotion-Focused Therapy | The meaning of experiential work within an evolving account of self and situation. | A reflective formulation should stay answerable to the experience. |

All give some attention to a development after it appears. They differ in whether the emphasis is receiving, somatic experience, relational impact, or meaning. Let the Change Register brings these into conversation without turning the word integration into a single biological or therapeutic process.

## A worked example

The following fictional encounter continues Nora's situation from the opening. It stands on its own as an illustration of the broader pattern, rather than a demonstration of one approach's protocol.

Nora has described agreeing too quickly when a colleague announces changes to a shared project. She smiles, says that the change is fine, and later feels resentful about the extra work. In an imagined exchange during therapy, she has been exploring a sentence that allows her to pause.

Her first attempts sound like polished requests for more consultation. The sentence that fits more closely is simpler: “Wait. I haven't decided yet.” Saying it brings a little relief. She can have time before knowing whether she wants to agree or refuse.

The therapist is ready to help her rehearse what might follow. Nora is interested in that practical question, but she also notices something unfamiliar in the pause after the sentence. She says that the relief is easy to lose as soon as she imagines managing the colleague's response.

They agree to give this moment a little attention before moving on. The therapist asks what is different now. Nora first says that she feels more confident. After considering the word, she changes it. Confidence suggests knowing the answer. What feels different is permission not to know it yet.

That correction makes the development more precise. She notices a little less urgency to arrange her face and choose the least inconvenient answer. There is still anxiety about the colleague. The anxiety and the newly available time are both present.

The therapist describes the moment as a significant breakthrough. Nora becomes more formal and says that she hopes she can keep it up. The therapist notices the shift and asks how the description landed. Nora says it made the pause into something she will now have to demonstrate.

They slow down. The therapist acknowledges that their enthusiasm has added a standard she did not ask for. The earlier experience does not need to become a requirement. Nora can decide whether anything useful remains of it after that exchange.

She does still recognize the sentence. Repeating it quietly to herself brings less relief than before, but its meaning remains clear: she does not owe an immediate decision. She does not want to make it more powerful. She wants a moment in which the statement is allowed to be ordinary.

The therapist follows that preference. In the quiet, Nora notices a little pleasure in having found her own words. She had not borrowed a more assertive personality. She had corrected several formulations until the expression fitted what she actually needed.

When the therapist asks what it is like to recognize that participation, she becomes tearful. At first she thinks the tears must mean the useful feeling has gone. On closer attention, she describes sadness about how often she has assumed that uncertainty disqualifies her from having a say.

They leave room for that sadness. There is no need to return immediately to confidence or ask her to enjoy the progress. The meaning of the new possibility includes recognition of its earlier absence. She can be pleased about finding words and sad about needing so much preparation to claim an ordinary pause.

Nora also says that it helped to be able to correct the therapist. The sentence had become fitting through disagreement about wording, and the therapist's praise had needed correction too. The account of the session includes both her participation and the other person's willingness to change direction.

She does not want to conclude that she can now handle every exchange with the colleague. The therapist agrees that the session has not established that. What has become available is a particular expression, an experience of having time, and a clearer sense of the conditions under which that difference is recognizable.

Near the end, they return briefly to the practical situation. Nora would like to consider using a pause, but she does not want to rehearse further today. She is tired and feels that more practice would return her to performing the response correctly.

She chooses a short phrase to retain: “Time before an answer.” It describes what mattered more accurately than “be confident.” She may write it down later, but no particular way of remembering it is required. The phrase is a reference to the experience rather than an instruction to reproduce a mood.

They finish by acknowledging what remains unresolved. The colleague may still dislike waiting, and the project's arrangements may still need discussion. Nora's anxiety is not evidence that the earlier difference was false. It is part of the situation into which any new response would have to go.

The immediate outcome is modest but distinct. Nora noticed a change, corrected how it was described, explored the pride and sadness that followed, and identified her own contribution alongside the support she received. The useful moment became more recognizable without having to be inflated into proof of lasting transformation.

## Why this movement may help

One possible contribution is greater specificity about what was useful. A vague statement such as feeling better can leave the person with little sense of what changed. Nora's recognition of time before an answer identifies a capacity and a condition. That is more usable than a general demand to be confident.

A second contribution is a richer account of agency. Someone can notice that they corrected a proposal, tolerated uncertainty, expressed a need, or chose an amount that fitted. The point is not to award credit but to make participation visible. A person may then know more about how they engaged, rather than remembering the shift as something done to them.

A third possibility is the recognition of relational conditions. Having company, being allowed to revise, or receiving a direct answer can become part of what the person knows was helpful. This can inform future requests and expectations while preserving the distinction between this encounter and other relationships.

A fourth contribution is room for the reaction to change. Relief can expose grief; pride can bring embarrassment; a new option can bring uncertainty about what follows. If those responses are ignored, the person may experience the useful moment as less welcome or more demanding than the therapist assumes. Attending to them can keep the account accurate and the work responsive.

A fifth possibility is that a small development becomes easier to recognize again. A phrase, bodily quality, or relational detail can provide a reference without becoming a technique for controlling feeling. Remembering what support was like differs from being able to summon it at will, but the reference may still help the person discriminate what fits.

These are clinical rationales, not proof of a single mechanism. Iwakabe and Conceição's initial task-analytic research on metatherapeutic processing examined selected exemplary therapy events and developed a process model involving affective and reflective tracks. It offers a structured account of a clinical task; it does not establish that pausing with a change independently causes lasting improvement for every person. [Author manuscript of the task-analytic study](https://www.aedpresearch.com/uploads/3/7/6/5/37653853/5._metatherapeutic_processing2014may.pdf).

Similarly, an outcome study of a broader treatment that includes savoring or integration cannot by itself isolate those components' effects. The shared pattern remains an educational synthesis across methods with different evidence bases.

There is also a simpler value: allowing an encounter to end with a more adequate account of what happened. The person can say that something helped a little, that a celebration did not fit, or that the useful change was different from what the therapist thought. Accurate recognition matters even when no broader claim can yet be made.

## Conditions, pitfalls, and limits

### Establish that there is a difference to explore

The title should not become an assumption that every session contains improvement. A person's apparent relaxation or more fluent speech may have another meaning. Their own account takes priority over a therapeutic narrative of progress.

A therapist can invite attention to a possible shift while remaining willing to hear that nothing useful changed. Sometimes the fitting response is to acknowledge a miss, revisit the goal, or stop. Making room for that answer is part of honest reflection.

### Avoid turning recognition into compulsory celebration

Enthusiasm can be welcome, awkward, or pressuring. Some people like a direct acknowledgment; others prefer a quiet, ordinary response. Cultural meanings of pride, modesty, gratitude, and visibility also differ.

The person should not need to mirror the therapist's excitement or provide a positive conclusion. Gratitude is not the price of care. The absence of a warm emotional response does not establish that a useful development failed to register.

### Let uncertainty and difficult feelings belong

The response to a new experience can include fear, grief, anger, disbelief, or an ordinary lack of interest in dwelling. These are not automatically obstacles to integration. They may clarify the experience's meaning or show that the proposed focus is not fitting.

A change may also remain mixed. Someone can feel more able to choose and less certain about the consequences of choosing. The task should not simplify that into either success or failure. What matters is the person's actual relationship to the development.

### Distinguish attention from holding a state in place

Dwelling can become effortful when the person tries to preserve relief or reproduce the strongest moment. The instruction to take it in can sound like a warning that the benefit will disappear unless they do it correctly.

No fixed amount of attention guarantees retention. A helpful quality can change or end. Registering the experience can include recognizing that it was brief, depended on support, or is not currently accessible. The person does not have to make it permanent to acknowledge it.

### Keep reflection connected to the event

A polished account of growth can become detached from what occurred. Someone says they are learning self-compassion while their actual discovery concerned a specific repair. A therapist may substitute a preferred framework for the person's simpler words.

The developing meaning should remain answerable to the experience. It can be revised, narrowed, or left incomplete. The same applies to bodily inquiry: repeated requests for sensations can obscure a clear verbal recognition rather than deepen it.

### Recognize shared conditions without making the helper indispensable

A development may have needed an attentive relationship or a particular environment. Acknowledging that support is accurate. It should not imply that the person possesses no capacity of their own or can access it only through this helper.

Conversely, describing the result as entirely self-generated can erase the conditions that made it possible. A useful account includes the person's participation and the contribution of support. Future access may require seeking or negotiating comparable conditions rather than simply remembering to try harder.

### Preserve the scale and scope of the observation

A felt shift does not establish a permanent trait change. Feeling stronger during an enactment does not predict how a manager will respond. A sense that a phrase is true does not verify a memory or diagnose an underlying cause.

The word integration can refer to several things: more ease, clearer meaning, a received experience, or a developing connection across situations. These meanings should not be collapsed into claims of neural installation, memory rewriting, or guaranteed resilience. The observation needs to be described at the level actually available.

### Leave the next step open

A useful moment need not immediately become homework, an announcement, or a difficult conversation. The person may want to rest or allow the meaning to remain provisional. Converting every development into a test can repeat the problem the encounter was beginning to loosen.

There may also be an urgent practical concern. Someone who needs to arrange transport, clarify a deadline, or finish the session should not have to complete a reflection ritual first. Registering can be brief, deferred, or omitted when another need takes precedence.

## Its place in the wider process

[[make-a-new-experience-possible|Make a New Experience Possible]] concerns access to a fitting difference. Let the Change Register concerns the experience and meaning of having encountered that difference. A new event and its impact can be explored almost together, but they are still distinguishable movements.

[[let-contrasting-experiences-meet|Let Contrasting Experiences Meet]] brings an expectation into relation with relevant different experience. Registering does not require a contradiction. A person may simply recognize what being accompanied or having more room was like. Conversely, noticing a meaningful contrast does not automatically reveal what it is like to have noticed it.

[[follow-the-whole-wave|Follow the Whole Wave]] attends to the development of an emotional process, including settling and a stopping place. This pattern gives particular attention to a difference that becomes available within that development and to the response it evokes.

[[support-what-protects|Support What Protects]] may become relevant if change brings a new pressure, fear, or concern about consequences. [[hear-the-whole-response|Hear the Whole Response]] makes room for these reactions alongside relief or pride. They do not have to be dismissed for the original development to count.

[[discover-what-makes-the-response-understandable|Discover What Makes the Response Understandable]] can clarify what the development means and why receiving it is complicated. [[give-experience-a-form|Give Experience a Form]] can provide a phrase, gesture, or symbolic reference through which a difference remains recognizable. Nora's sentence is one such expression, while the meaning she retains is more particular than the sentence alone.

[[find-the-next-living-expression|Find the Next Living Expression]] asks what continuation fits: a decision, action, conversation, different form of contact, or rest. Registering gives that question a more adequate starting point without deciding its answer in advance.

[[mourning-the-self|Mourning the Self]] gives more space to grief that can emerge when a new possibility makes an earlier absence more visible.

## Expressions and sources

Let the Change Register is an editorial family of moves. AEDP metaprocessing is its clearest named expression here, while receiving, savoring, and integration in other methods have their own purposes. The article does not claim that all these activities are equivalent or that a particular pause produces durable change. All encounters are fictional.

Diana Fosha's [introduction to AEDP 2.0](https://www.apa.org/pubs/books/undoing-aloneness-transformation-suffering-flourishing-sample-chapter.pdf) grounds the account of metatherapeutic processing within AEDP. The institute's [affective change processes](https://aedpinstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/AEDP-91-for-INTERVENTIONS-with-DESCRIPTIONS.pdf) distinguishes the experience of change from adjacent kinds of emotional and relational work. Jennifer Imming's [The Work of AEDP](https://aedpinstitute.org/journal/the-work-of-aedp/) supplies a clinical account of processing a relational repair.

Iwakabe and Conceição's [author manuscript](https://www.aedpresearch.com/uploads/3/7/6/5/37653853/5._metatherapeutic_processing2014may.pdf), associated with their [2016 task-analytic paper](https://doi.org/10.1037/int0000016), provides a research account of metatherapeutic processing. Its process-model aim is distinguished from proof of an isolated intervention effect.

Eugene Gendlin's [The Client's Client](https://focusing.org/gendlin/docs/gol_2149.html) grounds the receiving comparison. Ron Kurtz's [A Little History](https://hakomi.com/a-little-history/) grounds the Hakomi account of time given to a missing experience and its integration.

Classen and colleagues' [Sensorimotor group-treatment study](https://sensorimotorpsychotherapy.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/10/A-Pilot-RCT-of-A-Body-Oriented-Group-Therapy-For-Complex-Trauma-Survivors-An-Adaptation-of-Sensorimotor-Psychotherapy.pdf) situates savoring within a broader intervention. Pat Ogden's [teaching material](https://sensorimotorpsychotherapy.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/Trauma-Dissociation-Body-Handout.pdf) discusses bodily resources and the possible difficulties of positive experience. Neither source warrants a claim that a particular period of savoring installs a permanent state.

Raja Selvam's [protocol account](https://integralsomaticpsychology.com/protocol-embodying-emotion) and [workshop presentation](https://congress.eabp.org/2023/site/wp-content/uploads/Embodying-Emotions-EABP-Workshop-September-8-2023.pdf) supply the emotional embodiment comparison. Elliott, Auszra, Herrmann, Cunha, and colleagues' [EFT therapist competencies](https://emotionfocusedtherapy.eu/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/FC-EFT-Competence-v1.03-2.pdf) ground the account of consolidating change and meaning in individual Emotion-Focused Therapy. Their conceptual frameworks remain distinct from the unifying editorial description.
