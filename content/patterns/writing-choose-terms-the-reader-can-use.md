A specialist can write a perfectly accurate sentence that leaves the intended reader unable to use it. The obstacle may be a technical term, an abbreviation, or an ordinary word with a local meaning. Knowing every word's dictionary definition does not necessarily reveal what the sentence asks someone to understand or do.

This pattern helps when an explanation assumes membership of a profession or team, when a glossary expands labels without clarifying them, or when simplification removes a distinction the reader actually needs. **Choose vocabulary for the reader's task, and give necessary unfamiliar terms enough explanation to become useful.**

The aim is shared understanding. Sometimes an ordinary phrase can replace the specialist term entirely. Sometimes learning the term is part of what enables the reader to participate.

## Begin with what the reader must do

Writers often start with the language of the system they know. Readers may approach from a different direction: they want to finish a task and do not yet know which internal category it belongs to.

Imagine an invented community print studio sending instructions to a poet ordering a first booklet. The studio's message says:

> Please approve the proof before we proceed to production. Once approved, changes will require a new proof and may alter the delivery date.

For an experienced print buyer, _proof_ may be sufficient. A first-time customer may not know whether the studio means evidence of payment, a sample booklet, or the PDF attached to the email. The term is especially consequential because approval permits the next stage.

Suppose this studio's agreed process is that the customer checks a PDF for wording, page order, and placement before printing begins. The instructions can explain that process at the point of use:

> Please check the attached PDF, which we call your proof. Check the wording, page order, and placement of text and images, then email us either your corrections or your approval to print. We will begin printing after your approval. Later changes will need another proof and may delay delivery.

The revision does more than substitute words. It connects _proof_ to an identifiable document and to the customer's decision. Retaining the term helps the customer recognize it in later messages. The explanation also makes clear what an approval does within this particular studio's process.

Calling it simply “your file” throughout would be easier vocabulary, but could obscure the distinction between the poet's submitted manuscript and the studio's prepared version. A necessary term can protect understanding once its role is available.

## Explain the concept, not just the label

Expanding an abbreviation is useful only if the expanded phrase means something to the reader. A newcomer can learn what its letters stand for and remain unable to act on the instruction.

In a separate invented workplace, a team records ordinary equipment problems in an internal system. A welcome note tells a new colleague to “check the SLA before escalating.” Spelling out _service-level agreement_ may still leave both the agreement's role and the next step unclear.

If the local agreement specifies how long the support team has to respond, the note can say: “Check the response time listed in the support agreement before asking the team leader to follow up.” If the colleague will repeatedly encounter the abbreviation, a following sentence can identify it: “The support pages call this agreement the SLA.”

The explanation needs to reflect the actual agreement. It should not imply that every service-level agreement concerns only response time, or that the local rule applies everywhere. The writer is making one term usable for one audience's immediate purpose.

The same care applies to definitions that merely exchange one unfamiliar word for another. An example, a contrast, or a statement of consequence may teach more than a formal category. In the print message, the reader learns what to examine and what happens after approval; a definition such as “a preproduction representation” would leave much of that work undone.

## Keep distinctions that expertise makes possible

Plain wording can become inaccurate when it collapses categories the reader must distinguish. The right response is often to explain the boundary rather than erase the term.

Imagine a rehearsal schedule that marks a session as a _read-through_. The organizer could replace that term with “rehearsal,” but actors might then prepare for movement, costume, or technical work that the session does not include. For newcomers, “Read-through: we will read the script aloud together; no staging or costumes” makes the label useful. For a group that already knows the format, the short label may be enough.

Audience knowledge is therefore specific. The same person may understand theatre terminology and be new to print production. A mixed group may include experts in several fields without sharing any one field's shorthand.

A brief explanation where a term first matters can give those readers a common starting point. A glossary can support later reference, but it asks readers to leave the passage and find a definition. When the next instruction depends on understanding the term, that detour may be an unnecessary burden.

## Avoid both insider shorthand and needless translation

Replacing every long word or expanding every abbreviation can make prose laborious for readers who already know the vocabulary. It can also interrupt an explanation with background unrelated to their task. Familiarity should be judged in relation to the intended audience, rather than inferred from the number of syllables or capital letters.

The most useful evidence comes from what readers actually understand. In the print studio, asking a new customer to describe what happens after receiving the proof could reveal a misunderstanding that “Is this clear?” would miss. That would test this explanation's usefulness; it would not establish that all customers need the same wording.

Ordinary words deserve attention too. A team may use _live_ to mean publicly accessible, actively monitored, or ready for final review. Substituting _available_ without checking the intended condition can leave the ambiguity intact. The writer needs the shared meaning as well as the familiar word.

Once a term has been introduced, consistent use lets the explanation build on it. Repeatedly replacing it with new approximations can force readers to learn several labels for one concept. A small amount of teaching can make later prose both more exact and more economical.

## Source note

The Australian Government Style Manual's [Plain language and word choice](https://www.stylemanual.gov.au/writing-and-designing-content/clear-language-and-writing-style/plain-language-and-word-choice) recommends learning the audience's vocabulary, explaining unfamiliar concepts, and retaining specialist terms when audience research supports them. The CDC Clear Communication Index's [guidance on words the primary audience uses](https://www.cdc.gov/ccindex/tool/page-7.html) recommends explaining unfamiliar terms where they occur and checking understanding with the audience. The print studio, support agreement, and rehearsal are original, hypothetical examples with locally specified meanings; they are not universal industry procedures or technical standards.
