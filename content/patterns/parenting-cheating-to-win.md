## 1. A recognizable situation

Your eight-year-old rolls a three, moves five spaces, and announces that they have won.

Their sibling objects. You saw the extra moves too.

“It doesn’t matter,” your child says. “You always let them win.”

You correct the move. They insist they did nothing wrong, push the board away, and begin crying.

Several things now need attention: the unfair result, the other player’s frustration, your child’s distress, and what will happen next time losing becomes possible.

**The aim is fair participation, with an opportunity to repair mistakes and learn to tolerate an uncertain outcome.** Winning fairly can remain important to the child. Cheating cannot determine the result.

## 2. What might be happening

Start by establishing what happened and whether the child understood the rule.

**It may be a mistake or an unclear agreement.** Miscounting, forgetting a rule, and knowingly taking an extra turn can look similar. Ask the child to demonstrate the rule or compare it with the instructions. House rules also differ between families.

**The child may understand fairness but struggle to act on it.** Knowing that cheating is wrong does not guarantee resisting an available advantage. Research with children aged three to six found an association between judging cheating negatively and cheating less in a laboratory task, but rule knowledge and behavior were not interchangeable. [Zhao and colleagues, 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12208014/).

**Losing may carry more meaning than the game itself.** Notice whether cheating follows several losses, occurs mainly against a particular sibling, or increases when others are watching. The child may experience losing as humiliation, proof of being less capable, or a threat to their place in the family. These are possibilities to investigate, not motives to assign automatically.

**The task may be poorly matched to their abilities.** A lengthy strategy game against much more experienced players offers a different challenge from a short game with a realistic chance of success. Difficulty remembering rules can add frustration.

**The surrounding rewards may encourage shortcuts.** Rankings, prizes, selection decisions, public scores, or adults’ reactions can make the outcome unusually important. Some children may also take an unfair advantage simply because it is available and rewarding.

Later, ask a specific question: “What felt hardest when you thought you might lose?” Also examine whether the pattern changes with simpler rules, different opponents, or less public scoring.

## 3. How development changes the picture

**Preschool children need help understanding the agreement.** Explain a small number of rules and demonstrate them during play. Check whether they can count moves, recognize whose turn it is, and understand the endpoint.

Young age does not mean intentional cheating is impossible. Experiments document it in preschoolers. But an adult should establish understanding before interpreting a rule violation as deliberate deception.

Use immediate correction: “The die shows three. Let’s count three spaces.” A lengthy lecture about integrity is unlikely to resolve a counting or rule-following problem.

**During the primary-school years**, children can increasingly understand how cheating affects another player and the meaning of a result. They can help restore the score, explain a disputed rule, and choose an alternative response to disappointment.

Responsibility should increase alongside support. An eight-year-old who understands the rule may still need help pausing before secretly changing a move.

**Older children and adolescents** encounter competition with wider consequences: team selection, tournaments, examinations, and online rankings. Discuss which assistance is permitted, how ambiguous rules are resolved, and how to challenge unfair conditions without secretly taking an advantage.

Academic evidence should not be generalized casually to younger children’s games. A meta-analysis found learning-focused goals associated with less academic dishonesty, but almost all included studies concerned secondary-school or university students and most used correlational designs. [Fritz and colleagues, 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-023-09753-1).

Individual differences matter too. Language, attention, memory, and emotional regulation affect how much support a child needs. An agreed adaptation—simpler rules, assistance, or a head start—is compatible with fairness when everyone understands it.

## 4. What needs balancing

Your child needs help with disappointment and a credible route back into play. Other players deserve a fair game and protection from repeated manipulation of the result.

Understanding why cheating happened does not require preserving its benefit. Equally, correcting the result does not require humiliating the child.

Autonomy includes choosing whether to join a game, asking for a different challenge, requesting clarification, or taking an agreed break. It does not include secretly changing the agreement for everyone else.

Parents also have practical limits. A family game cannot become an hour-long investigation into every move. Simpler rules, visible scores, and shorter sessions can make fair play easier to maintain.

Some consequences belong to other people or institutions. A friend may decline another game. A tournament may have a process for correcting an unfair result. Supporting your child includes helping them navigate those consequences without demanding immediate forgiveness from others.

## 5. Possible responses

The approaches below combine experimental findings, professional guidance, and practical synthesis. The suggested corrections and dialogue have not been validated together as a parenting program.

### A. Stop the unfair advantage and make a proportionate repair

**This fits when** you have clear evidence that a rule was broken.

Describe the action rather than assigning an identity:

> “You moved five spaces after rolling three. Your piece belongs here.”

Correct the move or score where possible. If the position cannot be reconstructed, agree on a fair resolution with everyone involved. Restarting may be reasonable, but it should not automatically erase another player’s legitimate lead.

You do not need a confession before correcting something you observed. If the child denies it, avoid making the entire game depend on winning that argument.

> “We disagree about what happened. I saw the move, and this is where the piece goes.”

Keep the emotional response separate from the result: “You really wanted that win” can accompany moving the piece back.

If fair play cannot continue, pause or end the game. Explain that decision through the activity: “We can play when the moves stay within the rules.” Avoid unrelated, escalating penalties.

This is a practical recommendation grounded in fairness and workable supervision, rather than a claim that one consequence has been proven best. [AAP guidance](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/family-life/family-dynamics/communication-discipline/Pages/Competition-and-Cheating.aspx) recommends addressing cheating while examining task difficulty and pressure, and cautions against excessive punishment.

### B. Make competition manageable and teach a response to losing

**This fits when** cheating appears around frustration, a large skill mismatch, or difficulty continuing after setbacks.

Before playing, choose a game the child can understand and complete without excessive strain. Options include shorter rounds, openly adjusted rules, practice without scoring, or occasional cooperative games.

An adaptation should be agreed before it affects the result. “You can start with two extra pieces while you learn” is different from secretly adding pieces when losing becomes likely.

Prepare one response the child can actually use:

> “If you want to change a move because you’re losing, put your hands beside the board and ask for a pause.”

Practise briefly during an ordinary game. During the pause, help them decide whether they can continue under the existing rules. They can remain unhappy. The score does not need to change to settle the feeling.

Model disappointment without turning the moment into a performance: “I wanted that point. I missed it. Your turn.”

Sportsmanship guidance recommends fair rules, respectful winning and losing, and adult modelling. These are professional recommendations; they do not establish a particular practice schedule or an ideal ratio of wins to losses. [Stanford Medicine Children’s Health](https://www.stanfordchildrens.org/en/topic/default?id=teaching-children-good-sportsmanship-1-4524).

### C. Reduce pressure around the result and inspect the competitive environment

**This fits when** winning seems tied to approval, status, or avoiding embarrassment.

Review what adults notice. Does the conversation after sport begin and end with the score? Do siblings mock the loser? Does a coach excuse rule-breaking when it benefits the team?

Ask about choices and learning alongside outcomes: “Which move worked?” or “What would you try differently?” Notice specific fairness that costs something: “You corrected the score even though it meant losing a point.”

There is causal evidence that reputational messages can affect cheating in narrow settings. Two studies involving three- and five-year-olds found that telling children they had a reputation for being smart increased cheating. These were short experimental tasks; they do not show that a single compliment damages character or that one alternative praise style prevents cheating long term. [Zhao and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1111/desc.12585).

For older children, examine the actual stakes: selection pressure, prizes, public leaderboards, or rewards that make each loss feel costly. Clarify permitted tools and assistance in online competitions. Check what “everyone cheats” refers to rather than accepting or dismissing it immediately.

The academic meta-analysis above found mixed associations for goals focused on demonstrating superior performance. It does not support the blanket claim that wanting to win causes dishonesty.

Where the culture itself rewards cheating, speak with the responsible adults. The child should not carry sole responsibility for fixing it.

### D. Build an honest route back into responsibility

**This fits when** the immediate incident is resolved but the child needs practice making fair decisions and repairing errors.

When everyone is calmer, discuss three concrete points: who was affected, what needs correcting, and what the child will do when the temptation returns.

For a younger child, that might mean rolling the die where everyone can see it and counting together. An older child might keep a shared score, ask for a ruling, or correct a submitted result.

Recognize disclosure without cancelling repair:

> “Thanks for telling me. We still need to remove the extra points. Let’s work out how.”

Offer bounded opportunities to participate responsibly. Transparent safeguards can coexist with trust; there is no need to declare the child permanently untrustworthy.

In five experiments involving 328 kindergarten children in China, children entrusted by an adult with a helping task subsequently cheated less than comparison groups. This provides causal evidence for that particular experience, not proof that saying “I trust you” or removing supervision will resolve repeated cheating. [Zhao and colleagues, 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38379064/).

Broader sports programs have some support too. A review of six intervention studies involving young people aged roughly ten to eighteen found improvements in measured respect for opponents and sporting conventions. Most studies were conducted in Spain, and outcomes largely used questionnaires. These were complete educational programs, not evidence that any isolated post-game discussion reduces actual cheating. [Systematic review and meta-analysis](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0252643).

## 6. Examples in practice

### A four-year-old who appears to take extra moves

A child counts the starting square as their first move and sometimes loses track of whose turn it is.

The parent demonstrates the movement and plays a short practice round with visible turn-taking. When the child later deliberately moves farther, the parent corrects that move using the same rule.

The response distinguishes learning the game from knowingly changing it. Both require guidance, but the explanation differs.

### An eight-year-old whose first attempt fails

A child agrees to ask for a pause when losing. Near the end, they hide an unfavorable card instead.

The parent restores the card. The child cries and refuses to continue. They end the game without awarding the disputed win.

Later, the child admits that asking for a pause felt like admitting defeat. They practise saying, “I need a minute, but I’m still playing.”

The original plan did not prevent cheating. It helped identify a more specific obstacle. The other player is not required to offer an immediate rematch.

### A fourteen-year-old using a prohibited online aid

A teenager says an unauthorized game aid is necessary because other players use it.

The parent checks the competition’s rules with them and distinguishes allowed settings from prohibited assistance. They address removal of the aid and any result that needs correcting.

They also ask whether teammates are pressuring the teenager to use it. If so, adult support may be needed around that relationship or competitive setting.

The teenager may still think the system is unfair. That concern can be pursued without treating a knowingly unfair result as legitimate.

## 7. Common mistakes and limitations

**Demanding that the child care less about winning.** Wanting to win is compatible with fair play. Teach what to do when winning is uncertain or unlikely.

**Letting the cheating stand to avoid distress.** This leaves the other player carrying the cost. Support the upset while addressing the advantage.

**Turning one action into a permanent identity.** “That move broke the rule” gives something specific to correct. “You’re a cheater” offers little guidance about returning to fair participation.

**Using promises as a complete solution.** In a study of 406 seven- to twelve-year-olds, voluntary promises reduced cheating in the Indian sample but not the German sample. The reasons for the difference remain uncertain. A promise is neither a guarantee nor a reliable test of character. [Kanngiesser and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.13952).

**Manufacturing constant defeat to teach resilience.** Repeatedly overwhelming a beginner does not reproduce balanced competition. Agree on an appropriate challenge rather than treating frustration as the lesson.

**Assuming a familiar tactic has proven lasting effects.** Brief experiments can establish an immediate effect within a task. They rarely establish enduring fairness across games, schoolwork, friendships, and settings without supervision.

**Ignoring adult exceptions.** Quietly accepting an incorrect call when it helps your team, while demanding honesty from the child, creates a real inconsistency worth addressing.

## 8. What to observe over time

Look beyond whether the child gets caught less often. Useful progress includes:

- Following agreed rules with fewer reminders.

- Correcting an advantage without being forced.

- Asking for clarification or a pause before changing the result.

- Remaining interested in play after losing.

- Disclosing cheating and participating in repair.

- Showing concern for other players’ opportunity to compete fairly.

Notice where the pattern remains. Cheating only against a particular sibling suggests a different context from cheating across games, schoolwork, and teams. Persistent difficulty with the rules may call for simpler instructions or assessment of a learning need.

Avoid setting traps to test whether the child has become honest. Use transparent support and ordinary opportunities to observe fair play.

Seek additional support when cheating is persistent and damaging relationships, accompanies substantial anxiety or aggression, or continues despite appropriate challenge, clear expectations, and consistent repair. A school professional, coach, or clinician can help examine the surrounding difficulties. The behavior alone does not establish a diagnosis.
