**Pattern 45 of 49 · Part 8: Arrange, Revise, and Finish**

**Use when:** Attractive layers accumulate until they hide the song’s focal material.

A new guitar part sounds attractive, so it stays. A keyboard doubles it, a second guitar adds movement, and a high line runs continuously above the singer. The arrangement becomes fuller while its central idea becomes harder to hear. Each part needs a reason to occupy the same time.

Give Each Part a Job means deciding what a part contributes to the combined music. Its function can change across sections. The goal is not to limit every instrument to one permanent role, but to make the relationships intentional.

## Working choice

Name each part’s contribution and separate competing roles through timing, register, duration, or removal. Doubling and overlap can reinforce an event; every instrument does not need a permanently separate role.

## Name the contribution

Useful jobs include establishing pulse, supplying bass direction, defining harmony, carrying melody, answering a phrase, connecting sections, and providing a sustained background. An instrument can perform several, but the writer should hear how they interact.

In Porch Light's opening, a plucked instrument introduces the seed. When the voice enters, that instrument can simplify into chord support. In the gaps after chorus lines, it can return as a response. The same sound now has different jobs in different places.

A part described only as “adding interest” may still be valuable, but the description is too vague to guide revision. What becomes less clear or less expressive when it is removed? The answer identifies its actual contribution.

## Separate rhythm, register, and duration

Two parts can have different rhythms and still compete in the same register. A sustained keyboard chord may mask a guitar's middle notes even when their attacks do not coincide. Moving one part higher, shortening its notes, or removing some chord tones can clarify both.

Likewise, placing parts in separate octaves does not automatically solve conflicting rhythms. A high repeated figure can interrupt a singer's phrasing even when its pitches sit well above the melody. Arrangement needs attention to several dimensions at once.

An original verse setting uses low bass roots, a sparse mid-register chord, and a voice whose phrases leave gaps. A high response enters only in those gaps. Each layer has a clear relationship to the others without requiring a complex production.

## Make a role map

| Part             | Verse job                 | Chorus job                | Bridge job          |
| ---------------- | ------------------------- | ------------------------- | ------------------- |
| Voice            | Scene and new information | Central request           | Admission and turn  |
| Bass             | Harmonic orientation      | Firmer repeating pattern  | Longer notes        |
| Chord instrument | Sparse support            | Broader sustained setting | Reduced texture     |
| High figure      | Mostly absent             | Answers between lines     | Brief fragment only |
| Drums            | Light reference           | Clearer groove            | Reduced or absent   |

This is one original arrangement of Porch Light. The map is not a rule that choruses need more parts. A quieter chorus could reverse several entries while keeping the same intention to clarify the focal line.

A part's job and its time of entry answer different, connected questions.

## Use doubling deliberately

Two instruments playing the same line can act as one composite sound. That is different from accidental redundancy. A bass and kick meeting on a downbeat can reinforce a shared event; two differently articulated melodies can blur the same phrase if their coordination is unclear.

A vocal double can thicken a line, while a harmony adds different pitches. Neither is simply another generic layer. The original phrase's intelligibility and emphasis should remain a reference when deciding whether the added part helps.

If two parts are nearly identical, one can also become a selective reinforcement: entering only at the title or final return. Their meeting then becomes an event rather than a permanent condition.

## Kerala: a vocal sound can have an instrumental job

Bonobo's **Kerala**, on [Migration](https://bonobomusic.bandcamp.com/album/migration), gives a recurring chopped vocal sound a role different from that of a continuous narrative lead singer. Its fragments contribute identity and rhythm while bass, drums, and sustained textures supply other kinds of motion and weight. Following the vocal sound alone, then the low part, makes that division easier to hear.

A useful role map describes what the listener follows, not an imagined list of tracks in the production session. A sampled voice can be a foreground motif; a synthesizer can provide punctuation rather than continuous harmony. Platform Three uses the same freedom with original materials: the bass provides the characteristic low gesture, a higher part answers, and a sustained layer can hold the setting. If every part starts answering at once, the roles become less distinct. Kerala's release identifies the recording; this discussion concerns audible roles rather than claims about the source sample, software, or the order in which Bonobo built the track. One sound can also change jobs as the arrangement develops.

## Arrange by removal as well as addition

A full loop can become a source from which sections are carved. Remove the high figure in the verse, withhold the bass in the opening, and reduce drums in the bridge. The song develops through changed combinations rather than a new instrument at every boundary.

This approach can reveal that a seemingly essential part is only covering another weakness. If removing the keyboard exposes an unclear bass line, the bass may need revision. If removing every accompaniment still leaves a confusing lyric, arrangement alone will not settle the writing.

Do not make subtraction a moral rule. Some music gains its character from dense layers or collective overlap. The relevant question is whether the density serves a recognizable musical intention.

This pattern helps when a mix feels crowded, when a vocal disappears after adding accompaniment, or when every section contains every part. It also makes collaboration more specific: “answer the vocal in the second half of each four-bar phrase” is clearer than “play something interesting.”
