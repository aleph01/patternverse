**Core question:** How can separate units make coherent decisions when the result that matters depends on all of them?

## What it is for

Imagine a hypothetical broadband provider. Sales signs up more customers, installation keeps crews busy, and support shortens calls. Each department meets its targets, yet households repeatedly chase appointments and wait for working connections.

The difficulty is not that nobody has goals. The goals reward different parts of a result that no department can deliver alone.

Shared Outcomes gives those units a common basis for choosing priorities: customers receive a working connection by the agreed date, with avoidable repeat visits reduced. Each unit retains its responsibilities, but leaders also examine whether their combined choices produce that result.

This pattern applies across teams, departments, business units, or partner organizations. It concerns organizational planning and resource commitments, extending beyond individuals agreeing to help one another finish a task.

## The core idea

**Agree on a result that requires several units, then connect their distinct contributions, measures, and priority decisions to it.**

An outcome is a change or benefit experienced, such as a customer obtaining a usable connection. An output is something delivered, such as an appointment booked. Outputs remain necessary; they do not establish that the intended benefit occurred.

Sharing an outcome does not mean giving everyone the same task or making every decision collectively. Units contribute different capabilities and remain answerable for their commitments. What changes is the basis for resolving conflicts between local targets and the wider result.

The UK government’s transformation guidance similarly asks leaders across organizations to design shared outcomes together and connect their delivery plans. [Cabinet Office, _The 7 Lenses of Transformation_, collaboration lens](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/7-lenses-of-transformation/the-7-lenses-of-transformation)

This can work with objectives and key results, service plans, or another planning method. Adopting a particular framework, reporting dashboard, or bonus scheme is not the pattern itself.

## How it works

![Sales, installation, and support make distinct commitments toward a shared customer result. Outcome evidence and operating constraints feed a joint review, where authorized leaders adjust priorities, capacity, or the plan. Shared direction preserves named responsibility for each contribution.](../assets/organizational-design/shared-outcomes.svg)

**Choose a meaningful boundary.** Name the people who should benefit, the change sought, and the period being considered. Keep the scope narrow enough for participating units to influence. “Improve customer experience” is too broad to resolve a disagreement about installation capacity.

**Agree what would count as progress.** Establish the baseline, population, time window, and meaning of each measure. Combine outcome evidence with earlier signals that help teams act. For the broadband illustration, completed bookings reveal activity; working connections by the promised date reveal more of the customer result. Check repeat visits, cost, and access for difficult-to-serve customers alongside speed.

**Map contributions without pretending certainty.** Sales might improve promise accuracy, installation might reserve suitable capacity, and support might identify unresolved connection failures. Describe why each contribution is expected to help. Treat that connection as a hypothesis to examine, rather than a guarantee that completing every project will improve the outcome.

**Give commitments owners and resources.** Each unit names an accountable person, agrees capacity, and exposes dependencies before finalizing its plan. Keep responsibility for convening the shared review explicit. Shared direction does not authorize one unit to allocate another’s staff or budget unilaterally.

**Make tradeoffs decidable.** Agree which decisions units can make independently and who resolves cross-unit conflicts. A shared outcome needs access to leaders who can change priorities, resources, or rules. Otherwise the review becomes a place to describe problems that nobody present can address.

**Review evidence together.** Use a common account of results, alongside frontline observations, customer feedback, and unit-level information. Investigate where improvement stalled or costs moved elsewhere. Record decisions, owners, and follow-up rather than merely collecting status reports.

**Revisit the agreement.** Demand, capacity, and external conditions change. Escalate missing contributions and conflicting obligations promptly. Revise an unrealistic target transparently, preserving the reason and evidence instead of quietly changing definitions to improve the report.

## Why it works

A shared result makes the consequences of local choices more visible. Sales can see when an attractive promise overloads installation. Installation can distinguish productive crew time from work that creates another customer visit.

Common evidence also gives leaders a basis for discussing tradeoffs. A unit can explain why helping another may improve the overall result even when its own activity measure temporarily worsens. Explicit commitments turn that explanation into a resource decision.

The mechanism depends on influence, usable information, and authority to respond. Units need to see how their work can affect the result, and leaders must tolerate honest reports of disappointing evidence. A target without those conditions can add pressure while changing little about how decisions are made.

## Examples

### England and Wales: reducing reoffending in the 2021–22 plan

The Ministry of Justice’s July 2021 Outcome Delivery Plan connected reducing reoffending with accommodation, employment, and continuity of substance-misuse treatment. Its indicators included employment six months after release, settled accommodation after three months, and timely engagement with community treatment. These went beyond counting completed prison or probation activities. [Ministry of Justice, _Outcome Delivery Plan: 2021–22_](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ministry-of-justice-outcome-delivery-plan/ministry-of-justice-outcome-delivery-plan-2021-22)

The plan described work with other departments and local partners, alongside a justice-led delivery programme. It also proposed evaluating implementation and intended outcomes to decide which approaches should continue, change, or stop. The wider government planning framework explicitly supported departments planning together toward cross-cutting outcomes. [Cabinet Office, _Outcome Delivery Plans_, July 2021](https://www.gov.uk/government/collections/outcome-delivery-plans)

This illustrates strategic alignment across responsibilities that one department cannot fully control. It documents a planning arrangement and proposed evaluation, not proof that the arrangement reduced reoffending or that these historical responsibilities remain unchanged.

### GitLab’s documented security planning process

GitLab’s Security OKRs handbook, in a version marked last modified June 2024 and consulted in October 2026, describes functional leaders proposing objectives or contributing to existing ones. Draft reviews identify dependencies across functional areas. A dependent team must confirm its ability to support another team’s key result before that result is finalized. [GitLab Handbook, _Security OKRs_](https://handbook.gitlab.com/handbook/security/okr/)

If capacity is unavailable, the key result is narrowed or removed, and the capacity decision is recorded for leadership planning. Objectives and key results have named responsible individuals; a key-result owner can sit outside the department owning the objective. Reporting includes progress, risks, and blockers.

The arrangement demonstrates how common direction can coexist with distinct ownership and explicit negotiation of resources. Its contribution to this pattern is the planning agreement, rather than evidence that every listed key result measures an outcome or that the process improved security performance. The handbook documents expectations, not independent confirmation of execution in every quarter.

## Useful when

- **Local targets improve while the overall result disappoints.** Customers or colleagues still experience gaps between units.
- **No unit can achieve the priority alone.** Several capabilities must contribute to a meaningful change.
- **Plans assume unconfirmed help.** Departments commit to results before securing another unit’s capacity.
- **Tradeoffs recur without resolution.** Leaders need a common basis for choosing between competing local improvements.

## Common mistakes and limits

**Sharing a slogan.** A broad ambition needs meaningful evidence, contributions, and decisions behind it.

**Making everyone accountable for everything.** Keep named owners for contributions, review, and unresolved decisions. Collective attention cannot replace specific obligations.

**Imposing identical targets.** Units affect an outcome in different ways. Shared direction should make their contributions coherent, rather than erase useful specialist measures.

**Rewarding a single number.** Faster connections might conceal repeat visits or exclusion of difficult customers. Preserve quality, cost, and fairness checks.

**Confusing influence with control.** External events and long delays complicate attribution. Investigate contribution and context before judging performance or assigning rewards.

Maintaining shared outcomes consumes leadership attention. Use it for genuine interdependence; independently achievable work may need straightforward local ownership. Deeply conflicting mandates may require a policy, funding, or structural change before a common target becomes workable.
