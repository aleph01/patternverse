## A familiar moment

Your phone lights up with a message: “You still haven't done it?” You have been trying to finish three other things. Your face warms, your shoulders tighten, and a reply is already forming: “Maybe do it yourself.” Your thumb hovers over Send.

The issue may deserve a direct conversation. The immediate question is whether the next few seconds will help you have that conversation. STOP offers a brief interruption in which you can notice the urge and choose what to do with it. You do not have to settle the disagreement or become calm before beginning.

## Understand the skill

STOP is a distress tolerance skill from Dialectical Behavior Therapy, or DBT. Distress tolerance means getting through a difficult moment without adding avoidable problems. STOP stands for **Stop, Take a step back, Observe, and Proceed mindfully**. [DBT Self Help's STOP guide](https://dbtselfhelp.com/stop/) describes this established sequence.

The skill separates an emotion from the action it suggests. Anger can be present while you wait before sending a message. Anxiety can be present while you ask for clarification. A pause creates an opportunity to consider the situation; it does not guarantee that the opportunity will feel comfortable.

Proceeding mindfully means acting with awareness of what is happening and what matters to you. Sometimes that involves speaking firmly, leaving, asking for help, or arranging a later conversation. Pausing is useful when it makes a more effective response available.

## When to use it

Look for the moment just before an action: reaching for your phone, talking faster, composing a cutting comment, or wanting to cancel everything after one difficult exchange. You can also use STOP after noticing that a reaction has already started. There may still be a next sentence or next click to choose.

Practise with ordinary frustration before relying on it during a very intense moment. If stopping to reflect would delay an urgent protective response, act for safety first. Do not freeze in traffic, remain in a threatening interaction, or postpone necessary medical care to complete the steps. During driving or another attention-demanding activity, keep attention on the task and return to the exercise later.

## How to practice

The four steps below preserve the DBT sequence. The physical examples are optional adaptations for everyday use.

1. **Stop.** Interrupt the action for a moment. Take your finger away from Send, stop adding words, or set down the object you are about to throw. “Pause” can be your private cue.
2. **Take a step back.** Create a little space. Put the phone down or say, “I need a moment before I answer.” If comfortable, let one ordinary breath pass. You do not need a forced deep breath.
3. **Observe.** Notice the outside situation and your inner response: what was actually said, what you are thinking, what you feel, and what you want to do. Keep an interpretation separate from an observation.
4. **Proceed mindfully.** Ask what action would help with your goals and what might make things harder. Choose one next move, including a further pause if needed.

[Linehan's distress tolerance teaching notes](https://www.guilford.com/add/linehan7_old/lin-p-4teaching.pdf?t=1) support the pause-and-choice purpose. A particular pause length is not part of the acronym.

## See it in action

Return to the message. You stop with the unsent reply still on the screen. You place the phone face down and lean back in your chair. The irritation remains.

While observing, you separate a few pieces: “The message says I haven't done it. I'm thinking they don't appreciate my work. My jaw is tight. I want to send something sharp.” The thought about appreciation may be understandable, but the message alone does not establish the other person's intention.

You want the practical issue resolved and want to avoid spending the evening in an argument. Proceeding might mean replacing the draft with: “I'm working on other deadlines. Which part do you need today?” It could also mean waiting ten minutes and then answering.

The other person may reply impatiently. You might still need to state a boundary. The useful difference is that you chose the next message with your goal in view. STOP has not required you to agree with the criticism or suppress your anger.

## Try it yourself

**Pause, Notice, Choose** takes about 3–5 minutes. Use a manageable situation, keep details in your own notes, or select the fully written message example. You will physically pause, create a little space, observe, and choose one next action.

You will leave with a short cue-and-action reminder. The practice is an original interactive adaptation of STOP. It includes optional observations and a brief experiential pause; it does not assess whether you performed the skill correctly.

## When it feels difficult

**“I notice too late.”** Start at the first point you do notice. If you have already sent one sharp message, pause before sending another. A smaller first step is noticing the aftermath without criticizing yourself, then identifying one earlier cue for another time.

**“Taking a step back feels like avoiding the issue.”** Give the pause a practical boundary when appropriate: “I want to answer this carefully. I'll come back after lunch.” Start by delaying one sentence. Returning may require a plan, but you do not owe renewed contact where it would be unsafe.

**“I'm still angry, so it didn't work.”** Ask whether you interrupted an action or saw an additional option. Those observations matter even when the feeling stays strong. Your smallest step can be taking your hand off the keyboard. You can then seek support or use another distress tolerance skill instead of demanding instant calm.

## Bring it into daily life

Choose one predictable, low-pressure cue, such as feeling impatient while a page loads. Briefly stop, step back, observe, and choose whether to continue or change tasks. This gives you a chance to learn what a pause feels like before a harder situation.

Which early cue could remind you that there is still a next action to choose?

“When I notice my thumb hovering over Send, I can try putting down the phone and checking what response would help.”

## Practice — Pause, Notice, Choose

Create a brief pause before acting on an urge Allow 3–5 minutes. Leave with a pause cue and a chosen next response. All personal text is optional; pause or end whenever needed. This is an original educational adaptation.
