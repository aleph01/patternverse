Noel brings an unopened letter from his brother to therapy. They have not spoken for two years. Noel wants to understand what receiving the letter has stirred up before deciding whether to read it.

His therapist suggests placing the envelope on the table. Noel agrees. A moment later he is talking quickly about everything that happened between them, barely hearing the questions. The envelope is closed, but the encounter has already become much larger than either of them expected.

Putting it back in his bag helps a little. More useful is clarifying that understanding his reaction does not commit him to opening it. With that distinction available, he can approach one part of the experience: the feeling that somebody else's request has already become his obligation. The letter remains unread. Contact with what it means has become more possible.

## The difficulty this pattern addresses

Experiential work often invites someone closer to something: a feeling, memory, bodily sensation, impulse, imagined exchange, or unfamiliar possibility. Closeness can make important information available. It can also make participation harder if the encounter becomes too demanding, too prolonged, or too difficult to influence.

A person may want to explore a subject while finding the proposed form unmanageable. Talking about a conflict may be possible, while imagining the other person's face is too evocative. One specific acknowledgment may be welcome, while an extended appreciation feels exposing. A brief movement may clarify an impulse that a forceful enactment would obscure.

The reverse also happens. Someone can discuss a difficulty so remotely that none of its present meaning becomes available. They understand an interpretation but cannot tell whether it fits. They may need a more concrete example, more time, or enough relational support to stop explaining and notice what is happening.

There is no reliable correspondence between the apparent size of an intervention and its experienced impact. A single word can summon a whole relationship. A detailed narrative can remain relatively distant. Closing one's eyes, moving a chair, or being watched more closely may change the encounter more than adding another minute of discussion.

The listener can misread the response. Quietness may be steadiness, fatigue, concentration, compliance, or a loss of connection. Tears can occur with meaningful participation or with a sense of being carried beyond choice. An intense response does not by itself identify either productive work or overwhelm.

These uncertainties can produce unhelpful extremes. A therapist may keep reducing intensity until little contact remains, or press for fuller immersion because they assume greater feeling means greater progress. A person may believe that they must either avoid the whole subject or experience all of it at once.

Find a Workable Amount of Contact offers a wider range of possibilities. The amount, form, timing, distance, and available support can change. The aim is meaningful engagement that the person can still influence, rather than a predetermined level of emotion.

The pattern applies to wanted experience as well as distress. Affection, recognition, pleasure, confidence, and closeness can be difficult to receive. Making those encounters workable does not mean training someone to accept unlimited praise, intimacy, or attention. Their preferences and the purpose of the work still matter.

## The core move

**Adjust how experience is approached until there is enough contact to matter and enough room for the person to notice, respond, and choose.**

“Enough” is discovered through the encounter. It cannot be assigned from the severity of the event, the therapist's preferred pace, or comparison with another person. What was manageable yesterday may be too much today, while something previously distant may now be approachable.

Contact has several dimensions. Its amount includes how much material is evoked and for how long. Its form includes words, sensation, imagery, movement, and direct exchange. Its distance concerns how immediately the person is immersed in it or able to regard it. Support includes another person's participation, the setting, information, practical arrangements, and resources that actually help.

These dimensions interact. More support may make closer contact possible without reducing the emotion. A less vivid representation may allow a clearer meaning to emerge. Another person may remain deeply engaged while choosing ordinary conversation instead of inward bodily attention.

Somatic Experiencing distinguishes titration, concerning manageable increments, from pendulation, concerning movement between charged and resourced experience. Here they are compared with other ways of adjusting contact, while preserving their distinct purposes. [SE glossary](https://traumahealing.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/04/A-Glossary-of-SE-Terms-rev-4.24.21-1.pdf).

| Adjustment | What changes | A useful distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Titration | The amount or duration approached in an increment. | A short encounter can still be highly evocative. |
| Pendulation | Movement between charged experience and support, settling, or another available state. | Movement need not follow an equal or automatic rhythm. |
| Distance and representation | How immediately the experience is encountered: described, imagined, symbolized, or enacted. | More distance can support contact or increase disconnection. |
| Differentiation | The relationship to a feeling, belief, or part that has become total. | Perspective can coexist with emotion; detachment is not the necessary aim. |
| Wider support or context | What else is available while approaching the experience. | A proposed resource helps only if it is experienced as helpful here. |
| Greater specificity or vividness | How much of a remote account becomes personally recognizable. | Sometimes workable contact needs more access, rather than further reduction. |

Workability is a practical, collaborative judgment. Can the person follow what is happening well enough to participate? Can they convey uncertainty or influence the next move? Is the experience connected to the purpose they chose? These questions are more useful than treating calmness as the universal criterion.

Nor does the pattern require continuous self-monitoring. Asking someone to report sensations every few seconds can interrupt the very contact it is meant to support. Ordinary conversation, an agreed signal, and occasional checks may provide enough information.

A workable encounter can remain painful or effortful. It can also end without returning to the chosen material. The ability to stop is part of the arrangement, not a promise to take a break and then resume regardless of what the person wants.

## How the experience unfolds

These movements can recur in different orders. They describe responsive adjustment within a chosen direction, rather than a ladder toward progressively stronger activation.

### Establish what the person wants to approach

The subject and the task are different. Someone may want to understand their response to a letter without reading it, recognize anger without confronting anyone, or consider a memory without replaying its most vivid moment. Agreement about the subject does not settle the form of work.

Clarifying that distinction can itself make contact easier. A person who thought naming a wish would commit them to action may become able to recognize it once that assumption is examined. Sometimes the apparent problem of intensity partly concerns what they believe will be required next.

If the person does not want the proposed work, reducing its size does not resolve that disagreement. A smaller unwanted intervention remains unwanted. Choice of direction and manageability within that direction both deserve attention.

### Discover what is actually available now

The starting point may be a clear emotion, an image, a thought, a bodily response, or uncertainty. Someone may have a detailed account but little present feeling. Another may feel a great deal while finding words difficult. Neither presentation should automatically determine the next technique.

The therapist can ask about the person's experience and notice changes without assigning them a meaning. If speech becomes rapid, it may help to find out whether the person is eager, overwhelmed, trying to finish, or responding to interruption. An observed change is a reason for curiosity.

The present conditions belong in that inquiry. Fatigue, pain, sensory demands, limited privacy, and concern about time can affect participation. An adjustment to the setting may be more useful than an interpretation of emotional resistance.

### Change one relevant feature where possible

The work can become more specific, shorter, less vivid, less interpersonal, or more supported. It can also become more concrete when the existing account is too distant to use. The adjustment should answer a recognizable difficulty rather than a general instruction to regulate.

Changing one feature can make its effect easier to understand. Someone may keep discussing the same moment while looking away, or stay with the same feeling while the therapist speaks less. This is a practical preference, not a rule that complex encounters must be experimentally controlled.

The person need not demonstrate a bodily technique to establish that an adjustment has helped. Being able to find a word, ask a question, or correct the proposed direction can be meaningful feedback.

### Notice whether participation has become more available

The relevant difference is functional. A person can remain connected to what matters while having more room to respond. They might distinguish two feelings, recognize a limit, notice the listener again, or decide that a different subject needs attention.

Relief is useful information, but it can have different meanings. The material may have become more bearable, or it may have disappeared from awareness altogether. Either can be a welcome respite. They should not automatically be described as the same kind of experiential processing.

Likewise, less visible distress is not sufficient evidence of renewed participation. If the person feels farther away, cannot follow, or is continuing to please the therapist, another adjustment may be needed. Their account remains central to understanding the change.

### Allow movement without imposing alternation

Attention may move toward charged material, toward support, and back again. Sometimes that movement develops naturally. Sometimes an agreed pause or a shift toward the present setting helps. There is no requirement to spend equal time in each place.

Repeated switching can also interrupt useful contact. Someone who is finding words for grief may need the listener to stay with them rather than redirect attention at every rise in feeling. The therapist's discomfort with intensity should not set the rhythm.

A return remains a choice. The person might approach a different aspect, continue in conversation, or end the exploration. A pause is more credible when it is allowed to change the plan.

### Reassess the direction as well as the amount

Adjustment can reveal that the original task was mistaken. A person who cannot receive reassurance may need a factual question answered. Someone struggling with an imagined confrontation may want help deciding whether the relationship is worth further contact.

The pattern is useful only if it permits these discoveries. Otherwise, the therapist keeps changing the dose of a task whose purpose has ceased to fit. Workability includes whether this is still the work the person wants and needs.

The ending also supplies information. Enough time may be needed to return to practical matters, acknowledge what remains open, and make an understandable transition. Leaving something unfinished does not make the amount approached a failure.

### How different approaches implement the move

The six accounts below compare particular implementations and rationales. Their encounters are original fictional illustrations, not reported cases or evidence of effectiveness. Similar concerns about manageability do not make the approaches' explanations interchangeable.

#### Somatic Experiencing: manageable increments and movement around activation

SE's terms titration and pendulation distinguish the amount approached from movement between different experiential states. Resourcing and orientation can contribute to this work. The glossary's broader physiological explanations are part of the model; the observable account here concerns changes in attention, experience, and participation. [SE glossary](https://traumahealing.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/04/A-Glossary-of-SE-Terms-rev-4.24.21-1.pdf).

In a fictional encounter, Esther wants to explore the anger that followed a dismissive meeting. As she describes every exchange, she loses track of what she wants the therapist to understand. They agree to approach one moment: hearing her contribution called unnecessary.

That sentence alone proves highly evocative. Esther notices heat and an urge to argue her entire case. The therapist offers a pause. Esther looks toward the window and finds the wider room useful; being directed to her breathing would feel like another demand.

When she returns to the sentence, she notices something more specific: she had information the group needed. Her anger has a point she can name. A moment later she wants to stop examining sensations and talk about the meeting's practical implications.

The fictional movement includes a smaller entry and a change of attention, but no required cycle of activation and release. The room is useful because Esther experiences it as widening her options. The shift does not demonstrate a discharge of stored energy or establish that the conflict has been resolved.

#### Sensorimotor Psychotherapy: changing the conditions of bodily and relational contact

Ogden's teaching describes directed mindfulness, smaller actions, breaks into ordinary discussion, and attention to relational distance, posture, and gaze. It also emphasizes client control of physical interventions. These offer several possible adjustments to contact rather than a single instruction to become calmer. [Trauma, Dissociation and the Body](https://sensorimotorpsychotherapy.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/Trauma-Dissociation-Body-Handout.pdf).

In this fictional example, Leon wants to understand why he stops speaking when someone interrupts. His therapist suggests noticing the impulse in his hands. Leon becomes preoccupied with whether he is sensing the correct thing. The inward task has added a performance demand.

They return to ordinary conversation about a recent interruption. Leon spontaneously moves one hand slightly forward. With permission, they notice that movement without making it larger. He says that it means, “I wasn't finished.”

A suggestion to hold the hand up for longer is less useful. Leon starts monitoring whether the gesture looks assertive. They let the hand rest and stay with his words instead. He can describe the difference between wanting room to finish and wanting to silence the other person.

The illustration shows why a smaller movement is only one possible adjustment. Changing the channel and ending the movement matter too. More bodily attention would not necessarily create more meaningful contact. The useful feedback concerns what Leon can recognize and influence, rather than how clearly he performs a boundary gesture.

#### Focusing: making space without losing the relationship to what matters

Gendlin describes making a space by acknowledging concerns without immediately entering each one. His account also distinguishes the person's usual conscious responses from the developing felt sense. These are ways of relating to experience, not instructions to remove all troubling feelings before beginning. [The Client's Client](https://focusing.org/gendlin/docs/gol_2149.html).

In a fictional encounter, Adela says that everything feels impossible: a family disagreement, an approaching deadline, and a decision about moving. Whenever she begins one subject, the others arrive as evidence that she cannot cope.

Acknowledging the concerns separately helps her discover that they are related but not identical. The deadline has an immediate practical demand. The move carries uncertainty. The family disagreement is the subject she wants company with today.

At first she imagines putting the other concerns far away. This feels artificial, as though she is pretending they do not matter. A different image fits better: their papers can remain on the table without all being in her hands. She does not need to visualize this precisely; the distinction is enough.

Adela can now approach the disagreement as one concern within a larger day. She finds sadness that was difficult to distinguish from the overall pressure. The other problems remain real and may need action later.

Here space supports more specific contact. If the concerns had become remote or inaccessible, further distancing would have missed the purpose. The fictional outcome is a changed relationship to what is present, rather than a claim that the problems have been cleared away.

#### Internal Family Systems: differentiation that permits relationship with a response

IFS describes blending in terms of a part's feelings and beliefs becoming merged with the Self or another part. Its model outline includes unblending and cautions against approaching vulnerable material before the system is ready. Self and parts are the model's organizing concepts, rather than entities established by this article. [IFS glossary](https://ifs-institute.com/resources/research/ifs-glossary-terms); [model outline](https://ifs-institute.com/resources/articles/internal-family-systems-model-outline).

In this fictional encounter, Reuben receives critical feedback and says, “I am completely useless.” His therapist offers parts language, which Reuben dislikes. He says that the sentence feels true, and calling it a part sounds like a trick to dismiss it.

They use ordinary language instead. The verdict arrives quickly, and Reuben wants to understand why it feels so final. He notices that attending to the actual correction brings fear that he will discover he really cannot do the work.

For a moment, the fear and the verdict are distinguishable. Reuben can be interested in the fear without having settled whether the correction is fair. Then contempt appears: he should be stronger than this. That response also needs room rather than being ordered aside so that he can perform compassion.

The fictional change concerns differentiation while connection remains. The feelings need not become weaker for more perspective to appear. Conversely, sounding detached or repeating “a part of me” would not establish workable contact. A person who already feels remote may need more supported connection, rather than another request to step back.

#### Individual Emotion-Focused Therapy: enough access for productive emotional work

The individual EFT competency framework attends to both over- and under-regulation. It describes assessment of emotional activation and includes moderating as well as heightening within regulation work. Its emphasis is on the quality of emotional processing, rather than intensity alone. [EFT therapist competencies](https://emotionfocusedtherapy.eu/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/FC-EFT-Competence-v1.03-2.pdf).

In a fictional encounter, Hana gives a distant account of a friend's repeated cancellations. She can explain both sides but cannot tell what she wants. A more concrete moment helps: waiting with food ready while reading another last-minute message.

The detail brings sadness and embarrassment. Hana finds the sentence, “I wanted to matter enough to be told sooner.” Her therapist stays with this meaning rather than immediately asking for greater intensity. Hana then becomes absorbed in explaining why her friend should feel guilty, and loses contact with what she needs.

They return to her own sentence. A pause and a less accusatory description make the hurt more available again. She can recognize both disappointment and a wish for a clearer agreement.

The fictional movement includes greater access at one point and a change of focus at another. Regulation is not confined to bringing emotion down. Nor does emotional vividness by itself establish productive work. The relevant development is that Hana can recognize, symbolize, and consider the experience in relation to what matters to her.

#### Raja Selvam's emotional embodiment: varying extent, duration, and integration

Selvam's emotional embodiment seeks greater capacity for emotion through broader bodily experience, supported by regulation and integration. His seven-step account includes brief local work and a pivot toward integration. This has its own rationale; it should not simply be renamed SE pendulation. [Selvam's protocol explanation](https://integralsomaticpsychology.com/protocol-embodying-emotion).

In this fictional illustration, Marta recognizes sadness but experiences it as a tightly concentrated pressure. Remaining focused on that location becomes increasingly difficult. With a practitioner trained in the approach, she explores whether the sadness can be experienced through a somewhat wider area without prolonging attention to the most intense point.

Marta first reports a wider sensation but says that it feels less connected to the sadness. The practitioner does not count greater spatial extent as success. They return briefly to the situation and the particular loss. Marta can distinguish the emotion from a general awareness of her upper body.

Later she notices more ease, and they let the emotional situation recede while attending to that change. She does not need to maintain the sadness to prove that the encounter was genuine. Whether any further cycle is useful remains open.

The fictional example separates extent, intensity, meaning, and tolerability. A wider experience is not necessarily more manageable. Selvam's claims about bodily mechanisms and broad treatment benefits require evidence beyond a person's report that a particular adjustment helped. The point of comparison is flexible contact and capacity, while preserving the approach's distinctive account.

#### Comparing the implementations

| Approach | Particular adjustment | What would make the adjustment useful |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Somatic Experiencing | Work in manageable increments and allow movement around activation. | Charged experience and support can be encountered with more available participation. |
| Sensorimotor Psychotherapy | Change the bodily task, relational arrangement, or form of attention. | The person has more room to notice and choose within a relevant experiment. |
| Focusing | Acknowledge concerns and establish a relationship to one chosen concern. | Space permits more specific contact rather than making the difficulty disappear. |
| Internal Family Systems | Differentiate from a totalizing response without rejecting it. | Perspective and an interested relationship become more available. |
| Individual EFT | Moderate or deepen access according to the processing difficulty. | Emotion can be recognized and worked with meaningfully, rather than merely intensified. |
| Emotional embodiment | Vary bodily extent, duration, and attention to integration. | The emotion remains meaningful while the person's capacity to stay with it is considered. |

The approaches differ in what they attend to and what they propose explains change. A useful adjustment in one does not establish the validity of another's mechanism. The shared concern is whether the person can participate in contact with something that matters.

## A worked example

The following encounter is fictional. It illustrates the editorial pattern through several revisions, rather than presenting a clinical protocol or an expected result.

Noel has not spoken to his brother for two years. Earlier exchanges often began with an apparently simple request and developed into arguments about money and obligation. He does not know what this letter contains. He brings it because having it at home has become difficult to ignore.

His stated purpose is to understand his response before deciding whether to read it. He is interested and apprehensive. His therapist checks that opening the letter is not today's agreed task. Noel agrees, then takes the envelope out of his bag.

The therapist suggests putting it on the table so that they can consider what happens. Noel says yes. Almost immediately, he begins telling the history of the estrangement at speed. His explanation becomes increasingly detailed. When the therapist asks what he is noticing, he answers a different question.

The therapist does not assume that rapid speech establishes overwhelm. They say that they may have made the encounter too immediate and ask whether having the letter visible is helpful. Noel says, “I can't work out whether you're going to ask me to open it.”

The earlier clarification was not enough. Once the envelope appeared, Noel imagined a progression: put it down, look at it, discuss it, open it. Declining the last step seemed like failing to complete what he had begun.

The therapist acknowledges that their suggestion could have implied that direction. They make clear that the letter can remain closed and ask whether Noel wants it on the table, in his hand, or back in the bag. He chooses the bag. Removing the visible object changes one condition, but he is still caught up in explaining why the estrangement was necessary.

They pause the account. The therapist asks what would be useful to understand today, now that no reading is expected. Noel says, “Why it feels as if he's already in the room asking for something.”

The task becomes more specific. It concerns the effect of receiving contact, rather than the whole relationship or the unknown contents of the letter. Noel can stay with that distinction briefly, then feels an urgent wish to throw the envelope away.

The therapist suggests noticing something supportive in the room or in a memory. Noel thinks of his grandmother's kitchen, then becomes more distressed. His brother was there too. A pleasant memory in one respect is not a separate place of safety in another.

The therapist stops pursuing the image. They do not ask Noel to search harder for a positive resource. They return to ordinary conversation about what just happened. Noel says that he was trying to provide the kind of memory the therapist wanted, even though it did not help.

That correction matters. The adjustment had created another small obligation. The therapist acknowledges this and asks whether a more practical form of support would be useful: clarifying the time remaining and keeping the next part of the discussion limited to one question. Noel wants both.

There is enough time to continue briefly and still leave room to finish. They choose the question of what the letter seems to require before it has been read. Noel says, “An answer.” He notices pressure in his chest, but does not want to concentrate on the sensation. He wants to find the rule behind it.

They stay with his words. Receiving something from his brother feels like entering an agreement he did not choose. Noel can now describe that meaning without recounting every earlier conflict. He is upset, but he can hear the therapist and correct their reflection.

The therapist says, “So you don't want to hear from him.” Noel says that this is too definite. Part of him does want to know how his brother is. What he does not want is for curiosity to become a commitment to help, argue, or resume regular contact.

The amount of contact has become more workable partly because its meaning is more accurate. The task is no longer choosing between opening himself to everything and refusing all interest. Noel can acknowledge curiosity while keeping the eventual decision open.

A suggestion to imagine reading the first line is unnecessary now. Noel notices that he is beginning to treat the session as practice for a harder test. He says that he would rather remain with the distinction they have found. The therapist accepts the limit without presenting it as a temporary retreat.

For a short time, Noel notices both apprehension and a little relief. Then he becomes tired. The therapist asks whether he wants to continue discussing the letter or turn toward ending. He chooses the ending. No final return to activation is needed to complete a pendulation cycle.

They review what is actually known. The letter is still unread. Its contents may include a request, an apology, ordinary news, or something else. None of those possibilities has been established by Noel's bodily response. His earlier experiences make the apprehension understandable while leaving the present facts uncertain.

The practical question is what to do with the envelope tonight. Noel decides to keep it in a drawer instead of carrying it around or leaving it visible. This is an arrangement he prefers for now, not an assignment to avoid the subject or a promise to read it later. He can reconsider with more information or support.

At the end, Noel says that the useful difference was being able to take an interest without entering a contract. He has not become calm about his brother, and the relationship has not been repaired. He has approached a meaningful part of his response while discovering that the form and stopping point can be changed.

## Why this movement may help

An encounter that is too demanding can leave little room for discrimination. A person may become occupied with getting through it, anticipating what comes next, or producing the expected response. An adjustment can make distinctions available again: one feeling from another, an image from a fact, a wish from an obligation, or an invitation from a demand.

When the encounter is too remote, a different adjustment may help. A particular detail can make an abstract account recognizable. A more accurate reflection can connect an explanation with what it means to the person. The relevant movement is toward usable contact, whichever direction that requires.

Changing the form of an encounter can also reveal what made it difficult. Noel's reaction was affected by the envelope's visibility, but also by an imagined requirement to open it. Putting the letter away helped partly; clarifying that the task could end elsewhere helped differently. Treating the difficulty only as excessive activation would have missed that meaning.

Choice matters because it changes the conditions of participation. A person who can decline a question, change position, or stop an experiment is doing more than enduring an intervention. Yet these options need to be credible in practice. A stated permission to stop loses meaning if every pause is followed by pressure to resume.

These are experiential and practical rationales. They do not establish a universal optimal arousal level or a mechanism in which emotion must be released in measured quantities. The metaphor of a window of tolerance can describe a workable range, but it is not a directly observed gauge that assigns a correct dose to every encounter.

The evidence needs to remain specific. Brom and colleagues' randomized study reported benefits of a full SE treatment compared with a waitlist. It does not isolate titration, pendulation, or this broader editorial pattern, and a waitlist comparison does not establish superiority to another active treatment. [SE outcome study](https://traumahealing.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/06/Somatic-Experiencing-for-Posttraumatic-Stress-Disorder-2017.pdf).

A different trial provides an important qualification. Van Vliet and colleagues compared EMDR preceded by STAIR skills training with immediate EMDR for adults with PTSD following childhood abuse. They found no significant advantage for the phase-based condition in the measured outcomes at post-treatment or follow-up. The study challenges a universal requirement for a separate preparatory phase; it does not settle every person's treatment needs or remove the need for assessment and responsive pacing. [Phase-based versus immediate trauma-focused treatment](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8612023/).

The clinical sources document how the six approaches conceptualize and adjust contact. They do not jointly validate this editorial synthesis. The fictional examples demonstrate distinctions, not treatment effectiveness. Practical workability in one encounter and lasting therapeutic benefit remain different claims.

## Conditions, pitfalls, and limits

### Workable does not mean comfortable throughout

A person can remain meaningfully engaged while feeling intense sadness, anger, fear, or shame. Rapidly interrupting every rise in emotion can communicate that the feeling is dangerous or unwelcome. The therapist needs to consider participation and purpose alongside intensity.

At the same time, willingness to endure is not proof that an encounter is useful. Someone may be following instructions while losing the ability to consider or influence what happens. Neither calm appearance nor determination settles the question.

### Do not infer capacity from a single outward sign

Speech, movement, gaze, tears, stillness, and changes in breathing have multiple possible meanings. Becoming quiet does not necessarily indicate regulation. Becoming more expressive does not necessarily indicate overwhelm.

The person's account, continuity of engagement, practical context, and changes over time belong in the judgment. Fatigue, pain, sensory differences, language, and medical factors may also affect presentation. The pattern does not turn bodily observations into a diagnosis.

### Keep distance distinct from disconnection

More distance can create perspective. It can also leave someone further from an experience they already find difficult to feel. Words such as “observe it” or “step back” do not identify the same process in every person.

When someone reports unreality, significant detachment, or difficulty following, adding distance or inward attention may be unhelpful. The work needs appropriate clinical judgment and may need to stop or change form. Such responses should not be praised automatically as successful unblending or calm.

### Let a resource prove useful through its actual effect

A chair, memory, bodily area, image, or relationship is not inherently regulating. A pleasant memory may include loss; physical support may feel trapping; attention to breathing may increase self-consciousness. The proposed support has to be encountered, not presumed.

A failed resource is information about this suggestion in this moment. It does not establish that the person lacks resources or is resisting help. Practical clarification, ordinary conversation, environmental changes, and a decision to finish can all be relevant alternatives.

### Avoid both compulsory escalation and endless preparation

A series of small steps can become an implied obligation to reach a larger intervention. The person may consent to the first while fearing that later refusal will disappoint the therapist. The destination should remain negotiable rather than hidden inside the pacing.

Preparation can also continue long after it serves a useful purpose. A person should not have to become perfectly regulated before accessing appropriate treatment. Moment-to-moment responsiveness and a mandatory separate stabilization phase are different claims, as the treatment comparison above makes clear.

### Distinguish helpful flexibility from a pattern of avoiding the chosen work

A pause may restore participation, reveal a different task, or close an encounter appropriately. Repeatedly leaving every meaningful point can also prevent work the person actually wants. The pattern should allow this possibility to be discussed without turning every limit into pathology.

The question concerns function over time: are the adjustments helping the person approach what matters, or has the work become organized around never encountering it? That question needs collaboration and clinical understanding. It cannot be answered by counting how often someone looks away or changes subject.

### Do not make the person responsible for perfect monitoring

Someone may not recognize a limit until it has been crossed. They may find it difficult to interrupt an authority figure, or discover only later that an exercise was unhelpful. Saying “You should have told me” shifts too much responsibility onto them.

The therapist remains responsible for noticing, checking, explaining, and revising interventions. Feedback after the event can still change future work. An agreed signal can help, but it is not a contract making all unreported discomfort the person's fault.

### Include context, risk, and practical needs

Workability depends partly on circumstances beyond the session. Privacy, ongoing danger, limited support, responsibilities after the meeting, and physical health can affect what is appropriate. Adjusting sensations cannot substitute for addressing an urgent practical need.

A manageable representation is also not proof of safety in an actual encounter. Imagining contact with an estranged relative does not establish that real contact would be wise. The experiential account and the external decision both need their own consideration.

### Let the ending be an ending

A person need not return to charged material once more to demonstrate flexibility, finish a cycle, or earn the right to stop. Enough may already have been approached. There may also be no useful contact that day.

An ending can acknowledge uncertainty and what remains open without manufacturing relief. The question is whether the transition is understandable and appropriately supported, rather than whether every session concludes in a calm or positive state.

## Its place in the wider process

Find a Workable Amount of Contact comes early in the proposed reading order because every later pattern depends on a usable way of participating. It remains relevant throughout the work. Greater contact may become possible, or the task may need to become smaller, different, more supported, or complete for now.

[[let-contrasting-experiences-meet|Let Contrasting Experiences Meet]] depends on enough access to the relevant experiences without turning comparison into forced immersion.

[[let-the-change-register|Let the Change Register]] can notice a modest but meaningful development: asking to pause, correcting the task, or recognizing that curiosity does not require action. [[find-the-next-living-expression|Find the Next Living Expression]] considers a continuation that fits what has emerged and the person's actual conditions.

The earlier method-oriented articles give fuller attention to [[titration|Titration]], [[pendulation|Pendulation]], [[clearing-a-space|Clearing a Space]], and [[unblending|Unblending]]. [[orienting|Orienting]] can help restore contact with the current setting. [[embodying-emotions|Embodying Emotions]] develops Selvam's distinctive use of bodily expansion, regulation, and integration. These remain specific expressions and neighboring methods within the broader comparison.

For Noel, workable contact did not mean reading a smaller piece of the letter. It meant approaching a more accurate question under conditions he could influence. Another person's next step might involve closer or longer contact. The pattern leaves that direction open to what the encounter reveals.

## Expressions and sources

**Find a Workable Amount of Contact** is an editorial name for a recurring concern across experiential approaches. It does not identify a standardized protocol, a numerical arousal target, or a single biological mechanism. Titration, pendulation, differentiation, clearing a space, emotional regulation, and embodiment preserve their distinct lineages and purposes.

All encounters are original fictional illustrations. They are not adapted clinical cases, records of actual treatment, or evidence for outcomes. The article explains the pattern without providing a guided practice or interactive exercise.

The main sources are:

- **Somatic Experiencing International, *Glossary of Terms* (2021 revision).** Approach-specific definitions of titration, pendulation, orientation, and resourcing. Used for terminology while keeping the model's proposed physiological explanations distinct from observed experience. [Read the glossary](https://traumahealing.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/04/A-Glossary-of-SE-Terms-rev-4.24.21-1.pdf).
- **Pat Ogden, *Trauma, Dissociation and the Body: A Sensorimotor Psychotherapy Approach* (2023).** Primary teaching material describing directed mindfulness, manageable actions, relational adjustments, and client control of physical interventions. [Read the handout](https://sensorimotorpsychotherapy.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/Trauma-Dissociation-Body-Handout.pdf).
- **Eugene T. Gendlin, “The Client's Client: The Edge of Awareness” (1984).** Primary discussion of making a space and a responsive relationship with felt experience. [Read the chapter](https://focusing.org/gendlin/docs/gol_2149.html).
- **IFS Institute, “IFS Glossary of Terms” and “The Internal Family Systems Model Outline.”** Primary descriptions of the model's vocabulary, blending, differentiation, and cautions about proceeding. The model's concepts are not presented here as a diagnosis or literal architecture established by the examples. [Read the glossary](https://ifs-institute.com/resources/research/ifs-glossary-terms); [read the outline](https://ifs-institute.com/resources/articles/internal-family-systems-model-outline).
- **Robert Elliott, Lars Auszra, Imke Herrmann, Carla Cunha, and the EmpoweringEFT@EU team, *Therapist Competencies for Emotion-Focused Therapy Practice* (2021).** Framework addressing over- and under-regulation, emotional activation, moderating, and heightening. Specifies clinical competencies rather than an independently tested dose of emotion. [Read the framework](https://emotionfocusedtherapy.eu/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/FC-EFT-Competence-v1.03-2.pdf).
- **Raja Selvam, “The 7-Step Protocol for Embodying Emotion” (2023).** Primary account of brief local emotional work, wider embodiment, and attention to integration. Used to explain the approach rather than endorse its broad outcome claims. [Read Selvam's account](https://integralsomaticpsychology.com/protocol-embodying-emotion).
- **Danny Brom and colleagues, “Somatic Experiencing for Posttraumatic Stress Disorder: A Randomized Controlled Outcome Study” (2017).** Randomized comparison of a full SE treatment with a waitlist, relevant to the distinction between evidence for a treatment and evidence for an isolated component. [Read the study](https://traumahealing.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/06/Somatic-Experiencing-for-Posttraumatic-Stress-Disorder-2017.pdf).
- **Noortje I. van Vliet and colleagues, “Phase-Based Treatment Versus Immediate Trauma-Focused Treatment for Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder Due to Childhood Abuse: Randomised Clinical Trial” (2021).** Comparison of STAIR followed by EMDR with immediate EMDR. Challenges a universal preparatory-phase requirement while leaving individual assessment and applicability to other populations open. [Read the study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8612023/).
