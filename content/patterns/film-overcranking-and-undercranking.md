*Choose how much screen time an action receives.*

A dancer’s leap lasts a fraction of a second, but the filmmaker wants us to study its shape. A miniature building collapses convincingly, yet its debris falls too quickly to suggest a large structure. A comic chase needs a burst of energy beyond what the performers can produce naturally.

All three problems involve the relationship between **how quickly images are recorded and how quickly they are shown**.

Record more images during an action, then play them more slowly, and the action expands. Record fewer images, then play them faster, and it contracts.

These are the principles behind **overcranking** and **undercranking**—names inherited from cameras whose operators advanced the film with a hand crank.

## The opening puzzle: why does running the camera faster make the action slower?

Suppose a jump takes one second.

A camera recording at 48 frames per second captures 48 pictures of that jump. If the finished film shows those pictures at 24 frames per second, it takes two seconds to display them.

The performer jumped normally. The recorded images give us twice as long to watch.

Reverse the relationship: record twelve pictures during that second, then show them at 24 frames per second. The jump now occupies half a second.

**Overcranking means capturing faster than the intended playback rate. Undercranking means capturing slower.** RED’s camera documentation explicitly distinguishes recording frame rate from the project’s playback time base. [RED: recording frame rate](https://docs.red.com/955-0225/955-0225_V1.7%20Rev-A%20RED%20PS%2C%20V-RAPTOR%20%5BX%5D%208K%20VV%20Operation%20Guide%20HTML/Content/4_Menus/b_ProjSet/RecFrameRate.htm)

For playback at 24 frames per second:

| Capture rate | Apparent movement | One second of action occupies |
|---|---|---|
| 12 fps | Twice normal speed | 0.5 seconds |
| 24 fps | Normal speed | 1 second |
| 48 fps | Half normal speed | 2 seconds |
| 120 fps | One-fifth normal speed | 5 seconds |

These examples assume every captured frame is shown once, with no additional retiming.

High-frame-rate recording does not automatically create slow motion. Footage recorded and shown at the same rate retains the event’s original duration.

### Puzzle one: how does a sword stroke become an extended visual event?

In Zack Snyder’s *300*, moments of combat expand so that a body’s trajectory or a weapon’s movement becomes unusually readable.

Cinematographer Larry Fong describes regular-speed and high-speed units working simultaneously, with high-speed photography reserved for selected actions. The production used rates including 50, 75, and 100 fps, with a Photo-Sonics camera reaching 360 fps for particularly high-speed work. [American Cinematographer: Fong on photographing *300*](https://theasc.com/article/few-against-many-300/)

Recording additional stages of a movement supplies material that can be spread across a longer screen interval.

The effect can serve several purposes: clarify choreography, prolong anticipation, emphasize an impact, or make a fleeting pose feel sculptural.

The final rhythm also depends on editing. Moving between ordinary speed and stretched time tells the audience which parts of the action deserve special attention.

### Puzzle two: how does a small collapsing building acquire convincing weight?

A miniature can reproduce a building’s appearance while revealing its size through movement.

Debris falling a short physical distance reaches the ground quickly. Shown at ordinary speed, that collapse may feel like a model falling apart.

Overcranking stretches the photographed movement, helping the short fall suggest a much larger one.

For Guillermo del Toro’s *Frankenstein* (2025), the effects team built miniature versions of Victor’s laboratory and photographed their destruction. VFX supervisor Dennis Berardi reports that the quarter-scale laboratory interior was shot at around **144 fps**.

The team explored frame rates through tests, including how fire and water behaved. [American Cinematographer: *Frankenstein*’s miniature photography and production gallery](https://theasc.com/article/frankenstein-miniature-cinematography/)

Here the finished effect may be intended to read as an ordinary full-size event. Slow playback compensates for the miniature’s physical scale.

That compensation has limits. Water droplets, flame behaviour, materials, and the way a model breaks also influence its apparent size. Frame rate is one part of the construction.

### Puzzle three: how can a comic boxing match move with impossible precision?

In *City Lights* (1931), Chaplin and his opponent move around the referee in a tightly coordinated boxing routine.

Undercranking allows rehearsed actions to become quicker on screen. Performers can execute complicated business at a manageable pace, with playback supplying additional speed.

Silent-film specialist Ben Model identifies a sequence from the routine as having been photographed at **16 fps**, based on filming records he examined. At 24 fps playback, that produces movement one-and-a-half times as fast.

His practical demonstrations also reveal the performance problem: everyone in the image must account for the speed change. A performer who should appear relatively ordinary may need to move more slowly during filming. [Model’s analysis and filmed demonstrations](https://silentfilmmusic.com/undercranking-parallel-exit-4/)

Undercranking therefore works with choreography. It can give a carefully arranged action the apparent spontaneity of a frantic scramble.

Model explores the technique further in Criterion’s [*A Study in Undercranking*](https://www.criterionchannel.com/supplements/bPgWD5Zr/a-study-in-undercranking).

Historical projection speeds also matter. Some accelerated-looking silent footage is being shown faster than intended; Chaplin’s official screening guidance discusses restorations using different original speeds. [Chaplin Film Concerts: projection-speed considerations](https://cineconcert.charliechaplin.com/faq)

## Frame rate and shutter exposure are separate controls

Frame rate determines how frequently images are captured. Exposure time determines how much movement each individual image records.

A short exposure produces relatively crisp moving edges. A longer exposure records more motion blur.

They interact. With a 180-degree shutter setting, each frame is exposed for half of its recording interval. Increasing capture speed therefore shortens each exposure:

- At 24 fps, exposure lasts approximately **1/48 second**.
- At 48 fps, exposure lasts approximately **1/96 second**.
- At 120 fps, exposure lasts approximately **1/240 second**.

Consequently, overcranking at a fixed shutter angle gives each frame less light. Maintaining the desired exposure requires compensation elsewhere. [RED: shutter angle and exposure-time relationships](https://docs.red.com/955-0196/955-0196_V1.7%20Rev-B%20RED%20PS%2C%20KOMODO%20Operation%20Guide%20HTML/Content/4_Menus/01_Image_LUT/Shutter.htm)

Undercranking can permit longer exposures and more blur, but that appearance depends on the shutter settings chosen. Fast motion does not have one compulsory texture.

## Why capture the extra frames instead of slowing ordinary footage later?
A one-second recording at 24 fps contains 24 photographed stages of the event.

Stretch it to five seconds and those stages must somehow fill 120 displayed frames. The editor can repeat frames, blend them, or generate intermediate images.

A 120 fps recording already contains 120 photographed stages from that second. It supplies actual observations of changes that a lower-rate recording never captured.

Interpolation can still be useful, but overlapping objects, fine details, and newly revealed surfaces can make its reconstruction difficult.

## What needs planning?
The whole recorded image changes speed: performers, camera movement, background activity, smoke, and clothing.

An undercranked chase may become conspicuous because pedestrians in the background suddenly hurry. An overcranked camera move may feel unexpectedly sluggish.

Lighting also deserves testing. High-speed capture can reveal flicker that was unobtrusive at another rate. [RED’s flicker guide](https://www.red.com/red-101/flicker-free-video-tutorial) explains the interaction between lighting pulses, exposure, and frame rate.

Sound must fit the altered timing too, particularly where dialogue or musical synchronization matters.

## How it relates to neighbouring tricks
**Speed ramps** change the relationship between action time and screen time during a passage, allowing movement into and out of slow motion.

**Time-lapse** extends the undercranking principle to widely separated exposures, compressing long processes.

**[Bullet time](/pattern/film-bullet-time)** separates viewpoint movement from the progression of the action. Ordinary overcranking slows the photographed camera movement along with the subject.

**[Miniature photography](/pattern/film-miniatures)** uses frame rate to help physical models suggest the movement and weight of larger objects.
