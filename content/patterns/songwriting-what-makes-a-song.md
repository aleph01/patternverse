**Pattern 1 of 49 · Part 1: Start Making Songs**

**Use when:** A draft has several attractive parts, but adding or removing a layer makes it unclear what the song is about musically.

Someone hums a tune at the bus stop. Another person recognizes it before a single word has been sung. Elsewhere, the opening drum pattern identifies a track before its singer arrives. A song can leave different kinds of memory: a melodic shape, a rhythmic gesture, a particular sound, a line of language, or the moment a familiar section returns.

## The move

Identify the relationship that makes the song recognizable and preserve it while other material changes.

Compare two versions with one part removed. In Porch Light, retain the E–G–A seed and its one–one–two rhythm while simplifying the accompaniment. In Platform Three, retain the bass timing and its gap while changing the upper sound. Ask what relationship disappears, rather than which instrument is missing. Keep that relationship audible in the next version. This identifies the material to protect; choosing one foreground hook is a later, narrower decision.

Identity can live in a groove or a sound as well as a tune; a stripped vocal performance is not a universal test.

## Hear jobs rather than equipment

There is no single ingredient list that separates all songs from all other music. For this course, a song is an organized musical experience that gives a listener something to follow through time. Most of our examples involve a voice and words; some explore how instrumental material can develop with similar care.

Imagine an original recording with a soft kick drum, a low repeated note, keyboard chords, and a voice. The drum helps establish pulse and rhythmic character. The low note helps orient the harmony and contributes its own rhythm. The chords frame the melody. The voice carries a phrase and a speaker's perspective.

These are roles, not permanent assignments. A guitar can supply the rhythmic drive. A bass can carry the most memorable melody. A voice can act as a rhythmic texture rather than deliver a narrative. The first useful distinction is between what a part is played on and what it contributes.

Remove the keyboard from this imagined recording. The vocal and bass may still imply the harmonic setting. Remove the drum, and the repeated vocal rhythm may preserve a pulse. Remove every accompaniment part, and the song may remain recognizable in a solo voice. This does not prove that arrangement is unimportant. It reveals which relationships currently carry the identity.

## Meet Porch Light

Our first example begins as a small melodic seed, E4–G4–A4. The numbers identify octaves: C4 is middle C in this course. Its rhythm lasts four quarter-note beats: one beat on E, one on G, two on A. At this point it is a fragment, not a finished chorus.

The fragment eventually belongs to Porch Light, a song about returning to a shared home and learning that welcome does not remove responsibility. Its ordinary musical setting is C major, four beats per bar, at 84 quarter notes per minute. These names become clearer in later patterns. For now, they are labels that let us keep track of the same example.

The verse uses C, Am, F, and G, each for two bars. The central request is “Leave the porch light on.” The song's development will involve what those words mean before and after the second verse. Its emotional change cannot be supplied by a chord chart alone.

## Meet a different kind of continuity

Platform Three begins with a D-centered bass pulse and short instrumental figures. Its identity is initially rhythmic and textural. A higher repeating line enters later; the bass becomes less busy during a thinner middle passage. The return is recognizable even without a sung chorus.

This second example prevents one workflow from becoming a rule. It also makes comparisons possible. Where Porch Light can change through a new verse, Platform Three may change through register, timing, or the removal of a part. Both need decisions about what remains and what develops.

## A small idea can hold a large piece together

Beethoven's **Symphony No. 5, first movement** gives an instrumental example of identity surviving change. The [Boston Symphony's work notes](https://www.bso.org/works/beethoven-symphony-no-5) introduce the work and its recurring opening idea. For a named performance, use the [hr-Sinfonieorchester with Philippe Herreweghe](https://www.hr-sinfonieorchester.de/media/videos/konzert-videos-20-21/beethoven-5-sinfonie,video-beethoven-sinfonie-5-herreweghe-100.html), first movement. Hear the opening's three short attacks and longer arrival, then notice how that rhythmic relationship remains recognizable when the surrounding music grows busier. The identity does not depend on replaying the opening at the same volume, pitch, or orchestral weight every time.

This is a symphonic movement rather than a song-form template. Its useful lesson for a songwriter is smaller: a distinctive relationship can connect passages that otherwise differ greatly. In Porch Light, the seed's rhythm can pass from the voice to an instrumental answer while the harmony changes. In Platform Three, the bass's placement and gap can do similar work. Protecting that relationship allows the arrangement to develop without requiring every original layer to remain. The work notes provide context; the orchestra page provides the performance.

## A song is more than its inventory

Listing a song's chords, instruments, and tempo is a useful description, but it does not explain the relationships between them. Four familiar chords can support many different songs. Two songs with the same instruments may feel unrelated. A single repeated note may become expressive through timing and context.

The course therefore follows decisions: which idea to repeat, when to let it rest, what the next line reveals, how the bass supports a phrase, and why the ending occurs where it does. Technical vocabulary should make those decisions easier to hear and discuss.

The examples focus on several related songwriting traditions, not every musical practice. Standard notation is optional. The [[songwriting-example-songbook|original songbook]] preserves the examples, and the [[songwriting-glossary|glossary]] supplies quick definitions as they appear. Ableton's [Learning Music introduction](https://learningmusic.ableton.com/) offers a complementary view of how musical layers combine; our course follows their development into original songs.
