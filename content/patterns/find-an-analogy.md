### Borrowing a clearer relationship

A designer tells a client that a page cannot give equal emphasis to every item. The client hears this as a refusal to acknowledge the importance of their work. Explaining visual hierarchy again does little to help. Then the designer compares the page to a meeting in which everyone speaks at the same volume at the same time. Each person may have something worthwhile to say, but the listener still needs an order in which to hear it. The comparison makes a relationship visible that the technical explanation had left abstract.

Robert Dilts's Analogy pattern in Sleight of Mouth uses a similar relationship in another context to reconsider a belief. The value of an analogy lies in the structure it carries across domains. In this example, competing visual elements and competing speakers both create a problem of allocating attention. The comparison becomes useful because the client understands the second situation well enough to reason about the first through it.

Analogies can loosen a conclusion that has become tied to the familiar language of a particular problem. A person may find it difficult to reconsider a belief directly because every term in the discussion already carries a settled implication. Another domain introduces a different set of expectations. It can make a neglected distinction easier to grasp, even when the original facts remain unchanged.

### Similarities that do explanatory work

Suppose a team believes that a good plan should never need revision. A useful comparison is a route chosen before a journey. Planning helps decide where to go and anticipate obstacles, but a closed road gives new information. Revising the route can be evidence that navigation is working. The team can use this analogy to distinguish abandoning a destination from updating the means of reaching it. That distinction allows a revised plan to be evaluated on its merits instead of being treated automatically as evidence of incompetence.

The relevant connection is specific: both planning and navigation involve choosing actions before all conditions are known. Other features of travel do not necessarily transfer. A business plan may involve obligations to people who cannot easily take another route, and changing it may require negotiation. The analogy becomes more useful when these limits are named. It gives a model of updating under uncertainty, while leaving the actual costs of updating available for analysis.

A creative example shows the same selectivity. Someone expects every writing session to produce a finished passage and interprets exploratory work as wasted time. Comparing drafting to a musician's rehearsal can reveal that preparation has outputs other than performance. The writer may discover rhythm, test a voice, or identify a weak transition without producing publishable text. This does not mean that writing and music are identical, or that indefinite rehearsal is always productive. The comparison clarifies one relationship between preparation and finished work.

### A vivid comparison can outrun its accuracy

An analogy can feel persuasive long before its structural fit has been examined. Calling a budget a household budget, an organization a family, or a mind a computer brings familiar expectations into an unfamiliar situation. Some may help; others may mislead. A family analogy, for example, can encourage care while also making professional boundaries seem disloyal. The image's emotional appeal is separate from its explanatory adequacy.

This is why the comparison should be treated as a model with a limited job. The designer's meeting analogy explains competing demands on attention. It does not establish the exact size of a heading or the correct order of every item. Those choices still require inspection of the page, its users, and its purpose. When an analogy becomes the evidence for every subsequent claim, the original situation can vanish beneath the borrowed story.

Finding a second analogy can expose what the first one leaves out. A plan might be a route, but it might also resemble an agreement among travelers. The first image highlights adaptation; the second highlights obligations and coordination. Their differences help the team see which aspect it is currently neglecting. Several partial models can be more informative than one compelling metaphor that appears to explain everything.

### Returning to the original situation

Find an Analogy is useful when direct explanation has become repetitive or a relationship remains hard to picture. Chunk Up can identify the general relationship that the comparison needs to preserve. Try Another Model of the World can provide an unfamiliar interpretation without necessarily borrowing another domain. Inspect How You Know helps distinguish the insight produced by an analogy from evidence that supports a conclusion about the actual case.

The best result is a clearer question about the original situation. The client can ask how the page should sequence attention. The team can ask which parts of the destination remain stable and which parts of the route require updating. The writer can distinguish useful exploration from avoidance. The analogy has done its work when the reader can return to the concrete problem and see a relationship that was previously difficult to name.

## Pattern details

### Use when

- Direct explanation keeps repeating without understanding.
- An abstract relationship is hard to picture.

### Goals

- Make a neglected relationship understandable.
- Return to the original case with a more useful question.

### Mechanisms

- relational mapping
- model comparison

### Not ideal when

- A vivid metaphor is being substituted for evidence about the actual case.

### Difficulty

easy
