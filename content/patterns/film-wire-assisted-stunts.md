*Give a real performer an invisible source of support and movement.*

A fighter leaps across a courtyard, stays airborne longer than seems possible, and lands lightly. Someone struck in a fight flies backward across a room. An astronaut drifts through a corridor, turning without touching the floor.

These actions present a shared filmmaking problem: **the body needs to move in a way that ordinary strength, momentum, and gravity will not produce.**

Wires let filmmakers reshape that movement while photographing a real person. A harness connects the performer to a rig that can support, lift, pull, rotate, or control their descent. Camera placement and visual effects then conceal the assistance.

The attraction is the combination: an extraordinary trajectory with the detail of a photographed performance—expressions, moving clothes, changing light, and interaction with the set.

## The puzzle: how can a leap last that long?

![Carrie-Anne Moss is suspended on wires for Trinity’s rooftop leap.](https://theasc.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/Matrix-667.jpg)

*The Matrix (1999) — Behind the scenes.* Carrie-Anne Moss is suspended on wires for Trinity’s rooftop leap. The support extends the trajectory; framing, editing, and cleanup deliver the apparently unaided movement. [Image source](https://theasc.com/article/flashback-the-matrix-cinematography/). Credit: Production image published by American Cinematographer.


Imagine a performer appearing to cross a courtyard in a single extended bound.

An ordinary jump follows a trajectory determined largely by its launch. Once airborne, the jumper has limited control over where their body travels.

With wire assistance, an external force continues acting during the leap. The rig can support part of the performer’s weight and carry them farther than their takeoff would allow. The performer contributes the recognizable action: pushing off, arranging the body in flight, reaching toward a destination, and preparing to land.

Remove the visible support, and the movement appears to originate entirely from the character.

**The central trick is to preserve convincing bodily action while changing the forces acting on it.**

Wires can contribute very different things to a scene:

- **Extend a movement.** A jump travels farther, rises higher, or ends with an unusually gentle landing.
- **Create an impact reaction.** A controlled pull supplies the acceleration of someone being thrown or blasted backward.
- **Support flight or floating.** The performer remains suspended while changing pose or moving through the set.
- **Control a descent.** The rig shapes the apparent fall and its stopping point.
- **Protect a performer.** A safety connection secures someone during a physical stunt, without necessarily supplying the movement that viewers notice.

That last distinction matters. A person attached to wires might be performing an extraordinary action themselves. Knowing that wires were present does not, by itself, explain which parts of the action they created.

### Example: *Crouching Tiger, Hidden Dragon* (2000)—how can people run across rooftops as though they weigh almost nothing?

During the rooftop pursuit, the characters take enormous strides and cross gaps with a flowing lightness. Their movements establish a world in which martial skill includes a kind of freedom from ordinary gravity.

Cinematographer Peter Pau described the performers being suspended from a construction crane while a separate camera crane followed them. Changes in recording speed also helped refine the rhythm of their steps.

The camera’s movement contributes strongly: it accompanies the performers through the space, making the flight feel like an unfolding encounter rather than an isolated trick. [American Cinematographer’s production account explains the rooftop work and its choreography.](https://theasc.com/article/crouching-tiger-hidden-dragon-cinematography/)

The bamboo fight added a different difficulty. Removing wires meant restoring the fine leaves and branches they crossed. Pau described that delicate reconstruction in a separate interview. The physical performance and the invisible cleanup were both substantial parts of the illusion. [Read the illustrated interview with Pau.](https://www.livedesignonline.com/business-people-news/crouching-tiger-hidden-dragon-behind-scenes-dp-peter-pau)

### Example: *The Matrix* (1999)—how can a fight move like an impossible dance?

The fighters leap, turn, and redirect their bodies with a precision and grace that exceed ordinary combat.

Action choreographer Yuen Woo-ping brought wire-harness work into the choreography alongside extensive martial-arts training. American Cinematographer’s original coverage includes a production photograph of Carrie-Anne Moss suspended during a rooftop leap, and explains how the filmmakers wanted wire work to give the action its heightened quality. [See the production report and photographs.](https://theasc.com/article/flashback-the-matrix-cinematography/)

The reusable idea is broader than “make someone jump higher.” Assistance can alter the quality of an entire movement: how quickly a body rises, how long a turn unfolds, or how lightly someone seems to recover.

Training remains visible in the result. The performer must make the assisted action read as intentional, maintaining posture, gaze, and coordination throughout.

### Example: *Inception* (2010)—how can someone float along a hotel corridor?

The hotel sequence combines several physical methods. One corridor set rotated, allowing the apparent direction of gravity to change during the fight. A duplicate corridor stood vertically, with the camera looking upward and performers moving on wires.

From that viewpoint, movement through a vertical structure becomes movement along an apparently horizontal hallway. American Cinematographer documents both versions, along with a third approach using a removable trolley rig. [See the set photographs and production explanation.](https://theasc.com/article/inception-cinematography-pfister-nolan/)

This reveals a powerful variation: **change the relationship between the performer, the set, and the camera so that an achievable movement suggests a different physical situation.**

The final images also required cleanup. DNEG’s effects team described removing wires and reconstructing parts of Joseph Gordon-Levitt’s pinstriped clothing where the rig obscured it. [Their retrospective explains that work.](https://www.inverse.com/entertainment/behind-the-scenes-visual-effects-in-inception)

### Example: *Mission: Impossible—Rogue Nation* (2015)—which part of the airplane stunt do the wires do?

When Ethan Hunt clings to the outside of the airborne transport plane, Tom Cruise really was photographed on the aircraft. Safety cables secured him; visual effects artists removed them and altered the surrounding landscape.

A later moment uses assistance differently. Hunt and the payload are apparently pulled out of the cargo hold. Cruise performed that movement on a studio setup, where a mechanical pull accelerated him backward. The aircraft interior was reconstructed around the photographed action.

One sequence therefore demonstrates two roles: securing a performer during location filming, and generating a dramatic movement for a composite shot. In the linked report’s plane-stunt featurette, watch the visible attachment during filming, then compare the finished flight shots. The shot of Hunt being pulled out with the payload demonstrates a different job: producing the movement itself. [FXGuide’s interview with visual effects supervisor David Vickery explains both, with behind-the-scenes footage.](https://www.fxguide.com/fxfeatured/hold-on-the-vfx-of-rogue-nation/)

## Why the body can still betray the support
Deleting the wires does not automatically make the movement convincing.

A suspended person may hang from a noticeable pivot point. Their torso might rise before their legs react, or they may swing like a pendulum when the story calls for purposeful flight. Clothing can gather around the harness. A landing can lack the weight the preceding movement leads us to expect.

The appropriate movement depends on the scene. Dreamlike martial arts can embrace floating grace. A grounded thriller may need a sharp acceleration and an awkward recovery. Weightlessness needs a different rhythm again.

The rig, choreography, performance, camera, and playback speed must support the same physical impression.

## Where do the wires go?
Sometimes framing keeps them outside the picture, or the performer’s body hides them from the chosen angle. Otherwise, effects artists reconstruct the image beneath them.

That replacement might come from nearby pixels, another moment in the recording, or separately photographed scenery. Complex shots can require rebuilding a larger area of the background.

The repair must hold together in motion. A patch that looks convincing in a still image can flicker or slide during playback. Grain, blur, lighting, and moving edges all need to remain consistent. [FXGuide’s detailed discussion of wire removal explains these difficulties.](https://www.fxguide.com/fxfeatured/the_art_of_wire_removal/)

## When the pattern helps—and what it costs
Wire assistance is especially useful when a scene benefits from a recognizable performer interacting physically with the environment, while the required trajectory exceeds unaided movement.

It also introduces constraints. Rigging can limit camera positions and movement; harnesses affect clothing; repeated takes demand coordination and physical effort. Specialist stunt and rigging teams develop and rehearse the action around the performer and the shot.

For some movements, an animatronic, a digital double, a different set orientation, or an editorial construction may be more effective. Films often combine these methods within one sequence.
