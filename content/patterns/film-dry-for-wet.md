*Create the experience of being underwater while filming in air.*

A character floats through a submerged room. Their clothes drift, light ripples across the walls, and distant objects disappear into a murky blue-green haze.

Filming that scene underwater would make the water supply much of its own behavior. It would also complicate camera movement, communication, lighting, and the performance itself.

Filmmakers sometimes choose to construct the underwater impression on a dry stage instead.

**Dry for wet recreates the visible effects of submersion while photographing performers, objects, or [miniatures](/pattern/film-miniatures) in air.** Suspension supplies floating movement; atmosphere and lighting suggest water around the subject; visual effects can complete hair, clothing, particles, bubbles, and scenery.

The technique works by distributing the jobs that real water would otherwise perform.

On a dry-for-wet stage, a performer can hang above a dry floor. Moving light patterns cross the surrounding set, and stage haze makes shafts of light visible.

The finished view removes the support and adds further evidence of submersion. Hair spreads outward, clothing billows, particles occupy the space, and the distant environment loses clarity.

There is no single ingredient that transforms the scene. The impression comes from several compatible clues:

- The body appears supported without standing on anything.
- Loose materials respond as though surrounded by a fluid.
- Light and visibility change across the space.
- Small suspended elements reveal an otherwise invisible medium.

**The audience infers the water from what it does to everything inside the image.**

## Floating is a movement problem

A suspended performer can remain clear of the ground, but suspension alone does not reproduce swimming.

Water supports the body while also resisting movement. A hand stroke, a turn, and a drifting sleeve each respond differently. On a dry stage, those relationships must come from choreography, mechanical assistance, altered playback speed, digital animation, or a combination.

The performer contributes intention: looking toward a destination, reaching, recovering balance, or reacting to another character. The support system supplies the trajectory that cannot be sustained unaided.

Slow motion can help lengthen movements and soften their rhythm. It still needs suitable action to begin with. Slowing an ordinary jump does not automatically give it the character of buoyant drifting.

## The space around the body needs to participate
Stage haze can make light beams visible and reduce clarity across a set. That gives filmmakers a photographic starting point for the sense of looking through a medium.

Moving light patterns provide another clue. The bright, shifting patterns associated with light passing through a rippling surface are called **caustics**. Projected patterns can suggest that behavior across scenery and performers.

Their use depends on the environment. A shallow, sunlit interior and a submarine traveling through deep darkness need different lighting.

Color and distance also matter. Water changes the light traveling through it, so a convincing underwater world needs more than a uniform blue tint. ILM’s work on *Aquaman* included tools specifically designed to control the absorption of light and color through seawater. [The studio explains that work here.](https://www.ilm.com/vfx/aquaman/)

### Example: *The Shape of Water* (2017)—how can an entire apartment seem submerged?

The opening presents a dreamlike underwater domestic space, with Elisa and household objects apparently floating.

Cinematographer Dan Laustsen described photographing the sequence on a smoke-filled stage, using projectors to supply moving light. Suspension supported the floating elements, while wind machines helped move clothing. Higher-speed photography softened the motion, and visual effects added further underwater elements.

The result suits the film’s fairy-tale atmosphere: a familiar room acquires a different physical logic.

[Laustsen’s ARRI interview describes the photography, including the opening and closing dry-for-wet sequences.](https://www.arri.com/news-en/del-toro-and-laustsen-craft-shape-of-water-)

The production also used actual water for the flooded bathroom scene. Laustsen explains that the bathroom set was placed inside a specially constructed tank. Different scenes therefore received different solutions, according to the interaction they needed to show. [His detailed interview explains that distinction.](https://behindthelensonline.net/site/interviews/interview-exclusives/danlaustsenshapeofwater/)

### Example: *The Hunt for Red October* (1990)—how can a miniature submarine travel through deep ocean?

A miniature submarine does not need to be submerged to look submerged.

For the film’s underwater effects, miniature vessels were photographed on smoke-filled stages using controlled camera and model movement. The atmosphere helped establish depth and visible illumination, while compositing supplied additional elements.

ILM artist Scott E. Anderson described creating particle effects for water flow and bubble trails. His account includes a revealing problem: an early torpedo trail behaved incorrectly because the photography had simulated travel by moving the camera rather than moving the torpedo as expected.

The photographed movement looked right, but the particle simulation needed to account for how that apparent movement had actually been constructed.

The example shows two complementary parts of dry for wet: **create a persuasive photographed object, then make the surrounding medium respond consistently to its apparent motion.**

[Anderson’s interview includes footage and explains the integration of particles with the miniature photography.](https://beforesandafters.com/2020/03/02/particles-ilm-and-the-hunt-for-red-october/)

### Example: *Aquaman* (2018)—how can people converse and fight while floating underwater?

The film’s underwater world demands sustained acting, complex movement, and flowing hair and costumes.

Director James Wan described filming performers on harness-connected rigs against bluescreen. Those rigs helped simulate floating and swimming. Visual effects then developed the surrounding world and altered the movement of hair and clothing.

Hair required particular planning. Wan explained that practical hairlines were retained while hair was controlled during photography, allowing digital strands to supply the floating behavior afterward. [His interview describes the rig-and-effects approach.](https://www.thewrap.com/aquaman-director-james-wan-underwater-world-rigs-wigs-visual-effects/)

Here, the photographed performance becomes one component of a substantially reconstructed underwater image. The method provides control over facial acting and choreography while assigning difficult fluid behavior to simulation.

[The production’s visual effects producer discusses that broader construction, with imagery and a behind-the-scenes featurette.](https://vfxvoice.com/mastering-water-producing-the-effects-for-aquaman/)

## Hair and clothing are demanding witnesses

A face can remain expressive while the body hangs in a convincing pose. A sleeve falling straight downward may still reveal the real direction of gravity.

Loose materials continually describe their surroundings. Hair can spread, trail, curl, or settle. Fabric can lag behind a gesture and continue moving after the body stops.

Fans provide useful movement, but air and water produce different behavior. Digital replacement or augmentation can therefore be more effective than trying to achieve every detail physically.

The amount of reconstruction varies. A distant silhouette may need little refinement; a close view of long hair may require extensive work.

## Particles and bubbles need a reason to behave as they do

Suspended particles help establish depth and relative motion. Material passing close to the lens can connect the foreground performance to the larger environment.

Bubbles provide stronger directional information: their movement helps establish which way is up and how the camera or character is moving.

That makes them useful, but also capable of exposing a mismatch. A body, camera, bubble trail, and surrounding particles must share a coherent spatial relationship.

A convincing scene also needs restraint. Every underwater environment does not require constant streams of bubbles or equally dense particulate.

## Where the method reaches its limits
Dry for wet is especially useful for stylized floating, extended performances, miniature photography, and scenes requiring camera access that would be difficult in a tank.

It becomes more demanding when the action depends on physical water contact: breaking the surface, splashing, pouring, grabbing soaked fabric, or struggling against a strong current.

Those interactions may favor actual water, a separate effects element, or a more extensive digital reconstruction.

The dry stage introduces its own constraints. Supports affect posture and framing, wires need removal, and the movement of costumes may require repair. A production gains control over some aspects of the scene while taking responsibility for recreating others.

## Connections to other patterns

**[Wire-assisted stunts](/pattern/film-wire-assisted-stunts)** provide suspension and controlled movement for apparent floating.

**[Overcranking](/pattern/film-overcranking-and-undercranking)** changes the rhythm of photographed action, helping suitable movements suggest underwater resistance.

**[Miniatures](/pattern/film-miniatures)** allow vessels and environments to be photographed under controlled conditions before the underwater world is completed.

**Projection** can supply moving illumination, including the rippling light patterns that help scenery appear submerged.
