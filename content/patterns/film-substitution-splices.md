*Make an impossible change by removing the time it takes.*

A magician wants to turn an apple into a rubber duck. A filmmaker wants a character to disappear, a drawing to become a real object, or a performer to become a dummy before something destructive happens.

The difficulty is the transformation itself. Replacing an apple takes time. Someone must enter the scene, remove it, put down the duck, and leave.

A **substitution splice** removes that interval. Film the scene before the replacement, film it afterward, and join the two matching moments. On screen, the ordinary work of changing things becomes an instantaneous event.

The audience sees a continuous space and assumes continuous time.

## The opening puzzle: how did the apple become a duck?


Imagine the two shots. The magician, table, curtain, and camera position remain approximately the same. Only the object changes.

Between the selected moments, a stagehand makes the replacement. That interval never appears in the finished sequence. The editor joins the apple directly to the duck.

The magician’s gesture supplies an apparent cause. The cut supplies the transformation.

This separation makes the pattern remarkably flexible: the action that seems to cause the change can be a wand movement, a sneeze, a finger snap, an impact, or nothing at all.

The classic method uses a camera secured in one position:

1. Record the scene with the original person or object.
2. Stop filming and preserve the positions of everything that should remain unchanged.
3. Remove, add, or replace the subject.
4. Resume filming.
5. Trim and join the footage so the surrounding action matches across the change.

The last step matters. “Stop the camera and start again” makes the technique sound entirely automatic. Historical film cameras could produce unsuitable frames during stopping and restarting, and the performers’ movements still needed careful matching. The Cinémathèque française describes how Méliès refined his effects with precise cuts and joins after filming. [Cinémathèque française: Méliès’s effects techniques, pp. 17–18](https://www.cinematheque.fr/zooms/melies/fr/telechargement/ZOOMMELIES-VF_PDF.pdf)

With a digital camera, you can even keep recording throughout the replacement and delete the interval afterward. The principle remains the same: **preserve the setting while removing the time needed to change its contents.**

### Puzzle one: how does a seated woman disappear?

In Georges Méliès’s *The Vanishing Lady*—*Escamotage d’une dame chez Robert-Houdin* (1896)—a woman sits on a chair, is covered with a cloth, and disappears. The routine also includes the appearance of a skeleton and the woman’s return.

On a theatre stage, this kind of disappearance requires concealed machinery and a route for the performer to escape. Filming provides another option: stop the camera, let the performer leave, and resume from a matching arrangement.

The cloth gives the transformation a theatrical rhythm. Its removal tells the audience exactly when to look for the result.

The reusable trick is **removal**: replace an occupied space with an empty one. The same structure can make a person vanish from a doorway or an object disappear from someone’s hands.

The Cinémathèque’s presentation includes the film and explains its relationship to the stage illusion. [Watch *The Vanishing Lady* and read Laurent Mannoni’s account](https://www.cinematheque.fr/henri/film/52460-escamotage-d-une-dame-chez-robert-houdin-georges-melies-1896/)

### Puzzle two: how can a performer become a dummy without an obvious interruption?

*The Execution of Mary, Queen of Scots* (1895), produced by Alfred Clark with William Heise operating the camera, uses substitution for a simulated execution.

The Library of Congress records the method explicitly: filming stopped before the execution, the performer playing Mary was replaced with a dummy, and filming resumed.

The audience has already accepted the figure as a living person. Matching its costume, position, and surroundings lets that identity carry across the replacement.

Here the substitution is meant to escape notice entirely. Its purpose is to transfer the next action to a prop while preserving the apparent identity of the subject.

This example also predates Méliès’s *The Vanishing Lady*. Méliès became a celebrated practitioner of the technique, but its history includes other early filmmakers. [Library of Congress: film and production notes](https://www.loc.gov/item/00694120/)

### Puzzle three: how does a drawn bottle become something you can pour from?

In J. Stuart Blackton’s *The Enchanted Drawing* (1900), an artist sketches a face, a wine bottle, and a glass. He then appears to take the bottle and glass off the paper and use them as real objects.

The Library of Congress explains that the tricks were achieved by stopping the camera and making substitutions.

This is a particularly useful variation because the change crosses between two kinds of representation: a flat drawing becomes a physical prop. The performer’s reaching action connects the two states, making the replacement feel like an object being extracted from the picture.

The same construction could turn a pictured flower into a real flower, a toy into its full-size counterpart, or a sketch into a finished product.

[Watch *The Enchanted Drawing*](https://www.loc.gov/item/2024600560/). For the technique, see the Library’s [*Innovative Animators*](https://www.loc.gov/loc/lcib/9906/animate.html).

## What makes the join convincing?
The unchanged parts of the image provide evidence that the scene continues.

A steady table and background help the viewer accept the object’s transformation. A curtain that jumps sideways, a sudden exposure change, or a performer whose shoulders shift can reveal the missing interval.

For a simple demonstration, control four things:

- **Framing:** secure the camera and avoid touching it during the replacement.
- **Appearance:** keep lighting, exposure, focus, and white balance consistent.
- **Position:** mark where props and performers belong.
- **Timing:** choose the join by comparing the frames immediately before and after the change.

A held pose is easiest to match, but movement can also work. Repeat a gesture across the two takes and cut where its position and speed align.

A passing cloth or brief obstruction can conceal small discrepancies. It still needs continuity of its own: a cloth that abruptly changes shape can expose the edit.

## When the pattern is useful
Substitution splices suit changes that should feel sudden: disappearances, instant costume changes, comic transformations, magical repairs, or substitutions between performers and props.

They also create a particular kind of humour. An impossible event can happen in an otherwise ordinary, steady image. A character’s delayed reaction then becomes part of the gag.

They are less suitable when the audience needs to inspect a gradual transformation. A face slowly becoming another face requires intermediate states; a substitution splice presents the change as a jump.

## How it relates to neighbouring tricks
**Stop-motion animation** also changes things between exposures, usually through many small steps that create movement. A substitution splice can make one large replacement between stretches of live action.

A **dissolve** blends two images over time. A substitution splice joins them abruptly.

A **match cut** connects shots through a visual or conceptual resemblance. Here, the matching serves a specific illusion: the scene appears to continue while something inside it changes impossibly.

A **hidden cut** is the broader family. Substitution splices use that concealed interruption to make a replacement appear instantaneous.

## Try the pattern

Put an apple on a table and film yourself pointing at it. Hold still, replace it with a duck, return to the same position, and continue the gesture.

Remove the replacement interval with a straight cut. Then inspect the join one frame at a time.

You may find that the duck is convincing immediately, while a tiny movement of your elbow gives everything away. That is the craft of the effect: arranging the ordinary details carefully enough that the impossible change becomes the thing the audience believes.
