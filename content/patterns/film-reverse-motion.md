*Film the manageable action, then reverse its direction.*

A broken object rebuilds itself. A flower gathers its scattered petals. Something lying on a table suddenly flies into a waiting hand.

Making those events happen physically would require elaborate mechanisms—or an entirely different way of constructing the image. Yet their opposites are often straightforward: an object can break, petals can fall, and a hand can release something.

**Reverse motion turns that ordinary action into its extraordinary opposite by reversing the order of the recorded images.**

The useful question is: **would the effect become easier if we filmed its ending first?**

The photographed event can progress through three states:

**Intact vase → breaking vase → scattered fragments.**

Reverse the image sequence and the progression becomes:

**Scattered fragments → converging pieces → intact vase.**

A real shot would contain many more frames, preserving the paths taken by individual fragments. Those paths are traversed backward, so pieces that scattered outward now converge on their original positions.

The filmmaker does not have to guide every fragment into place. The recorded destruction already contains the necessary relationships.

This is why reverse motion works particularly well for effects involving assembly, recovery, attraction, or restoration.

## The same reversal can create two different illusions
Sometimes the audience is meant to recognize that time is running backward. Falling debris rises, smoke contracts, and ordinary actions visibly undo themselves.

In other shots, the filmmaker wants the surrounding world to feel normal while one event appears magical. An object might leap into a hand without suggesting that the whole scene has entered reverse.

The second approach requires more careful staging. Everything visible in the reversed footage—including the performer’s movements—will also reverse.

A convincing effect depends on deciding which clues to reveal and which to control.

### Puzzle one: how does a demolished wall stand up again?

The early Lumière film *Demolition of a Wall* provides a direct demonstration.

Workers bring down a wall. When the footage is shown backward, debris gathers, dust contracts, and the structure rises into place.

The rebuilding requires no additional construction on screen: it emerges from the order in which the photographed event is presented. SFMOMA’s discussion of the film examines this reversal and the possibilities it opens within an apparently simple recorded action. [SFMOMA: *On Demolition of a Wall*](https://openspace.sfmoma.org/2017/06/demolition-of-a-wall/)

The reusable trick is **turning destruction into construction**. The same principle can make a collapsed arrangement reform or scattered material collect itself.

### Puzzle two: how can a torn flower recover its petals?

In Jean Cocteau’s *Testament of Orpheus* (1960), a hibiscus flower appears to reconstitute itself.

The effect reverses the photographed action of dismantling the flower. Detached pieces return to the places from which they came, giving the gesture an impossible delicacy.

The Cinémathèque française identifies the scene as an example of Cocteau reversing the flow of time through footage assembled backward. Its article also includes archival photographs from the film. [Cinémathèque française: Cocteau’s altered space and time](https://www.cinematheque.fr/article/2093.html)

This variation makes the reversal feel like a character’s power. Hands, object, and timing form a small performance: the flower seems to respond to an intention.

A similar construction could make a scattered arrangement gather around a gesture. The filmed action must supply a suitable path, while the performance gives that path its apparent meaning.

### Puzzle three: how can a photograph “undevelop”?

At the beginning of *Memento* (2000), a Polaroid image fades toward blankness. It then returns into the camera.

A familiar process becomes unfamiliar through reversal. We expect the image to emerge and strengthen; watching it disappear provides an early clue that the sequence is moving backward.

The opening also prepares the viewer for a story concerned with uncertain memory and the relationship between events and evidence. [Filmmaker’s introduction and interview with Christopher and Jonathan Nolan](https://filmmakermagazine.com/110075-past-imperfect-christopher-and-jonathan-nolan-on-memory-metaphysics-and-memento/)

There are two distinct devices here. The opening contains **reversed movement within shots**. Much of the film’s broader structure presents scenes in reverse chronological order, while actions inside those scenes proceed normally.

A story can use either device independently.

### Puzzle four: how can forward and backward action occupy the same scene?

*Tenet* (2020) makes that problem central to its action.

Some people and objects appear to travel in one temporal direction while others travel in the opposite direction. Reversing an entire shot would reverse everything in it, so the production needed several approaches.

The production notes describe performers executing movements in different directions, vehicles configured for forward or backward driving, and cameras capable of photographing in reverse. Cinematographer Hoyte van Hoytema explains that IMAX worked with the production to modify camera mechanics and electronics for reverse operation. [*Tenet* production notes, including crew accounts](https://www.in70mm.com/presents/1970_imax/2021_tenet/press/index.htm)

The resulting sequences depend on choreography, photography, and effects working together. A person physically performing backward and footage being played backward are separate controls; combining them changes how the final movement reads.

The production also had to track which state every character and object occupied. Script supervisor Steve Gehrke discusses that continuity challenge in [Filmmaker’s *Tenet* interview](https://filmmakermagazine.com/110797-tenet-script-supervisor-steve-gehrke/).

## Why the performer matters
Suppose an object should fly into someone’s hand.

One possible setup begins with the performer holding it, then releasing it along an appropriate path. Reversed, the release becomes an arrival.

But the surrounding gesture needs attention. A natural forward throw includes preparation, acceleration, release, and follow-through. Reversing all of that may produce a conspicuous backward throw rather than a convincing magical catch.

The performer can adjust the filmed action for its eventual playback: the hand’s position, finger movement, gaze, and reaction all contribute.

The camera movement reverses too. A push toward the subject becomes a retreat. Planning the finished shot therefore means thinking about the entire image in its final direction.

## What gives reverse motion away?
Some events carry strong clues about their direction:

- **Smoke and splashes:** dispersed material appears to collect itself.
- **Human movement:** walking, blinking, and recovering balance can look unnatural backward.
- **Secondary motion:** hair and clothing may seem to anticipate the action that should move them.
- **Background activity:** another person’s reversed gesture can expose the method.

These clues can be useful when the intended effect is openly uncanny. For a concealed trick, framing and staging should limit unwanted clues.

Sound also needs its own decisions. Reversing the image does not require reversing the soundtrack. Newly designed sound can make an impossible movement feel purposeful, forceful, or surprisingly ordinary.

## When the pattern is useful
Reverse motion is especially useful when the desired action has an easily photographed opposite: restoration from breakage, attraction from release, assembly from scattering, or emergence from disappearance.

Its limitation is the available trajectory. Reversal preserves the photographed path and reverses its progression. It cannot supply a different path merely because that would make a better magical effect.

## How it relates to neighbouring tricks
**Stop motion** constructs successive states individually. Reverse motion can reuse an already recorded progression in the opposite order.

**[Substitution splices](/pattern/film-substitution-splices)** omit the interval in which something changes. Reverse motion shows a continuous change whose direction has been reversed.

**[Speed changes](/pattern/film-overcranking-and-undercranking)** alter how quickly an event unfolds. They can accompany reversal to adjust its rhythm.

**Compositing** allows separately photographed elements to share an image, including elements intended to appear to move in different temporal directions.
