The purchasing team has reduced its handling costs. IT is completing more tickets. Managers approve requests within their own targets. Yet an employee with a broken laptop still waits more than a week for a replacement.

At the review meeting, every department can demonstrate improvement. The employee can demonstrate the delay.

This is a recognizable coordination problem: people improve the part of the work they control, while the overall result receives less attention. The next round of improvement often repeats the pattern—faster forms, more automation, tighter departmental targets—without examining what happens between departments.

## The core move

**Define the result that matters, follow the work across boundaries, and judge proposed improvements by their effect on that result.**

In Lean and systems thinking, this is the distinction between **local optimization** and improving the wider system. A change can benefit one person or department while creating extra work, delay, or difficulty elsewhere. The LeSS systems-thinking guide describes how these effects arise from interactions, policies, and assumptions across an organization. [LeSS: Systems Thinking](https://less.works/less/principles/systems-thinking)

“The whole” needs a practical boundary. For the employee, the result is having a suitable, working laptop with the necessary access. Closing the purchasing ticket completes only one contribution to that outcome.

Improvement must also respect agreed constraints: cost, quality, accessibility, reliability, and the demands placed on people. Faster delivery is one possible objective, not a complete definition of success.

## Busy people, waiting work

Local targets are attractive because they are easier to assign and measure. A manager can count approvals, purchases, or resolved tickets. Following a request through several teams requires cooperation and shared information.

This connects to **resource efficiency versus flow efficiency**. Resource efficiency emphasizes productive use of available capacity. Flow efficiency examines how much of an item’s journey involves receiving active work rather than waiting. A department can keep its specialists occupied while requests spend most of their lives between steps.

Niklas Modig, coauthor of *This Is Lean*, describes organizations with apparently efficient departments but poor overall flow as “efficient islands.” His account also acknowledges a trade-off: maintaining spare capacity to improve responsiveness can carry real cost. [Modig’s explanation of the efficiency matrix](https://hups.com/blog/why-even-the-best-organizations-get-stuck-in-silo-thinking)

Consider batching. Approving twenty requests together may reduce administrative interruptions. But a request arriving just after the batch closes must wait. The saving belongs to one activity; the delay belongs to the person needing the result.

Similarly, increasing one department’s output can simply enlarge the next department’s queue. Theory of Constraints addresses this by aligning work around the system’s limiting constraint, rather than maximizing production independently at every step. [Five Focusing Steps](https://www.tocinstitute.org/five-focusing-steps.html)

## A replacement laptop: three different improvements

Consider a **hypothetical** internal equipment service. A replacement laptop is already in stock. Its journey contains four activities:

- Check the request: one working hour.
- Authorize the replacement: one hour.
- Configure the device: two hours.
- Hand it over: one hour.

Five hours of hands-on work appear manageable. But between these activities, the request waits thirty-two working hours for batch authorization, eight for IT capacity, and sixteen for pickup.

The complete journey takes **sixty-one working hours**.

The authorization team proposes automating its checks. Authorization falls from one hour to half an hour—a substantial improvement in that activity. With the other timings unchanged, the employee receives the laptop after sixty and a half hours.

The saving is real. Its effect on this employee’s waiting time is small.

A conversation across the service produces another proposal. Managers review smaller batches more frequently. IT coordinates configuration slots with expected approvals. Completed devices become available for pickup more often.

In an illustrative redesigned journey, the three waits fall to eight, two, and four hours. The five hands-on hours remain. Total elapsed working time becomes **nineteen hours**.

The friction is organizational. Managers must interrupt other work more often. IT must coordinate with people outside its ticket queue. The pickup desk questions whether more frequent availability will disrupt its staffing.

These concerns belong in the decision. The service owners agree to test the arrangement for standard replacements, retaining required checks and monitoring workload, errors, and cost.

The figures illustrate a mechanism; they are neither measured results nor a forecast. A real trial might expose constraints that prevent those reductions. It might also reveal that the automation and the scheduling changes are worth combining.

## How to put the pattern into practice

Start with a result people can recognize. “Employees regain usable equipment” gives a different boundary from “purchasing processes orders.”

Then follow actual examples across that boundary. Record where requests wait, return for clarification, change systems, or become someone else’s responsibility. Include the information needed to move the work, not just the visible production steps.

This resembles **value-stream mapping**, which examines the material and information flows from order to delivery. Its purpose is to understand the current arrangement and identify a better future arrangement. [Lean Enterprise Institute: Value Stream Mapping](https://www.lean.org/lexicon-terms/value-stream-mapping/)

A useful first step is to trace a few recently completed requests with someone who receives your team’s output. Ask what happened before and after your contribution, including work they had to repeat or repair.

Bring the relevant people into the improvement decision. Frontline staff know how work actually moves; users reveal whether the result helps; managers may control priorities, staffing, or service agreements. GOV.UK’s service guidance explicitly connects designers, policy staff, and frontline operations so changes in one channel account for their effects elsewhere. [Joined-up service guidance](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/service-standard/point-3-join-up-across-channels)

Agree an outcome measure alongside useful local measures. For the laptop service, that might include time until the employee can work again, successful setup, total handling effort, and staff workload.

An individual can expose a handoff problem and improve what they send to the next person. Changing departmental targets, budgets, or staffing commitments usually requires agreement across the people accountable for them.

## When the wider view helps

This pattern is especially useful when departmental targets improve while complaints persist, queues gather between teams, or savings in one budget create costs in another.

A second hypothetical example is customer support. Shorter calls may look efficient. If customers must call three times to resolve the same problem, handling time per call conceals the complete cost. Following the issue through to resolution changes what the team examines.

The wider view does not make every local improvement suspect. A department may reduce wasted effort without affecting another stage. That saving can remain worthwhile even when delivery time stays unchanged.

Nor must the team map the entire organization before acting. Choose a coherent user problem, understand the relevant connections, and make bounded improvements. GOV.UK’s “Solve a whole problem” guidance explicitly warns against both fragmented journeys and services that become too broad or complicated. [Solve a whole problem for users](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/service-standard/point-2-solve-a-whole-problem)

## Failure modes and signs of progress

One failure is choosing an incomplete “whole.” Faster delivery to the next department may still leave the final recipient waiting. Lower organizational cost may shift effort onto customers.

Another is treating a diagram as proof. A plausible explanation of how batching causes delay remains a hypothesis to investigate. LeSS emphasizes examining the assumptions within systems models and using discussion to improve shared understanding. [Systems thinking and mental models](https://less.works/less/principles/systems-thinking)

A third is measuring only the desired gain. Faster laptop replacement is less convincing if configuration errors, overtime, or disruption to other services rise.

Look for evidence across the journey:

- Do recipients achieve the intended result sooner or more reliably?
- Are repeat contacts, errors, and rework decreasing?
- Has effort genuinely fallen, or moved elsewhere?
- Are difficult requests improving alongside straightforward ones?
- Do the benefits persist after the initial attention fades?

The sources here offer management frameworks and practitioner guidance. They support investigating interactions and trade-offs; they do not guarantee that a particular redesign will succeed.
