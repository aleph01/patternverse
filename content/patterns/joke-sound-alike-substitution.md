*Replace a word or phrase with a similar-sounding expression that changes the meaning.*

A familiar sound points toward one word; the payoff asks listeners to hear another. A name, title or expression can become a different phrase without changing much of its sound. The listener performs the substitution.

## Example 1 · Tim Vine

Eye-care website

> Conjunctivitis dot com—that’s a site for sore eyes.  

— Tim Vine

**The turn:** Site replaces sight in a familiar expression.

[Source](<https://www.vice.com/da/article/tim-vine-tells-better-jokes-562-v17n10/>)

## Example 2 · Alex Horne

An unhappy job

> I used to work in a shoe-recycling shop. It was sole-destroying.  

— Alex Horne

**The turn:** Sole sounds like soul, turning an emotional description into a shoe-recycling pun.

[Source](<https://www.southampton.ac.uk/~lac/Comedy/DaveJokes/explore/>)

## Example 3 · Tim Vine

Asking about teenage skin

> So I said to a Scotsman: ‘did you have terrible spots as a kid?’ He said: “ac ne”.  

— Tim Vine

**The turn:** Acne sounds like a Scottish “ach, nae”: the supposed denial names the condition.

[Source](<https://www.irishtimes.com/news/in-praise-of-tim-vine-1.1905605>)

## What makes the structure work

The alternative needs a recognizable meaning and enough sound in common to be heard without a spelling lesson. Accent matters. A malapropism additionally characterizes a speaker as misusing words; a sound-alike pun can be knowingly made.
