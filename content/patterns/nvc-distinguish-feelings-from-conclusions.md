*An editorial pattern drawn from Nonviolent Communication. The examples and dialogue are fictional teaching illustrations.*

“I feel like I’m the only person who cares about this friendship.”

The sentence sounds vulnerable. It may also leave the listener preparing a defense: “That’s unfair. I do care.” Soon the conversation is about proving affection, while the speaker’s loneliness remains largely unspoken.

An emotional experience and an explanation of another person’s behavior often arrive together. This pattern gives each its own place.

## Find the experience inside the explanation

In NVC, feelings are distinguished from thoughts about what someone is doing or intending. “I’m sad,” “I’m uneasy,” and “I’m relieved” name experiences. “I feel that you are using me” expresses an interpretation. This distinction is a tool for clearer expression, rather than a test of whether someone’s distress is legitimate. [PuddleDancer Press, “Feelings and Needs We All Have”](https://nonviolentcommunication.com/learn-nonviolent-communication/feelings/)

An interpretation can still matter. Suspecting exploitation may be a reason to examine an agreement or protect your time. Separating the elements simply lets you say, “I’m exhausted and resentful. I’m also wondering whether you expect me to keep doing this without compensation.”

Now there is an experience to understand and a question to investigate. Neither has to carry the whole conversation alone.

## A friendship with uneven initiative

Mara has arranged the last three meetings with her friend Eli. Twice, Eli canceled on the day. Mara starts composing a message: “I feel like you don’t care whether we ever see each other.”

Under that conclusion she notices several experiences: disappointment about the canceled plans, loneliness, and anxiety that the friendship is changing. She also realizes that initiating every meeting has become tiring.

Her revised message says:

> “I’ve been disappointed about the last two cancellations, and I miss you. I’m also unsure whether you have room to meet regularly right now. Would you tell me how it’s looking from your side?”

Eli might explain that his work schedule has become unpredictable. He might admit that he wants less frequent contact. Mara has not guaranteed a comforting answer. She has made it easier to learn what is actually happening.

If Eli replies, “You’re making a big deal out of nothing,” Mara does not have to abandon her experience. “It matters to me, and I’m sad about it” is enough. An emotion does not need the other person’s permission to exist.

## Disagreement without a diagnosis

A designer receives extensive revisions to work they thought was nearly finished. Their first response is, “I feel undermined.” That word may capture something real about the interaction, but it leaves several possibilities bundled together.

After reflecting, they say:

> “I’m frustrated and anxious about the amount of rework. I thought we agreed on the direction last week. I’m worried that I don’t understand who can change it at this stage.”

This version reveals practical questions: Was there an agreement? Who holds decision authority? What work must be repeated? “Undermined” remains a possible interpretation, but it no longer has to be accepted before the conversation can proceed.

A positive example works similarly. After presenting an early idea, someone says, “I feel like the team finally believes in me.” A more precise expression might be, “I’m excited and relieved that people want to develop the idea.” The difference matters if approval later changes: the person can recognize the pleasure without treating one meeting as a definitive measure of their worth.

## Common mistakes

**Correcting vocabulary while missing the person.** If someone says, “I feel rejected,” replying, “Rejected isn’t a feeling” is unlikely to deepen contact. You can understand the expression and, if helpful, ask whether they are hurt, disappointed, or afraid.

**Using emotion words as proof.** “I’m scared, therefore you are dangerous” moves from experience to conclusion without examining the evidence. Fear deserves attention, and immediate protective action may be appropriate; a broader claim still requires its own consideration.

**Softening every strong emotion.** Anger does not have to become “slight discomfort” to sound acceptable. Accurate intensity can be important: “I’m furious about the broken agreement” is clearer than calm wording that conceals the actual stakes.

**Searching for the perfect label.** “I’m upset and don’t yet know exactly how” can be honest and useful. Emotional precision should support expression rather than become another reason to withhold it.

## Ownership without isolation

This pattern helps when conversations repeatedly collapse into disputes about intentions, or when someone can describe everyone else’s behavior but struggles to say how they are affected.

Naming an emotion does not make the surrounding circumstances irrelevant. A person can own their anger while also holding someone accountable for a broken promise. “These are my feelings” should never become “Therefore your actions have no consequences.”

The distinction also leaves room for mixed experience. A promotion can bring pride and dread. A boundary can bring relief and grief. Naming both may explain apparent contradictions better than forcing a single emotional verdict.
