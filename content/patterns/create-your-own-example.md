Examples supplied by a teacher are unusually helpful because they were chosen specifically to illustrate the idea. The surrounding lesson also gives away what concept the example is supposed to demonstrate.

Creating your own example removes those clues. To invent a valid new case, you have to decide which features of the concept are essential and which were merely part of the examples you were shown.

Suppose you are learning about confirmation bias. After reading examples involving politics and social media, try to invent one involving gardening, friendship, or choosing a restaurant. The new context forces you to extract the structure of the bias rather than imitate the surface details of the original examples.

The technique becomes stronger when you also create a near-miss. Invent something that looks similar but does not actually qualify, then explain why. If a person reads only restaurant reviews after already deciding where to eat, is that necessarily confirmation bias? What else would need to be true?

This is especially useful for concepts with fuzzy boundaries. Creating both examples and non-examples forces the learner to commit to an interpretation rather than simply recognize the teacher's choices.

The method works in creative domains too. After learning a particular compositional principle in photography, invent a completely different scene that uses the same principle. After studying a poetic device, write a line that uses it and another that looks similar but does something else.

Self-generated examples can be wrong, so verification matters. A learner can build an entire family of examples around a misunderstanding. Checking against a reliable source or knowledgeable person keeps the exercise anchored.

But even a wrong example can be revealing. It shows not only what the concept means in the textbook, but what you thought it meant.
