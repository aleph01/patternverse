Someone says, "I shut down when people ask what I want." Asked to describe a recent moment, they notice a sequence: their shoulders rise, their breath becomes shallow, they smile, then say that anything is fine. The global description has become a set of events that can be observed and discussed.

## Core idea

Tracking the unfolding experience means following these particulars as they change. Sensorimotor Psychotherapy gives close attention to sensation, posture, movement, and impulse alongside emotions, images, thoughts, and meanings. The person and practitioner study what is happening rather than treating an initial interpretation as settled.

The defining move is a change in resolution: a global description becomes a sequence that can be noticed and revised. Tracking remains close to what the person reports and what can be tentatively observed. It does not require a practitioner to translate every movement into a psychological explanation.

## What changes in the experience

A therapist might notice the smile and assume that the person is pleased. A different assumption might be that the smile conceals fear. Tracking begins closer to observation: the smile appeared when a preference was requested. What did the person notice then?

The answer may be surprising. Perhaps the smile is an attempt to buy time, an expression of affection, or a habit with no obvious significance. Bodily cues do not come with a fixed psychological dictionary. Their meaning is explored with the person in context.

### Differentiating sensation, emotion, and meaning

A statement such as "I feel rejected" can contain several components. There may be pressure in the chest, sadness, an image of being left out, and the prediction that a friend is losing interest. Differentiating these components does not invalidate any of them. It makes their relationships easier to investigate.

Imagine that the chest pressure lessens after the person changes position, while the sadness remains. Or the sadness changes after a misunderstanding is clarified, while the pressure persists. These differences are useful information. They can prevent every physical symptom from being treated as an emotional message and every painful prediction from being treated as a fact.

Precision remains personal. Some people readily describe texture and temperature; others experience images, action urges, or ordinary emotion words. Tracking should not become a vocabulary test.

### Exploring double signals

Processwork uses the term double signals for divergent messages within an encounter. A person says they want company while leaning away, or reaches to greet someone while stepping back. The divergence can provide a focus for inquiry.

One interpretation might be simultaneous wishes for connection and space. Another could be physical pain, cultural etiquette, or the room's arrangement. Both messages are allowed to remain uncertain. A therapist may invite exploration of the movement, but should not declare that the body has exposed the person's true intention.

The intervention chosen afterward may involve an experiment, a dialogue between positions, or simply more description. The signal is an entry point, not a complete explanation.

The subpatterns address different kinds of complexity. Differentiation separates components that have been bundled into one statement; double-signal exploration notices divergent expressions occurring together. Neither demands a single underlying truth. They may reveal a conflict, an ordinary bodily constraint, or simply a description that needs more context.

## A worked example

In the example, the person discovers that the shoulders lift before the thought that choosing will inconvenience someone. This timing suggests a useful experiment, but it does not establish the cause of the response. The person may explore a smaller choice, a different posture, or what happens when more time is explicitly available.

The therapist proposes that the raised shoulders might express fear of choosing. The client says they are actually bracing for an immediate follow-up question. When they name a preference, people often ask for reasons before they have had time to understand it themselves. The smile is an attempt to keep the exchange friendly while buying that time.

The therapist slows their own questions. With the extra pause, the client notices that a preference is available but feels tentative. They can say, 'I think I want the quieter place, and I don't know why yet.' Tracking has not uncovered a secret meaning in the shoulders. It has revealed an interactional sequence, corrected an initial inference, and helped the person state a preference at a pace they can own.

## Why the move may help

A plausible contribution is that a fast, apparently automatic event becomes available in smaller distinctions. If 'shutting down' includes bracing, anticipating interrogation, smiling, and losing the preference, different responses become conceivable. A change in timing may matter more than a general effort to feel confident.

Sensorimotor Psychotherapy places such observations within a body-oriented clinical formulation. Processwork supplies the double-signal concept. These are related ways of investigating experience, with different theoretical commitments. Observing a sequence does not by itself establish what caused it, whether the same sequence occurs elsewhere, or which intervention will help.

## When it helps—and what can get in the way

Tracking is useful when an experience feels undifferentiated, when a repeated sequence passes too quickly to recognize, or when discussion remains detached from what is happening in the room. It can also make helpful changes visible: a stronger voice, easier movement, or greater willingness to correct the therapist.

Tracking is useful when a client wants to understand a repeated transition that happens too quickly to describe. It can be less useful when the account is already clear and the missing element is practical action or another person's response. Greater detail is not automatically greater understanding; the detail needs to remain connected to a concern that matters.

## Boundaries and common misunderstandings

Excessive tracking can become intrusive self-monitoring. Someone already preoccupied with bodily sensations may need less inspection. A practitioner who continually comments on posture can also make a person feel scrutinized. Consent, selective attention, and the option to leave a cue unexplored matter.

Tracking does not diagnose physical illness, identify historical events, or prove a particular autonomic state. Its value lies in making experience more available to collaborative inquiry. Contacting the Felt Sense explores the meaning of a whole situation; tracking supplies a changing view of its particulars.

Practitioners should distinguish observation from inference in their language. 'Your hand moved away' leaves more room than 'Your body is refusing closeness.' Repeated comments on micro-movements can make a person feel scrutinized or perform for the observer. Uncertainty, bodily habits, disability, pain, and ordinary differences in expression all belong in the picture.
