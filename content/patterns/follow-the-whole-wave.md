Tomas begins talking about leaving the home he shared with his partner. His voice catches. He takes a breath, apologizes, and starts listing the practical advantages of the move. A moment of grief has appeared, then quickly become a problem to manage.

His therapist could keep asking about the loss until he cries more. They could also reassure him immediately and turn to the practical arrangements. Both responses might miss what is happening: Tomas is frightened that, if he begins feeling this, there will be no way to stop.

Another possibility is to accompany an amount of grief he can approach and notice what happens next. Perhaps tears come, perhaps a wish to speak, perhaps a pause. The work includes what follows the first stirring: changing intensity, new meaning, a need for distance, or a little more room to breathe. It also includes finding a place to stop while the loss still matters.

## The difficulty this pattern addresses

Emotional work can stop at the moment emotion becomes recognizable. Someone finally says that they are sad, then immediately explains why they should not be. They feel anger, then hurry to resolve it. A welcome feeling appears and is quickly turned into a plan for maintaining it. Naming the experience becomes an endpoint before there has been room to discover how it develops.

There can be understandable reasons for this. A person may have learned that distress burdens others, that anger escalates if acknowledged, or that needing comfort transfers control to someone else. They may also have obligations after the session or simply not want to approach more. The next move has to take those conditions seriously.

A different difficulty arises when emotional intensity becomes the main measure of therapeutic work. A person cries strongly, and the therapist keeps inviting them to go further. A protective impulse becomes a larger and larger movement. The implicit destination is a sufficiently impressive release. Attention to the person's actual response can be lost in pursuit of that destination.

A third difficulty occurs after something changes. The person becomes quieter, notices a new wish, or has a moment of ease. The conversation moves on before the difference can be recognized. The emotionally demanding part receives time and company; the transition toward rest receives less. Someone may leave with vivid contact with pain but little awareness of how contact with it changed.

Follow the Whole Wave brings these parts of an encounter into the same field of attention. It concerns the development of an experience over time, including its changing form and the conditions under which it can be approached, paused, or left.

The metaphor has limits. An emotional response need not make a single smooth rise, reach a peak, and return to zero. There may be several pulses, overlapping feelings, a continuing background mood, or no discernible change. Grief can recur because a loss remains real. Current danger or an unresolved demand can continue to evoke a response.

Research also gives little basis for imposing one emotional timetable. In a study based on recalled emotional episodes, Verduyn and Lavrijsen found substantial differences in duration, with event importance and rumination associated with persistence. That study does not prescribe how long a feeling should last in therapy or show that continuing emotion means a person is doing something wrong. [Research on emotion duration](https://www.ppw.kuleuven.be/okp/_pdf/Verduyn2015WELLA.pdf).

## The core move

**Stay responsive to the changing emotional process, including what follows its first expression, while preserving enough support and choice for the person to participate and stop.**

Whole means attending beyond the first recognizable feeling. A person may need company while an experience becomes clearer, develops an expression, changes quality, or gives way to something else. They may also need help noticing that enough has happened for now. The aim is continuity of attention across these developments, rather than uninterrupted attention to the most painful material.

Following involves participation as well as observation. The therapist may reflect a feeling, change the pace, make a limit explicit, or help the person return to the room. The person can say that an invitation does not fit. A pause can be part of following the process when it responds to what is actually needed.

A response can develop along several dimensions. Intensity may decrease while sadness becomes more specific. An angry feeling may become a clear wish to refuse. An initially diffuse unease may acquire words. Joy may become easier to experience without immediately minimizing it. None of these changes is adequately described by asking only whether the person is calmer.

The phrase what goes up must come down captures one useful possibility: a person who expects endless escalation may encounter an episode that changes and becomes more manageable. It becomes misleading if treated as a promise that all emotion subsides quickly when allowed. The article uses wave as a metaphor for attending to development, not a physical law of emotion.

Completion also needs a scope. Several different endings can occur within or around a session.

| Possible ending | What may have happened | What it does not establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| An episode becomes less intense. | The immediate feeling or activation has eased. | The issue that evoked it has been resolved. |
| An expression feels sufficient. | The person has said, shown, or acknowledged enough for now. | Every aspect of the experience has been expressed. |
| A new response becomes available. | A wish, boundary, meaning, or action tendency is clearer. | The response has been carried into daily life. |
| The encounter reaches a workable stopping place. | The person can pause, orient, and decide how to continue. | The emotional process is permanently complete. |

The practical question is what has changed, for whom, and within what limits. A person may finish a useful period of grieving while still missing someone. They may stop before any easing and benefit from having their limit respected. Both possibilities belong in an account of the whole encounter.

## How the experience unfolds

These movements describe things worth attending to, rather than stages a therapist should induce. Some encounters contain only one or two of them; others move back and forth.

### Make contact with the beginning that is actually available

An emotional response may first appear in words, a bodily quality, a gesture, a change of pace, or a memory. The person might recognize a catch in the throat as sadness, or say that the movement has no emotional significance. Following begins with their participation in identifying what is happening.

There is no need to enlarge a small response to make it worth attending to. Someone who says quietly, “I did want them to come,” may already be in meaningful contact with disappointment. A large display is not more inherently authentic than a quiet recognition.

### Attend to both the feeling and the conditions of contact

The amount of emotion available and the person's ability to remain engaged are different questions. Someone can feel considerable sadness while staying connected and able to choose. Someone else can speak calmly while feeling far away and unable to register what is being said.

The therapist therefore follows more than expressive intensity. Can the person say how the exploration is landing? Do they want to continue? Is there room for the surrounding setting and the other person, as well as the emotional material? A report of too much, too little, or not this way changes the work.

Support may involve less direct contact for a while. The person might prefer ordinary conversation, attention to surroundings, a practical clarification, or a different subject. These changes need not be interpreted as failure to remain with the feeling.

### Follow changes of quality, meaning, and impulse

A first response rarely exhausts the available experience. Irritation may become a clear protest, sadness may acquire a wish for company, or a frightened feeling may become more distinguishable from the anticipation of being frightened. The transition is discovered rather than inferred from a theory of which emotion should come next.

Attention can stay with a response without keeping it identical. A person who begins crying might eventually want to speak, remember something affectionate, or sit quietly. Repeating the most evocative question at every easing can prevent the very development the therapist hopes to support.

### Notice what happens after an expression

After saying something important, the person may need time to discover its effect. They have voiced a refusal, and their hand lowers. They have acknowledged missing someone, and a memory appears. They have cried, and now notice tiredness or a wish for water. The period after expression is still part of the work.

The therapist can be curious without announcing what a sigh or a quieter voice means. A sigh might accompany relief, resignation, discomfort, or fatigue. The person may be more at ease, or may have stopped because they felt an expectation to finish. The change needs checking in context.

### Distinguish movement from repetition

A response that returns is not automatically stuck. A new detail of a loss may bring another period of grief. A familiar argument, however, can also be repeated while little changes in contact, meaning, or choice. Following involves noticing that difference without blaming the person for repetition.

If the encounter is becoming more exhausting and less informative, the useful question concerns what needs to change in the work. More of the same intensity is only one possibility, and often not the relevant one. Another form of support, a different task, practical action, or a pause may fit better.

### Include a stopping place and the return to ordinary life

The session has a time boundary, and the person has a life outside it. Ending belongs to the work rather than being an interruption of the supposedly real emotional process. The person and therapist can distinguish what was approached, what remains open, and what would help with the transition.

A stopping place can be a felt easing, a clear enough expression, or simply a decision that this is enough today. It does not require becoming cheerful or unaffected. Continuing sadness can coexist with enough steadiness and orientation to leave. Where that transition is difficult, the remaining time needs to serve it rather than a further attempt to produce an emotional breakthrough.

### How different approaches implement the move

The following comparisons retain the source approaches' different aims. Their common concern with unfolding and integration does not make their mechanisms or procedures interchangeable. All accompanying encounters are fictional illustrations, not reports from the cited sources or complete demonstrations of treatment.

#### AEDP: accompany core affect and recognize the transition that follows

Accelerated Experiential Dynamic Psychotherapy gives emotional waves an explicit place in its account of change. Danny Yeung's description of the AEDP model presents the accompaniment of core affective experience through a period of expression and subsiding, followed by attention to the experience of change itself. This is a model of a therapeutic process, not a prediction that every emotional episode will take that form. [AEDP account of transformation](https://aedpinstitute.org/journal/transformance-and-the-phenomenology-of-transformation/).

Imagine someone grieving a long illness during which they felt they had to reassure everyone else. In the session they acknowledge being frightened too. The therapist stays engaged with that experience rather than immediately admiring their strength or moving to advice. The person cries, pauses, and says that the unusual part is having someone remain interested after they stop explaining.

More sadness arrives, but its quality changes. They feel less occupied with making the other person comfortable. Later they notice a wish to sit quietly. The therapist leaves room for that quiet, then checks what it is like to have had company. The person reports feeling tired and less alone, without describing the illness or its losses as resolved.

The 2025 AEDP intervention framework distinguishes processing core affect, regulating dysregulated experience, and metaprocessing transformational experience. Those distinctions matter here: accompanying emotion, adjusting its manageability, and reflecting on change are related but different tasks. A positive shift would invite attention if it appeared; gratitude, calm, or a prescribed ending should not be manufactured to complete the model. [AEDP affective change processes](https://aedpinstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/AEDP-91-for-INTERVENTIONS-with-DESCRIPTIONS.pdf).

#### Somatic Experiencing: follow manageable increments of activation and settling

Somatic Experiencing emphasizes tracking sensation, titrating activation, resourcing, and pendulation. Its glossary describes titration as working with small enough amounts and allowing settling between them. The relevant expression of this pattern is therefore often several small developments, rather than staying continuously with a large emotional surge. [SE glossary](https://traumahealing.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/04/A-Glossary-of-SE-Terms-rev-4.24.21-1.pdf).

In a fictional encounter, someone describes freezing during a disagreement. Remembering the other person's raised voice brings tension through their shoulders and an impulse to turn away. The practitioner and client follow a small amount of this experience while keeping the current setting available. When the person says the account is becoming too much, they leave the remembered exchange and attend to what helps them feel present.

Later, the person chooses to notice the turning impulse again. It is slight. They recognize a wish for more distance and find that imagining a choice about distance is enough for now. There is some ease in the shoulders and a clearer sense of being able to stop. Neither a large movement nor shaking is required for the encounter to be useful.

SE's own materials also frame part of the work as completing self-protective motor responses and releasing thwarted survival energy. Those are explanations within the approach. An observed tremor, breath change, or moment of ease does not independently demonstrate that stored trauma has been discharged or a particular past response completed. [SE description of its model](https://traumahealing.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/SEI-MEDIA-KIT-rev-March-2022.pdf).

The immediate feedback in this example concerns manageable contact and choice. A small amount can be enough, and a pause may be the fitting continuation.

#### Individual Emotion-Focused Therapy: follow the kind of emotion and its changing needs

Individual Emotion-Focused Therapy differentiates between emotions that can guide current action, entrenched maladaptive responses that call for transformation, and emotion that needs regulation. Greenberg's introduction emphasizes responding differently to these processes. This qualifies a simple instruction to remain with every feeling until it fades. [Greenberg's introduction to EFT](https://www.apa.org/pubs/books/Emotion-Focused-Therapy-Ch-1-Sample.pdf).

Imagine someone discussing a small correction at work. They repeatedly return to a familiar shame: they are fundamentally disappointing. The same verdict becomes more vivid each time they describe it. The therapist does not assume that greater shame will eventually produce completion.

As the work becomes more specific, the person recognizes how harshly they are addressing themselves and what they need when corrected. In an agreed therapeutic task, a protective objection becomes available: a mistake does not authorize humiliation. Sadness about years of self-attack may appear alongside that objection. The movement involves a new emotional response and meaning, rather than the old shame simply exhausting itself.

Greenberg describes this direction as changing emotion with emotion, including contact with painful experience and access to new adaptive responses. The contribution to this article is discriminating what kind of continuation is helpful. Some experience needs sustained accompaniment; some needs a different response, a change in task, or help becoming manageable. [Changing Emotion With Emotion](https://www.apa.org/pubs/books/changing-emotion-with-emotion).

The fictional example does not prescribe anger as the answer to shame. Its useful development is that more becomes available than repetition of the same condemning verdict. This account concerns individual Emotion-Focused Therapy, distinct from Emotionally Focused Couple Therapy.

#### Focusing: notice a felt shift and allow a next step

In Focusing, an initially unclear felt sense can develop through language, imagery, and attention. Gendlin describes a felt shift as a change in the concrete experience from which further facets of a situation may become available. The problem may still be unresolved. This gives the wave metaphor a different emphasis: the significant movement may be a small reorganization rather than an emotional peak. [Gendlin on Focusing and process](https://focusing.org/gendlin/docs/gol_2049.html).

Imagine someone unable to finish a letter to an old friend. They describe a knot and keep returning to the thought that they must find the right words. More effort produces the same knot. Given room to attend without forcing an answer, they recognize a quality of waiting: they are waiting for permission to say that the friendship mattered even if it does not resume.

That phrase brings a little easing and an unexpected wish to make the letter shorter. The person has not finished grieving the friendship or decided to send anything. The immediate situation is different enough that a small next step can be considered.

Gendlin's discussion of receiving a shift describes allowing a fresh development some time before old objections immediately crowd it out. This is relevant to the part of the process after the first movement. It does not mean that doubts or protective concerns must be excluded; in this article, they can remain available while the person notices the new development. [The client's client](https://focusing.org/gendlin/docs/gol_2149.html).

The feedback is the person's relationship to the changed whole. Quiet language and a slight easing can carry meaningful movement without a cathartic expression.

#### Raja Selvam's emotional embodiment: make room for integration as well as emotion

Raja Selvam's Integral Somatic Psychology emphasizes developing capacity for emotion through its conscious experience across more of the body. His seven-step account alternates work with emotional embodiment and attention to integration, including periods when the evocative situation and emotion move into the background. The approach therefore explicitly includes a change of attentional emphasis, rather than continuous intensification. [Selvam's protocol account](https://integralsomaticpsychology.com/protocol-embodying-emotion).

Imagine someone feeling pleased about an achievement but finding the excitement difficult to tolerate. They quickly minimize the news. In an agreed clinical exploration, the pleasure becomes accessible through more of their bodily experience. It is still unfamiliar, and they need pauses rather than encouragement to keep expanding it.

At one pause, they report easier breathing and a wish to sit back. At another, they say that they have stopped feeling pleased and are concentrating on doing the exercise correctly. These are different developments. The therapist has reason to explore the second rather than interpreting every quieter moment as successful integration.

Selvam's workshop material discusses variation in the amount and intensity of emotional embodiment. His clinical rationale is to develop capacity, not to require maximum intensity or constant full-body emotion. General research on embodiment does not by itself validate every outcome or physiological claim of this particular method. [Selvam's workshop presentation](https://congress.eabp.org/2023/site/wp-content/uploads/Embodying-Emotions-EABP-Workshop-September-8-2023.pdf).

The useful question in the fictional encounter is whether the person has more capacity to experience pleasure while retaining choice. Its eventual reduction is not the sole aim; being able to remain with something welcome can matter too.

#### Hakomi: include satisfaction and rest in a cycle of responding

The Hakomi method overview describes a sensitivity cycle involving clarity, effective action, satisfaction, and relaxation. It is a conceptual map of functioning, rather than a claim that every emotion follows four observable stages. Its relevance here is the attention given to what follows recognizing a need and responding to it. [Hakomi method overview](https://hakomiinstitute.com/overview-of-the-method/).

In a fictional encounter, someone recognizes irritation about being interrupted. They are used to immediately explaining why the interruption was reasonable. As the experience becomes clearer, a simple sentence appears: “I wanted to finish.” They say it in an imagined exchange, without making it louder or more forceful.

For a moment there is satisfaction in having stated something accurately. Then they begin evaluating whether it was assertive enough. The therapist notices the change, and the person recognizes that they have moved from acknowledging the wish into grading the performance.

They choose to return briefly to the ordinary fact that they wanted to finish speaking. The shoulders lower a little. A quiet pause feels welcome. They have not established how the other person will respond in real life, but the internal demand to keep proving the wish has less urgency for the moment.

This expression of the pattern includes allowing enoughness. The next meaningful movement may be rest rather than a stronger statement or a deeper interpretation. The conceptual cycle offers a question about where the process is going; the person's actual experience determines whether satisfaction or rest is present.

#### Comparing the implementations

| Approach | What it especially follows | What should remain distinct |
| --- | --- | --- |
| AEDP | Core affective experience and a possible transition into experiences of change. | Processing emotion and metaprocessing change are different tasks. |
| Somatic Experiencing | Manageable increments of activation, resources, and settling. | Observed bodily changes and the model's explanations of completion are not equivalent. |
| Individual Emotion-Focused Therapy | The type of emotion, its needs, and possible transformation. | Repeating a painful response differs from developing a new emotional response. |
| Focusing | A felt whole and small shifts that make further steps possible. | A felt shift can matter while the life problem remains unresolved. |
| Emotional embodiment | Capacity for wider emotional experience, followed by attention to integration. | Spatial extent, intensity, and tolerability are different dimensions. |
| Hakomi | Clarity, responding, and the possibility of satisfaction and rest. | Expressing a need does not establish a satisfactory response outside the session. |

These comparisons make whole a question about the actual process. A therapist might be following a changing emotion, a small bodily development, or the emergence of a new response. None requires treating the person as a container that must be emptied of a fixed quantity of feeling.

## A worked example

The following fictional encounter returns to Tomas. It illustrates the broader pattern without reproducing one approach's formal task or a protocol for grief work.

Tomas has moved out of the home he shared with his partner. The separation was necessary, and he can explain the reasons. He feels embarrassed that remembering the kitchen table makes him want to cry. When the feeling appears, he lists the advantages of the new apartment and returns to practical arrangements.

The therapist asks what the change of direction is like for him. Tomas says he is trying not to begin something he cannot stop. He imagines leaving the session unable to work for the rest of the day. There is a real obligation: he needs to collect his daughter in the afternoon.

The therapist takes that concern seriously. They have time to explore a little, but there is no agreement to pursue a large emotional experience. Tomas would like to understand what happens without becoming absorbed in the whole history of the relationship. They agree to keep his ability to pause and the ending of the session in view.

He describes one ordinary evening at the table. His voice changes, and he notices pressure around his eyes. There are a few tears. The therapist stays with him without asking for the most painful memory or reassuring him that the separation was right. Tomas says that the ordinary evening is precisely what he misses.

After a short period, he becomes very still and says he is fine. The therapist does not take the quieter appearance as sufficient evidence that the feeling has settled. Asked how fine feels, Tomas says that he is not quite there; he can hear the therapist but feels less connected to the room. They stop attending to the remembered evening.

Ordinary conversation helps him register the current setting again. He can say more clearly that the invitation to notice the grief had begun to feel like a requirement to stay with it. The therapist acknowledges that their earlier encouragement may have carried more pressure than intended. The option to stop needs to be an actual option.

Tomas decides to leave the memory aside for a while. He talks about the new apartment and the difficulty of arranging a home for his daughter. The practical topic is not dismissed as avoidance. It is part of what he is living through, and discussing it restores some sense of choice.

Later he brings up the table again himself. This time he does not want to revisit the evening. He wants to say one sentence: “I liked having a place where we all sat down.” Saying it brings sadness, but also an affectionate memory of his daughter inventing stories over dinner.

The therapist follows that change rather than returning him to the sharpest grief. Tomas smiles while still tearful. He says that missing those evenings does not mean wanting the old relationship back. A distinction is becoming available within the feeling.

The tears become less frequent. Tomas notices an urge to keep producing them because he thinks this is the useful part. Naming that expectation changes the encounter. He can admit that he would now prefer to be quiet. There is no further question for a while.

In the pause, he becomes aware of tiredness and the chair beneath him. The therapist checks whether this resembles the earlier distance. Tomas says that it feels different: he can feel the sadness, hear the room, and decide whether to speak. He is tired, and he is present. The account comes from his comparison, rather than from an assumption about what a posture or sigh indicates.

A little later he says that he wants to have dinner with his daughter at the new table. It is an ordinary wish, not a complete solution. The old evenings still matter. The wish gives the grief a continuation that he had not been able to imagine while trying either to suppress it or prove that he was processing it properly.

They leave enough time to consider the afternoon. Tomas does not want to start another memory. He would like a little unhurried time before collecting his daughter, and he identifies what remains to be done that day. The therapist does not promise that sadness will stay away or that another wave would mean the work failed.

At the end, Tomas describes the difference carefully. He is still sad about leaving. He has also encountered a period of sadness that changed, included a warm memory, and reached a place where he wanted to rest. More importantly, he could interrupt the exploration when it was becoming too much and later choose a smaller contact with the loss.

The useful development is not that grief went up and came down according to schedule. It is that contact, choice, expression, and recovery could belong to the same encounter. The separation and its practical demands remain real, and no lasting treatment outcome has yet been established.

## Why this movement may help

One possible contribution is a broader experience of what emotion can be like. Someone who expects a feeling to be either absent or overwhelming may encounter more gradations: sadness that can be approached briefly, a pause that does not erase it, or a change of quality that does not require solving the problem. That experience may make future contact feel less all-or-nothing.

A second contribution is continuity. The person receives attention not only while distressed but while something changes afterward. Their tiredness, uncertainty, affection, or wish to stop can be as relevant as the tears. This can counter the implicit lesson that strong distress is the main route to company.

A third possibility is access to information that appears later in an encounter. A feeling may first be diffuse and only subsequently reveal a wish, protest, or distinction. Tomas's wish for ordinary dinners became available after both a pause and a smaller return. Moving immediately from emotion to explanation would have left out that development.

A fourth contribution is learning about choice through the encounter itself. A pause can be requested and respected; a particular amount can be enough; a new direction can come from the person. These are observable features of participation. They do not depend on proving a physiological completion mechanism.

The article also gives recovery its own place. Emotional work can be demanding even when it is welcome. Time to notice what has changed and to return to ordinary activities can help connect the encounter with the person's actual life. A quiet period need not be filled with additional material simply because the session has not ended.

These are clinical rationales for the synthesized pattern. The source approaches offer different explanations, and their similarities do not establish one universal emotional cycle. Research on emotion duration supports attending to variability; it does not show that a particular therapeutic wave has a predictable endpoint. A change within one encounter also does not establish durable symptom improvement.

Finally, the useful movement may involve changing the relationship to a feeling rather than reducing it. Joy can become more bearable while remaining joyful. Grief can remain painful while becoming less lonely. A clear protest can replace diffuse agitation. Tracking these differences gives the work a more informative measure than either intensity or calm alone.

## Conditions, pitfalls, and limits

### Do not make the wave into a timetable

The claim that a feeling must disappear if it is fully allowed can turn continuing distress into an accusation. The person may conclude that they are thinking too much, resisting, or failing to feel correctly. Emotional persistence can have many contributors, including a continuing loss or problem.

The useful stance is curiosity about the course of this experience, with room for more than one account. Repeated activation may call for a different therapeutic task or attention to practical circumstances. It is not evidence that the person has refused to complete an emotion.

### Separate expression from the idea of emptying a reservoir

Speaking, crying, or moving may be meaningful without functioning as a discharge of a fixed store of emotion. Increasing expression is not automatically helpful. The purpose and effects of an action need to be understood in context.

This matters especially with anger. Kjærvik and Bushman's 2024 meta-analysis found that activities aimed at lowering arousal reduced anger and aggression overall, while arousal-increasing activities did not help overall. The findings challenge a general venting rationale; they do not show that naming anger, making a boundary, or all therapeutic expression is harmful. [Anger-management meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38518585/).

A person can recognize a forceful impulse without acting it out toward someone else. There is no requirement to shout, hit, tremble, or produce tears in order to have approached an emotion honestly.

### Check the quality of quieter moments

Less visible expression can accompany easing, tiredness, deliberate restraint, distraction, or feeling disconnected. Appearance alone cannot decide among them. The person's report, capacity to participate, and surrounding circumstances matter.

This does not mean treating every quiet moment as suspicious. Unhurried rest can be exactly what is needed. The distinction matters when someone says that they feel far away, cannot register the conversation, or are continuing mainly to satisfy an expectation. The useful response is to adjust the encounter, rather than insist that apparent calm proves successful processing.

### Keep regulation and contact in conversation

Attempts to settle experience can arrive too early and communicate that a feeling is unwelcome. Attempts to maintain emotional contact can continue too long and override a need for distance. Neither direction is inherently right at every moment.

A useful adjustment concerns the person's current need and response. Someone may want acknowledgment before a calming intervention. Someone else may need less evocative material before meaning is accessible. The therapist should be able to change direction without turning either contact or settling into the preferred performance.

### Notice when staying with it is producing little movement

A person may retell a grievance, repeat a harsh self-judgment, or search for the same absent feeling while becoming increasingly depleted. Familiarity and intensity can look like depth. It is useful to consider whether new contact, meaning, or choice is becoming available.

The answer may be no, in which case another response is needed. Emotional work can include practical problem solving, a more specific formulation, a different kind of relationship to the feeling, or rest. This is compatible with taking the emotion seriously.

### Leave room for different forms of expression

People vary in how emotion becomes visible and what kinds of attention fit. Cultural expectations, privacy, disability, pain, sensory preferences, and the therapeutic relationship can all shape the encounter. A quiet voice is not evidence of insufficient feeling, and an expressive voice is not evidence of greater capacity.

The relevant observation is whether this person can engage with what matters in a way that fits the current situation. Bodily attention or movement may be useful, adapted, imagined, or omitted. This article does not require a particular posture, body sensation, or expressive style.

### Respect the outer situation and the stopping point

A response to continuing mistreatment may not settle while the conditions remain unchanged. A person facing an ongoing practical demand may need resources or action. Tracking an inner wave should not relocate responsibility for those conditions entirely into the person experiencing them.

The same applies to time. An agreed ending is part of the relationship. It is usually more credible to acknowledge what remains unfinished than to imply that a further emotional effort will secure closure. A person can leave a subject open and still end the encounter with care.

## Expressions and sources

Follow the Whole Wave names an editorial family of ways to attend to emotional development over time. It is not a standardized treatment or a claim that all emotion shares one shape. The examples are fictional, and the source approaches' clinical explanations remain distinguishable from observations in an encounter.

Danny Yeung's [Transformance and the Phenomenology of Transformation](https://aedpinstitute.org/journal/transformance-and-the-phenomenology-of-transformation/) describes AEDP processing of core affect and subsequent transformational experience. The institute's [2025 affective change processes](https://aedpinstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/AEDP-91-for-INTERVENTIONS-with-DESCRIPTIONS.pdf) distinguishes emotional processing, regulation, and metaprocessing. This article treats those descriptions as a clinical framework rather than a promised sequence.

Somatic Experiencing International's [glossary](https://traumahealing.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/04/A-Glossary-of-SE-Terms-rev-4.24.21-1.pdf) grounds the account of titration, pendulation, tracking, and integration. Its [description of SE](https://traumahealing.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/SEI-MEDIA-KIT-rev-March-2022.pdf) supplies the approach's language of motor completion and survival energy. That model-specific explanation is not inferred from an observed bodily change alone.

Leslie Greenberg's [introduction to Emotion-Focused Therapy](https://www.apa.org/pubs/books/Emotion-Focused-Therapy-Ch-1-Sample.pdf) and [Changing Emotion With Emotion](https://www.apa.org/pubs/books/changing-emotion-with-emotion) ground the distinction between using, regulating, and transforming emotion. The comparison concerns individual Emotion-Focused Therapy.

Eugene Gendlin's [Focusing Ability in Psychotherapy, Personality, and Creativity](https://focusing.org/gendlin/docs/gol_2049.html) describes felt shifts and the further developments they can make possible. [The Client's Client](https://focusing.org/gendlin/docs/gol_2149.html) addresses receiving a fresh step. Their relevance here is the small development of an experience, rather than a requirement for catharsis.

Raja Selvam's [seven-step protocol account](https://integralsomaticpsychology.com/protocol-embodying-emotion) and [2023 workshop presentation](https://congress.eabp.org/2023/site/wp-content/uploads/Embodying-Emotions-EABP-Workshop-September-8-2023.pdf) ground the discussion of emotional embodiment and integration. The descriptions of the method do not independently substantiate broad claims of superior outcomes or a specific mechanism. Cedar Barstow's [Hakomi method overview](https://hakomiinstitute.com/overview-of-the-method/) supplies the sensitivity-cycle comparison.

Verduyn and Lavrijsen's [2015 study of emotion duration](https://www.ppw.kuleuven.be/okp/_pdf/Verduyn2015WELLA.pdf) provides a research basis for taking variability seriously. It uses recalled episodes and does not test the present synthesized pattern. Kjærvik and Bushman's [2024 meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38518585/) concerns anger-management activities and qualifies the general idea that increasing expression or arousal necessarily reduces anger. Neither study specifies a universal course for emotion in psychotherapy.
