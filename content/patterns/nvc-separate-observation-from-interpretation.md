*An editorial pattern drawn from Nonviolent Communication. The examples and dialogue are fictional teaching illustrations.*

The meeting ends, and you say to a colleague, “You dismissed my proposal.” Your colleague replies, “No, I didn’t. I asked a reasonable question.” Within a minute, the proposal has disappeared from the conversation. You are debating whether the colleague is dismissive and whether you are oversensitive.

Something happened in the meeting. Something also happened in your understanding of it. Separating those two things gives both of you a more workable place to begin.

## Describe the event before explaining its meaning

An observation names what you saw, heard, or otherwise noticed. An interpretation says what that event means. NVC makes this distinction central to honest expression: describe the relevant event without presenting an evaluation as though it were directly observed. Rosenberg also distinguishes stating an inference as your own from treating it as the only possible account. [Rosenberg, “Observing Without Evaluating”](https://nonviolentcommunication.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/nvc3-chapter3.pdf)

“You said, ‘We don’t have time for this,’ before I finished describing the proposal” identifies a particular event. “You don’t value my contribution” interprets that event. The interpretation may point to an important concern, but the quoted words do not establish the colleague’s entire attitude toward you.

The useful movement is from a verdict about the person toward an account of the moment. It creates a smaller question that can be examined: what happened here, and what did it mean to each of us?

## A proposal that never reached the table

Consider how the meeting conversation might continue:

> “When I was explaining the proposal, you said we didn’t have time and moved to the next agenda item. I hadn’t reached the part about costs. I’m frustrated because I wanted a chance to have the idea considered.”

The colleague answers:

> “I thought you were introducing another topic. I didn’t realize the cost estimate was part of the proposal.”

This reply does not settle whether the interruption was considerate. It supplies information that was unavailable while the conversation revolved around “dismissive.” The next step might be to schedule five minutes for the proposal, revise how agenda items are introduced, or discuss a recurring interruption pattern.

It is also possible that the colleague says, “Yes, I interrupted because I don’t think this project is worth more time.” The observation still helps. The disagreement is now visible rather than hidden inside an argument about tone.

## When a small event carries a large history

At home, one person says, “You always leave everything to me.” Their partner starts listing the chores they completed yesterday. Both have evidence, but neither knows which immediate problem they are trying to solve.

A more grounded opening might be:

> “After dinner on Monday, Tuesday, and tonight, I washed the dishes and packed tomorrow’s lunches. We had agreed to alternate those jobs. I’m worn out and want to revisit how we share them.”

Specificity makes the agreement and the repeated event discussable. It need not reduce a long-standing imbalance to a single isolated incident. When the pattern matters, name several representative events and the period they cover.

The same distinction helps with ambiguous silence. “You ignored my message” assumes that the message was seen and intentionally left unanswered. “I sent the message yesterday morning and haven’t received a reply” leaves room for several explanations while preserving the fact that you are waiting.

## Common mistakes

**Smuggling an accusation into a description.** “When you selfishly took the last portion” contains a judgment inside an otherwise concrete sentence. Removing the adjective reveals the event; the fairness concern can then be stated separately.

**Building a courtroom transcript.** A long inventory of exact timestamps can become a prosecution. Include enough detail to identify the issue. Precision should serve understanding, not overwhelm the other person with exhibits.

**Using the distinction to disqualify someone’s experience.** “That’s an interpretation” can become a sophisticated way to stop listening. A more helpful response is, “I can see it landed that way. Which part of what I said gave you that impression?”

**Waiting for perfect objectivity.** Memory is partial, and people notice different details. “I remember you saying…” or “I may have missed something…” makes uncertainty explicit without preventing the conversation.

## What this pattern can and cannot do

Separating observation from interpretation is especially useful when broad labels, disputed intentions, or words such as “always” and “never” keep derailing a conversation. It can also improve written feedback, where the recipient cannot immediately ask what you mean.

It does not require suspending judgment indefinitely. You can document repeated conduct, decide an agreement has been broken, or conclude that a situation is unacceptable. Describing the evidence clearly supports those decisions.

Nor must you secure someone’s agreement about every detail before setting a boundary. If an exchange becomes threatening, establishing a shared description can wait. The pattern helps clarify experience; it does not obligate anyone to continue a conversation they need to leave.
