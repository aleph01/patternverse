**Pattern 5 of 49 · Part 1: Start Making Songs**

**Use when:** A good fragment disappears, or its recording no longer tells you what made it promising.

A voice memo contains eleven seconds of humming, two seconds of silence, and a spoken “something like that.” Three weeks later, the writer cannot tell where the bar begins or what made the fragment exciting. The pitch contour survived, but the idea's useful context did not.

Capture Musical Seeds means preserving both the fragment and enough surrounding information to resume it. The record can be rough. It should answer what the idea is, what is provisional, and which next move originally seemed promising.

## Working choice

Capture the musical event together with its timing, context, and the next question worth returning to. A large archive is not development; retain enough information to act on a seed without documenting everything.

## Save the sound before the explanation

When a melody arrives, its rhythm, phrasing, and emphasis can be harder to reconstruct than its approximate pitch. A short recording preserves those relationships more effectively than an elaborate description alone. Instrumental skill is not a prerequisite: a quiet hum, a tapped rhythm, or a spoken line can carry the important contour.

For Porch Light, the initial record might contain the sung notes E4–G4–A4 followed by “one beat, one beat, two beats; perhaps an opening figure, not necessarily the chorus.” That sentence keeps the promising shape without pretending its eventual role is decided.

If exact pitch is unknown, say so. “Starts around the middle of my comfortable range” is more honest than an incorrect note label. Later, a keyboard or another musician can help identify it. A useful capture distinguishes what was heard from what was inferred.

## Keep a few contextual anchors

A fragment becomes easier to revisit with a working title, date, possible meter or pulse, and a brief note about its attraction. “The gap before the last note” is more actionable than “nice vibe.” The attraction may involve a word, an interval, an unusual accent, or a contrast between the sound and the line.

An original Platform Three note might read: “D-centered bass, about 104 beats per minute, low D on one, A after three; keep the empty second beat; high answer later.” The entry names what should remain available while leaving the instrumentation open.

The method can be entirely ordinary: one audio file and a few lines of text. A complex naming system is not required. An organized archive that delays every capture until the right folder is chosen defeats its own purpose.

## A voice memo keeps the uncertainty audible

Billie Eilish and FINNEAS discuss **everything i wanted** in a [Song Exploder episode with original voice memos](https://songexploder.net/billie-eilish). The [transcript's voice-memo passage](https://songexploder.net/transcripts/billie-eilish-transcript.pdf) places an unfinished vocal idea beside the later release. Their account describes recording ideas while trying them, including unsettled melody and language. The memo's usefulness is precisely that it preserves more than a polished lyric sheet: timing, accompaniment, delivery, and the possibility of another next line.

For Porch Light, a memo that catches the E–G–A gesture with its long last note is more recoverable than the words “rising melody.” A short accompanying note can identify the starting pitch and a possible answer without making them final decisions. The professional example supports saving an exploratory performance; it does not make exhaustive recording a requirement or prove that every saved fragment deserves completion. ## Separate a seed from a draft

A seed is a promising fragment. A draft makes more commitments: a section, lyric, progression, or rough arrangement. Confusing these stages can make a small idea feel like an abandoned full song before it has had a fair chance.

For example, a title “Borrowed Morning” may have no melody yet. That does not make it an unfinished failure. Its record might include two possible perspectives: someone enjoying a brief escape, or someone postponing an uncomfortable decision. Those are alternatives worth keeping until one suggests music.

By contrast, Porch Light's second draft has an eight-bar verse and chorus request. It deserves a separate version because changing its key or opening line now affects several connected choices. Keeping both files preserves the option of returning to the earlier, smaller idea without confusing the two.

## Record what changed and why

A useful version note explains a decision: “Lowered by two semitones because the chorus held too long at the top of the singer's range.” Another might say, “Kept the original key; simplified the chorus rhythm instead.” These notes turn an archive into a record of tested possibilities.

They need not justify every keystroke. Significant choices include a changed perspective, a new chord beneath a fixed note, a shorter intro, or a different ending. If several changes happen at once, record that fact rather than later attributing the improvement to one detail without evidence.

In this course, the canonical songbook keeps Porch Light's base progression stable and labels substitutions separately. That convention makes comparisons readable. A writer's personal archive can use different labels, but an example should not silently change its underlying material while claiming to demonstrate only a rhythm adjustment.

## Let the archive support selection

Not every captured seed needs development. Some repeat what another draft already does better. Some reveal no useful continuation. Some become ingredients in a different song. The archive is a source of possibilities, not a debt ledger.

A revisit becomes productive when it asks a concrete question: does this title suggest a scene? Does this melodic fragment have a convincing answer? Does the rhythm still feel distinctive at a different tempo? The next action can be small enough to reveal whether further work is warranted.

This pattern helps when ideas disappear between sessions, when old files become unintelligible, or when revisions overwrite the feature that first mattered. It can become counterproductive if categorization replaces contact with the music. Preserve enough to resume, then return to writing.
