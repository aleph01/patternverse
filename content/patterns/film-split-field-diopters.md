*Keep a nearby detail and distant action sharp in the same shot.*

A person stands inches from the camera. Across the room, someone opens a door. The scene depends on seeing both: the first person’s expression and the arrival they haven’t noticed.

Cutting between them would work, but it would break their shared moment. Changing focus would tell the audience where to look, one subject after the other. What if the filmmaker wants us to watch both simultaneously?

A **split-field diopter** lets different parts of the picture focus at different distances. It makes a close-up and a distant view coexist within one composition.

## The puzzle: how can both people be sharp?

![Near and distant Star Trek characters remain sharp in different parts of the same frame.](https://theasc.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/Shot-Craft-Diopters-1-01-Figure-2.4.13_1.jpg)

*Star Trek: The Motion Picture (1979) — Film frame.* Richard H. Kline’s split-diopter composition keeps subjects at widely separated distances readable together. The accessory changes focus over only part of the frame. [Image source](https://theasc.com/article/shot-craft-deep-focus-diopters/). Credit: Film image published by American Cinematographer.


Look at the *Star Trek: The Motion Picture* frame. Subjects at widely separated distances remain readable together, although an ordinary focus setting would make this difficult.

A useful clue is the background behind the nearer subject: that area can remain soft even while the distant subject in the other part of the frame is sharp.

The camera hasn’t acquired unlimited depth of field. Different regions of the image have different focus settings.

## How the trick works

A conventional close-up attachment is an additional lens placed in front of the camera lens, allowing it to focus closer. The common split-field version consists of a semicircular close-up lens with the other half of its holder left open.

The camera lens focuses on the distant subject. The additional glass changes the focus for the portion of the image containing the nearby subject. Light passing through the uncovered area retains the distant focus.

The result is **two distinct zones of focus**, each with its own range of acceptable sharpness. Objects between the selected distances can remain blurred. This distinction separates split-field photography from ordinary deep focus, where a continuous stretch of space appears acceptably sharp. [American Cinematographer: “Deep Focus — Diopters”](https://theasc.com/article/shot-craft-deep-focus-diopters/)

The attachment can rotate, allowing the boundary to run vertically, horizontally or diagonally through the composition. The division needn’t fall exactly down the middle. [Tiffen: Split-field Diopters](https://tiffen.com/products/138mm-split-field-diopter)

## The second puzzle: where is the join?
There is a transition where the additional glass ends. It can reveal the trick through a soft band or an unexpected change in sharpness.

Filmmakers often place that transition against something already dividing the picture: a doorway, pillar, equipment cabinet or dark area. A doorjamb, for example, can provide a plausible place to conceal it.

This makes set design and staging part of the optical effect. The filmmaker arranges the scene so that its natural divisions accommodate the lens’s divisions. The production account of *The Andromeda Strain* describes aligning the diopter edge with lines in the set to camouflage it. [American Cinematographer: Photographing *The Andromeda Strain*](https://theasc.com/article/the-andromeda-strain-may-1971/)

### Example: *All the President’s Men* (1976)—two kinds of information

*How can a nearby television image and the reporters working farther away remain readable together?*

A late shot juxtaposes footage of Nixon on television with Woodward and Bernstein continuing their work in the newsroom. The composition holds public presentation and investigative labor together.

Gordon Willis used split diopters because backgrounds sometimes carried information as important as the foreground actors. His own account identifies maintaining that relationship as a central photographic problem.

Read this composition as a storytelling choice: the audience can compare two activities without an edit separating them. [Gordon Willis’s production account, including the film frame](https://theasc.com/article/flashback-all-the-presidents-men/)

### Example: *The Andromeda Strain* (1971)—sharpness across a guarded space

*How can soldiers close to the camera on both sides remain sharp alongside a guard and car much farther away?*

Richard H. Kline used two split-field diopters for this composition: one covering each side, with the distant view visible between them.

It demonstrates that the pattern extends beyond a simple near-left/far-right arrangement. Several pieces of close-up glass can create several useful regions within one frame.

For a film about observation, containment and scientific procedure, this kind of composition makes relationships across a space immediately readable. The ASC article includes the soldiers’ shot and further examples from the laboratory. [Production account and illustrated breakdown](https://theasc.com/article/the-andromeda-strain-may-1971/)

### Example: *Hoffa* (1992)—a private conversation beside a public performance

*How can two nearby men at opposite edges of the frame stay sharp while a performer remains sharp onstage between them?*

In a theater scene, Danny DeVito and John C. Reilly converse in the wings while Bruno Kirby performs in the distance.

Cinematographer Stephen H. Burum focused the main lens on Kirby. Separate split-field attachments brought the two nearby actors into focus on opposite sides.

The arrangement effectively created a clear central opening surrounded by regions focused closer. It kept a private exchange and a public performance available to the audience at once. [Stephen H. Burum’s explanation, with *Hoffa* frames](https://theasc.com/article/shot-craft-a-closer-look-at-close-up-attachments/)

### Example: *Patrick* (2013)—making the optical effect part of the style

*Does an unusually sharp foreground-and-background composition necessarily mean the actors were filmed separately?*

For *Patrick*, cinematographer Garry Richards explicitly confirms that the split-diopter shots were photographed in camera using real attachments.

He and director Mark Hartley planned the compositions in advance. Dark backgrounds and shadows helped conceal the transitions. Richards describes the resulting look as part of their affection for the filmmaking of Brian De Palma and Hitchcock.

Here, the slightly conspicuous composition contributes to the film’s stylized atmosphere. An effect can serve the scene even when attentive viewers recognize it. [Garry Richards interview, with comparison frames](https://deepfriedmovies.com/2014/04/01/deep-fried-interview-patrick-cinematographer-garry-richards/)

## When the pattern earns its place
A useful question is: **what becomes meaningful when these two things remain visible together?**

Possible answers include:

- **Suspense:** someone’s face and an approaching danger.
- **Cause and effect:** a hand operating a control and the person affected.
- **Divided attention:** a conversation nearby and an event across the room.
- **Contradiction:** one character’s reassuring words and another’s revealing reaction.

These are possible applications of the mechanism. The strongest compositions give the audience a relationship to inspect, beyond the novelty of simultaneous sharpness.

## What makes it difficult
Actors must stay within the appropriate focus regions. A person crossing the transition may abruptly become soft. A composition that looks convincing in a still frame can expose its construction once people move through it.

Camera movement is possible, however. Willis describes Panavision building a rotating holder that allowed attachments to slide in and out during moving shots, including pans and zooms. Such shots require coordinated movement of the camera, subjects and optical attachment. [Willis on moving split-diopter shots](https://theasc.com/article/flashback-all-the-presidents-men/)

## Choosing between related tricks

A **rack focus** transfers attention between distances over time. A split-field diopter keeps selected subjects sharp simultaneously.

**Ordinary deep focus** is preferable when the intervening space also needs to remain clear.

**Tilt or slant focus** changes the orientation of the focus plane. It can suit subjects arranged along a sloping plane without introducing the same split boundary. Panavision describes its slant-focus lenses as providing a related effect without a dividing line to conceal. [Panavision: Slant Focus](https://www.panavision.com/camera-and-optics/optics/product-detail/sr-slant-focus)

Choose a split-field diopter when the scene gains something from holding separate distances together—and when the composition gives those focus regions somewhere convincing to meet.

[Selective-focus miniaturization](/pattern/film-selective-focus-miniaturization) uses a different distribution of sharpness: it deliberately blurs much of a large scene to suggest a small one.
