Learning something once does not mean it will remain available. Material that feels secure at the end of an afternoon can become surprisingly inaccessible a few weeks later. Cramming often works well enough for immediate performance, but it can create a misleading sense of permanence.

Spaced practice works with forgetting rather than pretending it will not happen. Instead of concentrating all study into one period, you return to the material across time. The return happens after some of the original accessibility has faded, which means the knowledge has to be reconstructed rather than simply continued from fresh memory.

A singer might learn a song on Monday, revisit it on Thursday, and sing it again the following week. A language learner may encounter the same vocabulary tomorrow, several days later, and then again after a longer interval. Someone learning the names of plants might revisit the same species during different walks rather than studying all the photographs in one evening.

The difficulty created by spacing is useful. If you review immediately, the material may still be so fresh that almost no retrieval effort is required. If you wait until everything is completely gone, you may simply be relearning from scratch. The interesting region lies somewhere in between: the material has become less available, but not inaccessible.

This is one reason spacing often feels less satisfying than cramming. A concentrated session produces visible fluency. Spaced practice repeatedly confronts you with partial forgetting. The learner may even feel as though progress is slower because every session begins with some friction.

That friction is not necessarily evidence that the method is failing. It can be part of what makes the memory more durable.

There is no universally correct interval. The right spacing depends on the material, prior knowledge, importance, and how long you want to retain it. Something needed only for tomorrow deserves a different schedule from a language, a professional skill, or a piece of musical repertoire you hope to keep for years.

Spacing also works at larger scales. Ideas can return in later chapters, courses, projects, conversations, and real-world encounters. The central idea is not to optimize a perfect review calendar. It is to give important knowledge repeated chances to survive time.
