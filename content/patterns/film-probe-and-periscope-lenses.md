*Put the viewpoint somewhere the camera body cannot fit.*

A camera travels between bottles on a kitchen counter. It slips through a circuit board as though the components were buildings. It looks along a miniature street from pavement level.

The puzzle is physical: a cinema camera is much too large to occupy those positions.

The solution is to separate the **viewpoint** from the bulky equipment recording the image. A small front optic enters the scene, while an extended optical system carries the image back to the camera.

Probe and periscope lenses make this separation practical. They can turn a tabletop into a landscape, but they also solve ordinary problems inside cramped sets.

## The first puzzle: what actually went through the gap?


For a journey between circuit-board components, only the slender front assembly needs to enter the narrow space. The camera body can remain outside.

A probe system uses a long, narrow optical assembly. In designs with interchangeable front lenses, a small front lens—the taking lens—forms the image. Additional lenses, called relay optics, pass that image along the assembly toward the recording camera.

This is an optical connection: the recording camera can remain at the other end of the assembly. Innovision’s technical brochure describes both its relay optics and the ability to move the tubular lens through objects. [Innovision Probe II brochure, with equipment photographs](https://cinetecnico.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/12/Probe-II-Lenses.pdf)

The important freedom is therefore more than magnification. It is **access to a different viewing position**.

## Probe, periscope and snorkel: the useful distinction

The names overlap, but they emphasize different capabilities.

A **probe** typically emphasizes reach: a narrow front assembly extends into a confined space.

A **periscope** emphasizes a redirected optical path. Mirrors or prisms allow the viewing direction and position to differ from the camera body’s orientation.

A **snorkel** is a common filmmaking name for related extended systems, often used to reach down into a scene while keeping the camera above it.

These capabilities can coexist. Innovision’s systems, for example, offered both direct and right-angle viewing. Research on the Kenworthy Snorkel likewise describes it as a periscope-type system intended particularly for miniatures and close-up work. [Lewis Brown’s study of the Kenworthy system](https://repository.rit.edu/theses/5117/)

For a miniature street, a periscope assembly can descend toward the model and turn to look along it. The camera stays above the buildings.

## The second puzzle: why does the model suddenly feel enormous?
A miniature photographed from above often reveals itself as an object on a table. Put the viewpoint inside it, and its architecture begins to surround the viewer.

The height must suit the apparent world. In a model built at 1:24 scale, a human eye height of 1.6 metres becomes about **6.7 centimetres**. A camera body can be awkwardly large even for that modest position.

A periscope lets the effective viewpoint approach the required height while the recording equipment occupies available space elsewhere.

Close foreground objects also pass rapidly across the frame during movement, while distant objects shift more slowly. That changing relationship helps the viewer read depth. The lens provides access; the viewpoint and movement help sell the scale.

### Example: *Marvin’s Room* (1996)—a journey between medicine bottles

*How can the camera make a long, continuous move through a crowded countertop?*

For the opening titles, the crew used an Innovision right-angle snorkel with motion control.

Motion-control specialist Don Canfield describes a tabletop route through hundreds of prescription bottles. The objects passed so close that some had to be removed just after leaving the picture to provide clearance.

The apparent freedom of the finished move depended on tightly coordinated focus, camera movement, lighting and prop handling.

This is a revealing example because the narrow lens alone did not make the path possible. **The crew also changed the space behind its moving viewpoint.** [Canfield’s firsthand account on Cinematography Mailing List](https://www.cinematography.net/edited-pages/CameraMovesThroughMiniatures.htm)

### Example: *For All Mankind*—inside the command module

*For All Mankind (2019) — Television production photograph.* Work inside a confined command-module set. The cinematographers describe combining snorkel lenses with small Technocranes to reach viewpoints that a conventional camera package could not occupy. This example is from television. [Image source](https://theasc.com/article/now-streaming/). Credit: Production image published by American Cinematographer.

*How can the audience occupy a cramped spacecraft alongside its astronauts?*

Cinematographers Ross Berryman and Stephen McNutt used snorkel lenses to reach into the production’s confined spacecraft interiors.

McNutt describes combining them with small Technocranes inside the command modules. Lighting was similarly constrained, so enhanced instrument-panel illumination and small LED sources helped supply the scene.

Here, the technique solves a full-size set problem. It creates access to a position among the performers and equipment, rather than requiring the whole camera to fit there.

Berryman also notes that the system lost light, making camera sensitivity an important practical consideration. [Production account and behind-the-scenes photographs in *American Cinematographer*](https://theasc.com/article/now-streaming/)

### Example: tabletop marble runs—entering a small world

*How can a toy become an environment the viewer seems to travel through?*

Empty Cup Media documents probe-lens work with marble runs, wooden trains and painted miniatures.

The filmmakers describe moving a probe on a slider across small dioramas. Their examples include following marbles through a wooden run.

The small front assembly provides clearance, while close focus and a wide view keep nearby detail connected to its surroundings. A track becomes a route through a space rather than simply a toy being observed from outside. [The filmmakers’ explanation, equipment images and example films](https://emptycupmedia.ca/portfolio_page/laowa-probe-lens/)

### Example: *Supernatural*—getting the viewpoint down to the floor

*How do you obtain an extreme low angle when the camera’s housing prevents its lens from going low enough?*

Cinematographer Serge Ladouceur describes carrying a Century Periscope specifically for extreme low-angle shots.

This shows why the pattern extends beyond macro photography. Sometimes the subject is an ordinary person in an ordinary room; the unusual requirement is the viewing height. [Ladouceur’s equipment discussion in *American Cinematographer*](https://theasc.com/article/supernatural/)

## An extension: move the view while the camera stays still

Some systems add articulated viewing heads and image rotation.

Panavision’s Frazier Lens System, for example, has a swivel tip that permits changes in viewing direction and image orientation while the camera remains stationary.

This separates another pair of operations that usually travel together: moving the recorded view and repositioning the heavy camera body. These are capabilities of particular systems, rather than features every probe possesses. [Panavision’s explanation and equipment specifications](https://www.panavision.com/camera-and-optics/optics/product-detail/flsc-frazier-lens-system)

## What makes these shots difficult
**Access does not guarantee clearance throughout a move.** The front optic may fit through a gap while the barrel, support equipment or camera eventually collides with something.

**Close focus remains demanding.** A wide view can include the surroundings without making every distance sharp. Tiny positional changes can move an important detail out of focus.

**Lighting needs room too.** The narrow assembly can reduce obstruction near a subject, but small apertures and optical light loss may require substantial illumination.

**Movement must suit the apparent scale.** A small tremor can become a conspicuous jolt when the scene is presented as a larger world. Precise support and repeatable movement can matter as much as lens choice.

## How it connects to neighboring tricks
**Macro photography** supplies close focusing and magnification. A probe adds a useful physical shape; a periscope adds an offset or redirected view.

**[Miniatures](/pattern/film-miniatures)** supply a small physical world. These lenses help put the audience inside it.

**[Wild walls and removable sets](/pattern/film-wild-walls-and-removable-sets)** create camera access by changing the environment. Probe and periscope systems create access by changing the optical assembly; productions can combine both approaches.

**[Forced perspective](/pattern/film-forced-perspective)** arranges apparent size relationships. A probe does not automatically produce that illusion, but it can reach the viewpoint from which the arrangement works.
