## A familiar moment

A friend invites you to a small gathering. You want to see them, but the thought of arriving makes you anxious. You imagine standing awkwardly by the door. Cancelling would bring immediate relief, so you begin drafting an excuse.

There may be good reasons not to attend: illness, exhaustion, a threatening person, or something else you need to respect. But suppose this is a safe, manageable gathering and fear of ordinary awkwardness is keeping you from something you value. Opposite Action offers a way to work with the urge to avoid it.

## Understand the skill

Opposite Action is an emotion regulation skill from Dialectical Behavior Therapy, or DBT. An **action urge** is what an emotion inclines you to do: fear may bring an urge to escape, anger an urge to attack, and sadness an urge to withdraw. An urge is information, not an instruction you must follow.

The skill begins by checking the facts and the usefulness of acting on the urge. When the emotion or its intensity does not fit the facts, or following the urge would be ineffective, you can consider acting in the opposite direction. The action needs to be safe and suitable for the situation. [DBT Self Help's overview](https://dbtselfhelp.com/opposite-to-emotion-action/) emphasizes both changing action and knowing when not to do so.

Opposite Action is more than thinking a positive thought. It involves behavior and, where accessible, expression, posture, and attention. Repeated practice may influence emotional patterns, but it does not promise immediate relief. The feeling can remain while you take a different action.

## When to use it

Look for a repeated urge that pulls you away from a valued, manageable activity: avoiding a safe conversation, hiding after a harmless mistake, or withdrawing from contact you actually want. First establish what the emotion is responding to and what action would help.

Fear of an actual threat calls for protection. Guilt after violating your values may call for repair. Illness, pain, hunger, fatigue, or another physical need should not be treated as an emotion to override. This practice does not instruct self-guided trauma exposure, hazardous confrontation, or pushing through medical symptoms. If the situation is unclear, gather information or seek qualified support before deciding to approach it.

## How to practice

Use this established progression; the bounded everyday action in the accompanying practice is an original adaptation.

1. **Identify the emotion.** Name the feeling you want to work with, as tentatively as necessary.
2. **Check the facts.** What is happening, and what are you predicting? Consider whether the emotion and its intensity fit the situation.
3. **Identify the action urge.** Describe what you feel pulled to do rather than judging your character.
4. **Check effectiveness.** Would acting on that urge help with your goals? If it would, a fitting response or practical problem solving may be preferable.
5. **Choose a suitable opposite action.** Define what moving in the other direction would look like in observable, safe terms.
6. **Do it all the way, within the chosen step.** Give the action your attention. For a safe social approach, that might mean greeting someone and listening rather than entering while continually rehearsing an escape. Adapt expression or posture to your abilities.
7. **Repeat and review.** Practise when appropriate and notice what happens. Adjust the size, support, or choice of action rather than judging a single attempt by its emotional result.

[Linehan's emotion regulation notes](https://www.guilford.com/add/linehan7_old/lin-p-3teaching.pdf?t=1) distinguish changing emotion-related actions from solving a problematic event.

## See it in action

For the gathering, you identify anxiety and the urge to cancel. The known facts are that you know the host, the setting is familiar, and no specific danger is apparent. The prediction is, “I'll be awkward and everyone will notice.” You cannot guarantee an easy evening, but ordinary awkwardness is different from a threat.

You want connection, so cancelling solely to escape the feeling seems unlikely to serve that goal. Your opposite action is a small approach: attend for a manageable visit, greet the host, and ask one person how their week has been. You can arrange your own transport and leave if you need to.

During the visit, anxiety remains. You turn your attention to the person's answer instead of continually evaluating your performance. The conversation is uneven; one pause feels long. You nevertheless complete the greeting and one exchange.

Later, you note what was possible and what support would help next time. That is more informative than concluding that the skill failed because you were nervous. If attending revealed a genuine concern about the setting, you would revise the plan rather than repeating the approach automatically.

## Try it yourself

**From Urge to Safe Alternative** takes about 7–10 minutes, plus any action you choose to try later. Use a manageable situation or the gathering example. You will identify the emotion and urge, check fit and safety, and either plan an opposite action or choose a more appropriate response.

If the plan fits, you can rehearse one small action or sentence. You will leave with a cue, action, and review point. This original interactive adaptation does not classify your fear as irrational, make a safety judgment for you, or require an exposure exercise.

## When it feels difficult

**“Doing the opposite feels fake.”** A different action does not require a different feeling. Start with behavior you can honestly choose, such as asking one question while anxious. You do not have to perform happiness or affection.

**“All the way means I have to endure anything.”** It means engaging with the selected action, not giving up boundaries or ignoring danger. Reduce the step until it is realistic and safe. A smaller first step might be preparing a greeting with a trusted person.

**“I tried once and still felt bad.”** Record the action and its consequences separately from emotion change. Check whether the situation was suitable and what made the step difficult. Your first adjustment may be more support, a smaller action, or deciding that another skill fits better.

## Bring it into daily life

Choose one safe, modest approach you have been avoiding and review it afterward. Ask what you did, what you learned about the situation, and whether repeating or changing it would help.

Which action could serve your goals while still respecting the facts, your limits, and your safety?

“When I notice an urge to avoid a safe activity I value, I can try checking the facts and choosing one manageable approach.”
