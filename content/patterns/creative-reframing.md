A writer opens a document intending to finish a perfect first draft. Every sentence feels inadequate, so almost nothing gets written. The task seems to demand better sentences. It may first need a different question.

## Core idea

Creative reframing changes how a situation is represented before trying to solve it. A frame selects the goal, the boundary, the assumed constraints, and whose experience matters. Those choices influence which actions appear sensible. Changing one of them can reveal an option that polishing the original answer would never produce.

A new frame is a possibility to investigate. Several accounts may describe different parts of the same situation, and the original one may remain useful.

## When to use it

Use it when familiar remedies keep failing, when every proposal is a variation of the same idea, or when a task already names its solution. It can also help when the way a goal is phrased makes beginning unnecessarily difficult.

## How it works

1. **State the current question.** Keep the concern visible, including uncertainty about what causes it.
2. **Change one consequential choice.** Try a different goal, scale, boundary, constraint, or affected person.
3. **Compare the implications.** Ask what each frame makes worth observing or trying. A useful change alters the possible actions, rather than merely sounding more positive.
4. **Return to the situation.** Choose one modest observation or attempt that could support, weaken, or refine the new account.

## Worked examples

### From finishing to producing material

The writer replaces “How do I finish this perfect first draft?” with “What rough material would make tomorrow’s editing easier?” The immediate target becomes a set of scenes, fragments, or notes rather than polished prose. The writer can now begin with the section they understand best and leave gaps elsewhere.

The standard of the finished work has not disappeared. It has been moved to the stage where revision is possible. If rough material still does not appear, the next question might concern a missing decision about the story rather than perfection alone.

### From faster answers to fewer repeated questions

A team asks how to answer support requests more quickly. Another frame asks which recurring uncertainties make people contact support in the first place. That opens possibilities such as clearer instructions or correcting a confusing product step, alongside additional support capacity. Looking at recent requests can show whether repeated questions are actually common enough for that frame to matter.

## Pitfalls and limits

Reframing can become a way to avoid an unwelcome fact or postpone an ordinary task. Keep the observations and genuine requirements in view. A more appealing story does not establish a different cause, and renaming a burden does not remove it.

Avoid announcing another person’s “real problem.” Offer an alternative account they can correct or reject. Stop generating frames when the next useful step depends on evidence or action.
