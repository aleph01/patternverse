## What it is for

Use this pattern when customers need several related products or services and spend too much effort finding suppliers, repeating information, and coordinating the pieces.

Someone replacing a kitchen needs more than cabinets. They may need measurements, a layout, appliances, delivery, installation, and help resolving problems along the way.

A business can create value by bringing those needs into one customer-facing destination.

One-Stop Shop makes a broader purchase or project easier to complete. The customer gains a familiar place to begin, connected choices, and fewer arrangements to manage independently.

The commercial opportunity is to serve more of the customer’s underlying need through the same relationship.

## The core idea

Organize the offering around a meaningful group of customer needs.

That group might be everything required for a particular project, several parts of a journey, or the recurring purchases needed to run a business.

The provider can supply the components itself, coordinate partners, or combine both approaches.

Integration can take several forms: a shared account, compatible product choices, connected information, coordinated scheduling, or support across stages.

A one-stop shop can keep its products and services separately priced. Bundling determines what is sold together; One-Stop Shop determines how much of the customer’s need can be addressed through one destination.

The strongest versions reduce coordination work as well as searching.

## How it creates value

**It reduces repeated search.** Customers can find related offerings without beginning a new supplier search at every stage.

**It connects decisions.** Choosing one component can inform the next. A kitchen layout produces a parts list; travel dates shape accommodation and transport choices.

**It carries information forward.** Measurements, preferences, purchasing approvals, or delivery details can remain available as the customer moves through the process.

**It makes handoffs easier.** The provider can help establish what needs to happen next, which prerequisites must be satisfied, and where to get assistance.

These benefits depend on the depth of integration. A wide assortment offers shopping convenience. A coordinated service can take on more of the work required to complete the outcome.

The promise should match what the business actually manages.

## Famous example: IKEA kitchens

IKEA connects kitchen products with measurement, planning, ordering, delivery, and installation services.

Its planning service helps customers select products, develop a layout, and receive a product list and estimates for additional services. Plan verification can include checking the design, confirming inventory, and placing the order. [IKEA: Kitchen Planning](https://www.ikea.com/us/en/customer-service/services/kitchen-planning/)

This turns a collection of product decisions into a more connected project journey.

The boundaries remain important. IKEA’s US installation guidance lists the work included, explains availability, and identifies preparation customers must arrange. It states that demolition is outside its installation offer and may require referral partners. [IKEA: Kitchen Installation](https://www.ikea.com/us/en/customer-service/services/kitchen-installation/)

The lesson is that a useful one-stop shop can have a defined perimeter. Customers benefit when they understand both the connected services and the remaining work.

## Famous example: Booking.com

Booking.com brings accommodation, flights, car rentals, attractions, and transportation into one travel destination.

Booking Holdings describes its “Connected Trip” strategy in terms of customers booking more than one travel category through the company. [Booking Holdings: Connected Trip](https://www.bookingholdings.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/BKNG-Q2-2025-Earnings-Press-Release.pdf)

The customer can address several parts of a trip through a familiar interface rather than discovering a separate booking channel for every component.

This is a platform version of the pattern. Many underlying services remain supplied by independent businesses. Booking.com explains, for example, that accommodation bookings generally create a contract with the accommodation provider. [Booking.com: How We Work](https://www.booking.com/content/how_we_work.html)

Convenient access therefore does not automatically mean that every component shares the same terms or that a change to one reservation updates the others.

The value depends partly on how clearly the platform handles those boundaries.

## Famous example: Amazon Business

Amazon Business combines a broad purchasing destination with tools for managing organizational buying.

Its offering includes a shared, multi-user account, approval workflows, and spending analytics. Purchasing controls can guide employees toward preferred products or sellers and route orders for approval. [Amazon Business: Purchasing Platform](https://www.amazonbusiness.com/brand-join-now), [Buying Tools](https://business.amazon.com/en/blog/purchase-controls)

The wider job is buying supplies while maintaining oversight.

Bringing product selection and purchasing administration together can reduce the effort of managing routine buying across departments.

The limitation is that a broad marketplace does not necessarily replace every specialist supplier, negotiated contract, or purchasing process. The useful scope depends on what the organization buys and the controls it requires.

This illustrates One-Stop Shop around a recurring workflow rather than a single project.

## The economics

The business can earn from several connected purchases through product margins, service fees, commissions, subscriptions, or coordination charges.

Serving an existing customer’s next need can spread acquisition and account-management costs across more activity. A broader relationship also creates opportunities for repeat purchases.

The customer may accept a higher price for reduced effort, greater confidence, or easier coordination. That willingness depends on whether the convenience is real.

Breadth adds costs. The business may need more inventory, specialist staff, partner management, connected systems, and support capacity. Problems can cross boundaries: a late delivery may disrupt installation, creating work beyond the original transaction.

The relevant measure is contribution across the customer relationship after those coordination costs. More categories and more revenue can still produce a less profitable operation.

## Choosing the scope

A strong one-stop shop has a useful perimeter.

“Everything for replacing a kitchen” provides a clearer organizing principle than an arbitrary collection of household services. “Routine business purchasing with approval controls” gives a broad assortment a shared purpose.

Expansion works best when the next offering removes a recognizable gap in the customer’s journey.

Some gaps are worth filling through partners. Others may remain outside the offer. Owning every capability is unnecessary if the handoffs work and responsibilities are understood.

The operating challenge is to absorb complexity without simply passing it back to the customer.

## Useful when

This pattern is especially useful when customers need several related purchases, compatibility matters, or delays between suppliers create frustration.

It fits complex projects, recurring administrative workflows, and journeys where the same information can help across multiple stages.

It is strongest when customers value convenience and the provider can coordinate the offering reliably.

It adds less value when customers prefer selecting specialists independently or when the components have little meaningful connection.

## Common mistakes and limits

**Equating assortment with completion.** More products do not automatically make the customer’s job easier.

**Promising more coordination than the business provides.** Customers need to know whether they are receiving referrals, booking assistance, project management, or responsibility for an agreed result.

**Expanding into weak capabilities.** An unreliable adjacent service can damage confidence in the whole relationship.

**Leaving customers to repeat everything.** Separate teams and systems can recreate the fragmentation the offering promises to reduce.

**Making comparisons difficult.** Itemized prices and understandable alternatives help customers assess the value of convenience.

**Creating dependence without dependable service.** Concentrating purchases with one provider can magnify the consequences of outages, delays, or poor support.
