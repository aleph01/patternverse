Writing advice often pulls in different directions. Be brief, but develop the scene. Explain the unfamiliar, but trust the reader. State the point, but leave room for discovery. Without a sense of whom the piece addresses and what it is trying to do, each instruction can seem equally binding.

This pattern helps when a draft tries to satisfy every imaginable audience, revisions make it smoother without making it more purposeful, or the writer cannot decide which useful material belongs. **Choose a plausible reader and an intended effect that writing decisions can serve.**

An effect might be practical understanding, a changed interpretation, a moment of recognition, or a sustained uncertainty. It need not be persuasion or an instruction to act. The choice gives the work a direction without guaranteeing how any actual person will respond.

## Describe the reading situation rather than a demographic

An audience label can be too broad to help. _Adults interested in art_ says little about what someone already knows, why they have opened the page, or what they need from it.

Imagine an invented pottery cooperative preparing a page about its open studio. The session allows people with previous studio experience to work independently using shared facilities. It is not a taught beginner's class. The draft opens:

> For fifteen years, the cooperative has supported creative exchange through a flexible programme of member-led sessions. Our open studio offers an inclusive environment for developing your practice.

The paragraph may reflect the cooperative's values, but it does not help a newcomer distinguish this session from a lesson. One possible intended reader is someone who has made pottery before but has never visited this studio. The immediate effect is that they can judge whether the session fits their present needs.

That description is more useful than a claim about an age group or personality. It identifies a relevant starting point, a reason for reading, and something the page should make possible.

The cooperative and its arrangements are hypothetical. They illustrate an editorial choice, not tested claims about how people use studio websites.

## Let the intended effect change the actual prose

A working intention earns its place by affecting the draft. For this reader and purpose, an opening might become:

> Open studio is independent working time for people who already know the studio skills their projects require. A host introduces you to this workspace and its shared arrangements, but the session does not include a pottery lesson. If you are looking for instruction, see the taught classes before choosing a session.

The revision gives the visitor a distinction needed for selection. It also raises a useful editorial question: _studio skills_ may still be too vague. The cooperative would need to specify its actual prerequisites and arrangements before publishing the page. Choosing a reader has exposed missing information; it has not supplied that information.

The fifteen-year history might belong later, where it contributes to an understanding of the place. It need not lead simply because it is a fact the cooperative values.

For a different piece addressed to members considering how to fund the studio, the history and purpose of shared working time could be central. The same information can be relevant in one reading situation and distracting in another. Selection follows the job this piece is meant to do.

## Choose what readers should understand, not merely what they should feel

Intentions such as _engage the audience_ or _make it compelling_ rarely discriminate between revisions. Almost any sentence can be defended under them.

More specific intentions identify an experience the writing can help create. The studio page should let prospective visitors distinguish independent access from instruction. An essay might let readers see why two apparently similar decisions require different judgments. A story might leave them aware that an agreement has solved a practical problem while deepening a personal one.

An emotional intention can be specific too. _Unease_ becomes more useful when the writer knows what makes the unease meaningful: perhaps a character's generosity repeatedly creates an obligation the recipient cannot comfortably refuse.

The intention should not substitute for the text. Labelling a passage _moving_ does not make its details carry loss. It helps the writer ask whether the chosen details develop the intended experience or merely announce it.

## Give a story direction without dictating its verdict

Consider a separate invented story. Two sisters clear their father's house. One has arranged transport for nearly everything; the other keeps postponing the decision about his kitchen table. The practical question is where the table will go. The writer wants readers gradually to recognize that accepting help can also mean surrendering a say in what happens.

With that intention, the efficient sister need not deliver a speech about control. She might label boxes before the other has finished looking through them, then quietly pay for storage when the table will not fit in the van. The second action complicates the first: her help can be generous and intrusive at once.

A version intended to end in uncomplicated reconciliation might resolve the transport and the relationship together. A version interested in the cost of accepting help might resolve the table's destination while leaving the sister unable to say whether she agreed freely.

Neither effect is automatically better. Choosing between them gives decisions about the ending, dialogue, and emphasis a shared purpose. It also leaves room for the material to change the writer's original understanding of the sisters.

## Treat the imagined reader as a working model

An imagined reader cannot establish how real people will respond. The studio's writer may assume newcomers understand _independent working time_ and later discover that some expect a tutor to be available throughout. A story's writer may intend mixed feelings and receive only a sense that one sister is a villain.

Those responses would give the writer something to investigate. They would not prove that the initial intention was wrong or that one reader's preferred revision must be adopted.

Useful assumptions concern relevant knowledge and circumstances. They should not turn into stereotypes about what a category of people can understand or enjoy. The same person may need elementary orientation in a pottery studio and sophisticated analysis in a different field.

Sometimes the writer is the first reader. Exploratory writing can begin without a defined audience, and a private draft can discover its purpose through composition. The intention becomes useful when choices need a direction; it need not be fixed before the first sentence exists.

## Allow the intention to change coherently

A draft may reveal a stronger purpose than the one that began it. The sisters' story might become less concerned with control than with the difficulty of receiving kindness after years of estrangement.

That change should reach the whole work. Leaving an opening that promises one experience while revising only the ending can produce a mismatch readers feel without knowing how to name it. Reconsidering the intention may justify restoring material previously cut or removing a polished scene that now belongs to a different story.

A piece can also serve more than one audience or effect. The useful question is whether those purposes can coexist in its available space and form. Separate sections, versions, or documents may serve incompatible reading situations better than one compromise opening.

The pattern gives revision a basis for judgment. It does not promise control over readers, require a commercial audience, or make every unfamiliar response a failure.
