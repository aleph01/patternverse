## A familiar moment

A colleague messages late in the afternoon: “Can you send the revised file today?” You are tired, several tasks remain unfinished, and the message lands as another demand. You type a reply, delete it, and type again. Then you send: “Try doing it yourself for once.”

For a moment, there is relief. Then comes unease. The deadline is still unresolved, and now there is a difficult exchange to address.

“I need to stop being reactive” gives you little to work with. Looking closely at this one episode can reveal more: the circumstances, thoughts, sensations, and smaller actions that came before the message—and the consequences that followed it.

## Understand the skill

Dialectical Behavior Therapy, or DBT, teaches **Behavior Chain Analysis** as a way to examine a specific behavior and the conditions surrounding it. The behavior you want to understand is called the **target behavior**. It is an action, such as sending a sharp reply, rather than a judgment such as “being a terrible colleague.”

The chain includes a prompting event, background conditions that made responding harder, intermediate links, and consequences. Links can include events, interpretations, feelings, body sensations, urges, and actions. An urge to shout and actually shouting are different links.

**Solution analysis** uses this account to identify possible changes: a different response at a particular point, preparation before the next episode, and repair afterward where needed. Understanding and responsibility can belong together.

The chain is a working account. Remembering that two things happened in sequence does not prove that one caused the other. You can mark uncertainty rather than filling gaps with a convincing story. [Linehan's General Handouts 6–7A](https://www.guilford.com/add/linehan7_old/lin-p-orientation.pdf?t=1) provide the established framework; the wording and interactive format here are original adaptations.

## When to use it

Try this after a manageable episode you want to understand: interrupting someone, sending a message you regret, or abandoning a task after frustration. Choose one occurrence rather than trying to explain your whole history.

It helps to have enough steadiness to look back and enough detail to describe at least part of what happened. If you are still in the middle of an escalating exchange, attending to immediate safety or taking a pause may be more useful first.

For behavior involving serious harm, work with a qualified professional; immediate safety and necessary medical care come before analysis. Examining your own response never makes you responsible for someone else's abuse or mistreatment. You can recognize a harmful environment and consider protective action alongside your own choices.

## How to practice

The established sequence has eight parts. You can return to earlier entries as you remember more. [DBT Self Help's chain-analysis guide](https://dbtselfhelp.com/target-behaviors-behavior-chain-analysis/) describes these components.

1. **Define the target behavior.** Describe what you actually did, with relevant words or actions. Replace “I lost it” with something observable.
2. **Identify the prompting event.** What happened just before the chain began? Separate an occurrence from your interpretation of its meaning.
3. **Notice vulnerability factors.** Consider relevant background conditions: fatigue, illness, ongoing stress, competing demands, or limited support. These provide context without excusing harm.
4. **Trace the links.** Move through the episode in small pieces. Include thoughts, feelings, sensations, urges, and actions. Mark gaps and uncertain ordering.
5. **Describe consequences.** What followed immediately and later? Include anything that briefly became easier as well as costs. Short-lived relief can help explain a behavior's appeal.
6. **Find alternatives at specific links.** Match a concrete response to a recognizable point. “Ask when it is needed” is more usable than “communicate better.”
7. **Plan prevention.** Consider preparation, support, or environmental changes that could make the next episode easier to handle.
8. **Consider repair.** Identify practical, proportionate steps to address consequences of your behavior where needed.

## See it in action

In the message example, the target is sending the sharp reply. The prompting event is the request for the revised file. Fatigue and competing deadlines are background conditions.

The remembered links are: “They don't notice how much I'm doing”; a clenched jaw; frustration; opening the reply box; typing, deleting, and typing again. Some of these may have overlapped. The person leaves the exact order uncertain.

The immediate consequence was relief. Later came unease and an unresolved deadline. Relief is worth noticing without treating it as the entire explanation. Clinical teaching examples distinguish these immediate and delayed consequences. [Veritas Collaborative's chain and solution analysis key](https://www.naadac.org/assets/2416/patient_vignette_-_key_for_chain_analysis_and_solution_analysis.pdf) illustrates that distinction.

Two possible intervention points emerge. When interpreting the request as a demand, the person could ask, “What time do you need it?” When repeatedly typing and deleting, they could pause before sending and prepare a factual response: “I can finish this tomorrow morning. Is that workable?”

Prevention might involve clarifying priorities earlier in the day. Repair might sound like: “My reply was sharp, and I'm sorry. We still need to clarify the deadline.” The colleague may remain annoyed. The analysis offers choices; it cannot guarantee an easy conversation.

## Try it yourself

The accompanying **Map the Episode, Plan the Intervention** practice takes about 15–20 minutes. Use a manageable personal episode, keep details in your own notes, or explore the fictional message example.

You will build a working sequence, distinguish immediate and later consequences, and choose an intervention, a prevention step, and any appropriate repair. You will leave with your own notes and a cue for a possible next action. Gaps can remain open. This self-guided experience is an original adaptation, rather than an official DBT worksheet or a complete treatment session.

## When it feels difficult

**“I can't remember all the links.”** Begin with the moment immediately before the target behavior, then work backward or forward. Keep a question mark beside a guess. Your smallest step might be recording one remembered thought. The aim is a useful account, not a perfect reconstruction. [Eric Gadol's teaching handouts](https://cls.unc.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/3019/2015/06/Handouts.pdf) explicitly allow uncertain links to be marked.

**“This turns into criticizing myself.”** Return to observable wording: “I closed the document” instead of “I always give up.” Include context and any moments when you tried to respond differently. Start by rewriting just one judgment as an action. You can acknowledge impact without making the episode a verdict on your character.

**“I understand the chain but still don't know what to do.”** Choose one recognizable link and one realistic alternative. If the behavior briefly provided escape from pressure, an alternative may need to provide a legitimate break. Start with a cue and a sentence, then ask what support would make it possible.

## Bring it into daily life

Choose one small intervention to test during an ordinary situation. Afterward, notice whether you recognized the cue, what you tried, and what needs adjusting. A plan that proved unrealistic gives you information about the conditions it needs.

Where was there a realistic opening for a different response, and what would make that response easier?

“When I notice myself typing and deleting an angry reply, I can try pausing and clarifying the request before sending.”

## Practice — Map the Episode, Plan the Intervention

Allow 15–20 minutes. Leave with a working chain and a specific intervention plan. No AI is used. All personal text is optional; you can use the example or keep details in your own notes. The runner's pause and end controls remain available throughout. Ending the run does not itself erase answers already entered.
