A person knows exactly what they want to say: they do not want another task added to their workload. In the conversation, however, their voice becomes apologetic, their body leans forward, and they offer to do part of the task anyway. The verbal boundary exists, but expressing it brings a conflicting experience.

## Core idea

Embodying a Boundary explores how a limit is sensed and expressed through distance, posture, gesture, movement, and voice. In Sensorimotor Psychotherapy, this can become a collaborative experiment in what makes a boundary feel available, difficult, or costly.

There is no universal bodily shape for assertiveness. A person can set a clear limit while seated, speaking softly, using communication aids, or moving very little. A theatrical push or commanding voice is not inherently more authentic.

The defining move is to connect a limit with a lived sense of distance, contact, and action. A verbal boundary may be completely clear while its expression evokes danger, guilt, or loss of connection. Exploring those responses can make the limit more inhabitable, without requiring a standard posture or an emotionally forceful performance.

## What changes in the experience

Another person may need a change in physical distance before they can think clearly. Asking the therapist to move a chair can provide a direct experience of preference being recognized. The practitioner must actually respect the request rather than interpret it as avoidance.

Imagine a client who can extend a hand in a stopping gesture but immediately smiles and retracts it. One possibility is a conflict between protection and belonging. Another is uncertainty about the experiment, discomfort with the therapist, or a cultural style of softening requests.

The movement becomes useful when it invites the person's account. They may notice, "I can say stop if I know you won't be offended." That condition gives the relational work a focus. The therapist's response to the limit is part of the experiment's meaning.

A boundary can also concern approach. Someone may want more contact but need control over its pace. Embodiment includes sensing a chosen yes, a pause, or a change of mind, rather than treating all boundaries as pushing away.

The person may discover a preferred amount of distance, a pause before answering, or a way of keeping warmth while remaining clear. Feedback concerns both the limit and the relationship: can the person sense what they want, communicate it, and retain choice when the other responds? Bodily steadiness does not guarantee that the recipient will respect the request.

## A worked example

In the workload example, the important discovery may be that declining a task feels like withdrawing care. The person leans forward to preserve connection. Exploring a steadier seated position might clarify that conflict, but the actual limit also requires an agreement about work. Posture cannot solve an exploitative workload.

Suppose the therapist encourages the person to lean back while declining the task. The person feels cold and unlike themselves. They want to remain engaged with their colleague, not signal withdrawal. The experiment changes: they keep the forward orientation but let the refusal stand without adding an apology or an offer to do the work later.

This feels more recognizable, but guilt appears. They can now discuss the belief that being caring means remaining available. The outcome is not perfect bodily congruence. It is a clearer distinction between maintaining contact and accepting another obligation. In the actual workplace, they may still need a discussion of workload, authority, and the consequences of refusal.

## Why the move may help

A lived sense of a limit can make preferences more recognizable before a conversation has already become overwhelming. It can also reveal where a verbal plan conflicts with anticipated consequences. These are possible contributions to awareness and agency, not proof that a particular posture changes the nervous system in a predictable way.

A boundary can remain abstract until the person encounters what it feels like to express it. Attention to that encounter can reveal the feared interpersonal cost or the bodily conditions that support choice. It can also make an early preference noticeable before a conversation has become too demanding.

Sensorimotor approaches use bodily exploration as part of broader therapeutic work. A firmer gesture in a session does not establish lasting assertiveness or demonstrate that future violations will be prevented. The immediate observation is whether the person can recognize and shape a limit more accurately, with practical conditions still in view.

## When it helps—and what can get in the way

The pattern is relevant when someone can explain a boundary but struggles to express it, notices conflict within an action, or has difficulty identifying comfortable distance. It may inform later Role Training, where an actual anticipated encounter is rehearsed.

This fits a discrepancy the person recognizes between knowing a limit and being able to express it. It may be less useful when the main difficulty is that the other person ignores clear refusals. Further refinement of posture can wrongly place responsibility on the person whose boundary is being violated. External support or a different arrangement may be the relevant change.

## Boundaries and common misunderstandings

Failure to express a boundary is not evidence of deficient embodiment. A person may realistically fear retaliation, financial consequences, or violence. Changing posture does not remove those risks. Protective planning and practical support may matter more than an expressive experiment.

Practitioners should not approach someone repeatedly to test where their boundary appears without clear agreement. A refusal does not need to become stronger before it counts. Consent can be expressed verbally, nonverbally, or by an established accessible method.

Boundaries can include consent to approach, uncertainty, and a request for time. A chosen yes is not less embodied than a no. The practitioner should not treat fear as invalidating a refusal, or wait for a sufficiently strong gesture before respecting it. A quiet or ambivalent limit still deserves a response.
