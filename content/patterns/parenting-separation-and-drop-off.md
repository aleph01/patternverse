## 1. A recognizable situation

Your three-year-old talks happily about nursery over breakfast. At the entrance, they grip your coat.

“Don’t go.”

You crouch down, explain when you will return, and offer a hug. They begin crying harder. A staff member reaches out while you watch the clock: you are already late for work.

“They’ll be fine once you leave,” someone says.

Perhaps they will. But you need to know what “fine” means: whether your child accepts comfort, connects with someone, and eventually plays—or remains distressed long after the goodbye.

**A helpful response addresses both sides of separation: a predictable departure and dependable care afterward.** The moment at the door cannot tell you everything.

## 2. What might be happening

Several different difficulties can produce clinging, crying, anger, silence, or physical complaints.

- **Being apart is itself difficult.** Does your child struggle across several safe settings, follow you around at home, or worry that something will happen to you?

- **The receiving caregiver is not yet familiar enough.** A child may cope well with one educator but struggle when greeted by someone different. Observe who can comfort them and how that relationship is developing.

- **Something about the destination is difficult.** Noise, bullying, toileting, unfamiliar language, learning demands, or harsh interactions can make leaving you especially frightening. Does distress concentrate around one room, person, lesson, or day?

- **The transition is unpredictable.** Rushed arrivals, changing entrances, uncertain collection arrangements, or adults repeatedly leaving and returning may make it hard to understand what happens next.

- **Their capacity is temporarily reduced.** Poor sleep, illness, a long break, moving home, bereavement, or parental illness may change how much support they need.

- **A repeated avoidance pattern has developed.** For an older child, staying close to a parent may bring immediate relief while making future separation feel harder. This is a possible maintaining process, not evidence that the child is deliberately controlling the morning.

Ask what happens throughout the day. Can the child accept comfort? Do they eat, communicate, explore, and join activities? Are there repeated episodes of distress, or mainly one difficult transition?

For school-age children, stomachaches and headaches can accompany anxiety without being consciously produced. Physical symptoms still deserve appropriate medical assessment; their timing does not prove that they are “just anxiety.” [AAP guidance on school avoidance](https://www.healthychildren.org/english/health-issues/conditions/emotional-problems/pages/school-avoidance.aspx)

## 3. How development changes the picture

**In infancy and toddlerhood**, separation distress is often developmentally expected. The NHS describes it as common from roughly six months to three years, sometimes lasting longer or returning during stress. Crying at separation alone does not establish an anxiety disorder or tell you whether the parent–child relationship is secure. [NHS guidance](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/babys-development/behaviour/separation-anxiety/)

A young child needs another adult to provide comfort and care. Explaining that you will return may help, but understanding the words does not mean they can manage the feeling alone.

**Preschoolers can understand more of the sequence**, especially when time is described through recognizable events: “After lunch and rest time.” They can help choose a goodbye routine, but should not have to decide whether their parent may go to work. The adults remain responsible for making the arrangement workable.

**Primary-school children can often help identify a particular fear and plan a response.** They may worry about your safety, getting lost, or being unable to find help. Persistent difficulty across separations deserves closer assessment, rather than assuming they should have outgrown it.

**In older children and adolescents**, apparent separation distress may involve worries about a parent, but it can also reflect bullying, panic, depression, learning difficulties, or an overwhelming school environment. Discuss concerns privately and involve them in planning. A toddler-style handover is unlikely to meet their needs.

Communication, sensory, learning, and emotional abilities matter alongside chronological age.

## 4. What needs balancing

The child needs comfort, trustworthy information, and access to adults who can help. They also need opportunities to build relationships and function away from their main caregiver.

Parents may need to work, attend appointments, or care for other family members. Educators have responsibilities to the child and the group. A plan that requires unlimited parental presence or intensive one-to-one staffing may be unavailable.

Those constraints should shape the support, rather than end the discussion. If shorter introductory days are impossible, a familiar greeting adult, a quieter entrance, and a clear communication plan may still be feasible.

The question is: **“What support makes this separation manageable, and how will we know whether it is working?”**

Necessary attendance and emotional wellbeing are both responsibilities. Neither a tear-free doorway nor attendance alone is a sufficient measure of success.

## 5. Possible responses

The approaches below combine professional guidance, treatment evidence, and practical synthesis. The suggested scripts and arrangements have not each been independently tested.

### A. Strengthen the relationship on the receiving side

**This fits when the setting or caregiver is unfamiliar, or the child struggles to obtain comfort after you leave.**

Arrange introductions when possible. Let the caregiver learn what helps your child: being held, sitting nearby, hearing a familiar song, looking at a book, or having space before joining others.

Ask concrete questions:

> “Who will receive him tomorrow? What will you do if he keeps crying? How will we review whether he is settling?”

Support continuity where staffing permits, with a backup adult for absences. For a younger child, familiarity with the person may matter more than enthusiasm for the activities.

NAEYC guidance, contributed by ZERO TO THREE, recommends close caregiver support, preparation visits, and partnership with families. These are professional recommendations, not evidence that one introduction schedule works for everyone. [NAEYC guidance](https://www.naeyc.org/resources/pubs/yc/jul2020/rocking-rolling)

The research on ordinary childcare transitions remains limited. A 2025 scoping review identified 20 studies of children under three, largely descriptive or examining associations, with varied methods and predominantly European settings. It did not establish a best settling-in schedule and did not assess study quality. [Review of childcare transitions](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-7102/15/5/589)

### B. Make the goodbye clear, affectionate, and predictable

**This fits when suitable care is available and the departure itself has become prolonged or confusing.**

Explain the sequence beforehand. At arrival, help the child connect with the receiving adult, give them your attention, and say when you will return in terms they understand.

> “One hug, then you’ll sit with Sam and choose a book. I’ll collect you after afternoon snack.”

Once the caregiver has taken over, leave according to the agreed plan. Avoid repeatedly restarting the goodbye or slipping away unnoticed.

The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends brief, consistent goodbye routines, affection, and understandable information about return. This is professional guidance; it does not establish an ideal number of minutes or justify leaving without a workable handover. [AAP separation guidance](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/toddler/Pages/Soothing-Your-Childs-Separation-Anxiety.aspx)

A brief goodbye can follow substantial preparation. It should not mean “hurry away regardless of what is happening.”

Agree with staff when they will update you and when continuing distress requires contact or a revised plan. A nursery app can support that communication, but a smiling photograph cannot summarize the whole day.

### C. Practise manageable separations when fear is the main obstacle

**This fits when the child has appropriate support but being apart remains unusually difficult.**

Choose a small, predictable separation with a trusted adult. Tell the child what will happen, return as arranged, and review how it went. Extend the challenge gradually as experience supports doing so.

For a toddler, this might involve a familiar caregiver taking over a short activity. For a school-age child, it might involve staying with a known adult while you step outside, then progressing toward a necessary school transition.

Practice should help the child experience support and manage separation; it should not become a surprise test or require them to stop feeling sad before it counts.

There is causal evidence for **complete treatments** when separation anxiety is clinically significant. In a small randomized trial, 43 children aged five to seven with diagnosed separation anxiety disorder received either a 16-session family CBT program or waited for treatment. The program improved separation-related distress, impairment, and diagnostic outcomes, but follow-up was only four weeks. It cannot establish that any individual goodbye technique caused improvement. [Schneider and colleagues, 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21494062/)

### D. Make a joint plan when school participation is affected

**This fits when distress produces repeated absences, frequent departures, or substantial difficulty remaining at school.**

If distress is leading to repeated absence or difficulty remaining at school, continue with [[parenting-school-reluctance-and-refusal|Reluctance or refusal to attend school]] for a fuller participation and return plan.

Bring together the child, family, school, and a clinician where needed. Specify:

- Who meets the child and where they go first.

- What support is available when distress or physical symptoms arise.

- Which environmental problems require action.

- What participation is currently manageable and when the plan will be reviewed.

If reduced attendance forms part of a supported return, define its purpose and review point rather than leaving it open-ended. Include arrangements for learning and peer connection.

AACAP recommends coordinated assessment and support, including attention to bullying, learning needs, family changes, and medical causes. [AACAP school-refusal guidance](https://www.aacap.org/AACAP/AACAP/Families_and_Youth/Facts_for_Families/FFF-Guide/School-Refusal-007.aspx)

Evidence supports cautious expectations: a meta-analysis of eight school-refusal studies found improvements in attendance but no statistically significant short-term effect on anxiety. The studies included randomized and nonrandomized designs. Getting through the entrance and feeling better are related goals, but improvement in one does not guarantee improvement in the other. [Maynard and colleagues, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1177/1049731515598619)

## 6. Examples in practice

**A sixteen-month-old cries during handover.**  
A familiar caregiver receives them, offers comfort, and stays close. Later, staff describe the child accepting cuddles, exploring, eating, and returning for reassurance between activities.

You continue the predictable handover while reviewing their experience. Progress may appear first in accepting the caregiver’s help, before crying at departure decreases.

**A five-year-old’s planned start fails.**  
You have visited the classroom and agreed on a goodbye routine. On the first morning, the named teacher is absent. Your child remains distressed for much of the session.

“Be more consistent” does not address the broken arrangement. You meet staff, establish a backup adult, and revise the introduction. If prolonged distress continues, you seek additional advice rather than repeating the same plan indefinitely.

**An eleven-year-old clings at the school gate.**  
Initially, everyone assumes separation anxiety. But they stay comfortably with relatives and attend weekend activities. In a private conversation, they describe humiliation during a particular lesson.

You work with school staff to address that treatment and arrange a supportive arrival. Separation practice alone would leave the central problem untouched.

## 7. Common mistakes and limitations

**Measuring only how quickly crying stops.**  
A quiet child may be engaged and comfortable, or withdrawn and unable to seek help. Ask about connection, play, communication, eating, and participation.

**Treating distress as proof that childcare is harmful—or harmless.**  
The doorway cannot establish either conclusion. Investigate the quality of care and the child’s experience over time.

**Turning “keep it brief” into “never reconsider.”**  
A predictable goodbye is useful only within an adequate care arrangement. Persistent distress, unsuitable support, or a child’s report of frightening treatment requires review.

**Demanding an impossible promise.**  
“Nothing bad will happen” cannot be guaranteed. Give reliable information about who is responsible and how changes will be handled.

**Blaming parental emotion.**  
A steady manner may help, but parents do not need to perform cheerfulness perfectly. Their worry can also identify something that needs attention.

**Assuming more parent involvement always improves treatment.**  
In a randomized study of 64 children aged eight to thirteen, separation-specific family CBT and a general child-focused anxiety program both produced improvement, without significant differences in diagnostic outcomes through one year. This does not prove the treatments identical, but it limits claims that one family approach is necessarily superior. [Schneider and colleagues, 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23607501/)

Evidence about ordinary drop-off routines and their long-term effects on trust or attachment is much thinner than evidence about clinical anxiety treatment.

## 8. What to observe over time

Look beyond the departure:

- Can the child increasingly accept comfort from another adult?

- Do they explore, play, learn, and seek help?

- Are periods of distress becoming more manageable?

- Is anticipatory worry affecting less of the evening or morning?

- Do sleep, appetite, attendance, and enjoyment remain broadly intact?

- Can the child express what helps or describe a specific problem?

Agree on an early review with the caregiver or school. Compare observations across several sessions rather than judging from one particularly good or bad morning.

Seek advice sooner when distress is severe, the child remains upset for long periods after departure, or daily functioning is deteriorating. The NHS recommends seeking support for substantial or prolonged separation distress; you do not need to wait through weeks of an evidently unworkable arrangement. [NHS guidance](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/babys-development/behaviour/separation-anxiety/)

For school-age children, repeated absence, significant physical complaints, fears about harm to a parent, or inability to manage separations across settings warrant assessment. New symptoms and reports of harmful treatment deserve investigation even when anxiety is already present.
