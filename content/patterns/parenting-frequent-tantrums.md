## 1. A recognizable situation

It is time to leave the playground. Your three-year-old wants another turn. You give a warning, offer a choice of routes home, and explain that dinner is waiting. They drop to the ground, scream, and kick when you approach.

You have tried several reasonable things. Your child is still upset, you need to leave, and another child needs your attention.

When this happens repeatedly, the question becomes larger than how to end this particular outburst: **What is making these situations so difficult, and what response will help your child become better able to handle them?**

Those questions require looking at both the child’s experience and the interaction that develops around it. A tantrum can involve genuine distress and a learned way of influencing what happens next. These can coexist.

## 2. What might be happening

“Tantrum” describes an outburst. It does not identify its cause. Before choosing a response, look for patterns in what happens before, during, and afterward.

| Possible contributor | Observations that might help distinguish it |
|---|---|
| **Hunger, tiredness, illness, or discomfort** | Outbursts cluster before meals, after poor sleep, or during particular physical activities. |
| **Difficulty communicating** | The child struggles to make a request, explain a problem, or understand what is expected. |
| **An overwhelming or unpredictable situation** | Noise, crowded spaces, sudden changes, or complicated instructions repeatedly precede distress. |
| **Frustration or loss of control** | Outbursts often follow interrupted play, blocked goals, or adults taking over. |
| **A pattern that has become effective** | Screaming repeatedly results in a purchase, prolonged negotiation, or escape from a particular demand. |

These are hypotheses to investigate, not labels to assign. The NHS identifies hunger, fatigue, frustration, and difficulty expressing feelings as common contributors in toddlers. [NHS guidance](https://www.nhs.uk/baby/babys-development/behaviour/temper-tantrums/)

Communication deserves particular attention. A study of 2,001 children aged 12–38 months found an association between smaller expressive vocabularies and more severe tantrums. Because the study was observational and relied on parent reports, it cannot establish that language difficulties caused the tantrums—or that language support alone would resolve them. [Manning and colleagues, 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7946112/)

For autistic children, NICE specifically recommends assessing communication, pain, physical and social environments, changes in routine, and other possible contributors before choosing an intervention. That guidance concerns a particular population; it also illustrates why a behavior-only explanation can miss important information. [NICE assessment guidance](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/QS51/chapter/quality-statement-7-assessing-possible-triggers-for-behaviour-that-challenges)

A practical starting point is a few brief notes about recurring episodes: what preceded them, what the child did, how adults responded, and how recovery happened. Include occasions when the same situation went well. Those comparisons may be more informative than the most dramatic incident.

## 3. How development changes the picture

Development affects both what an outburst might mean and what help is realistic.

| Developmental stage | How the response changes |
|---|---|
| **Toddlers, roughly 18 months–3 years** | Use short, concrete language and immediate support. A child may understand a rule while still struggling greatly to tolerate frustration or communicate a need. |
| **Preschool years, roughly 3–5** | Begin rehearsing simple alternatives outside the outburst: asking for help, requesting a turn, or using a familiar transition routine. Expect practice and adult assistance. |
| **School age and beyond** | Involve the child more in identifying recurring difficulties and planning responses. Frequent or severe outbursts deserve closer assessment rather than assuming they are an extended toddler phase. |

Tantrums generally become less common as children grow, but there is no birthday after which all outbursts become abnormal. AACAP emphasizes that recurrent or severe school-age outbursts, and aggression or substantial disruption at any age, warrant attention. [AACAP guidance](https://www.aacap.org/AACAP/Families_and_Youth/Facts_for_Families/FFF-Guide/Temper_Tantrums-136.aspx)

Frequency is also only part of the picture. Research in young children examines duration, destructiveness, triggers, and recovery alongside how often tantrums occur. Parent-report studies find considerable variation; they do not provide a universal stopwatch threshold for diagnosing an individual child. [Wakschlag and colleagues, 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22928674/); [Van den Akker and colleagues, 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9462137/)

Chronological age is a starting point. Language abilities, developmental differences, stress, and the demands of the setting affect what a child can manage.

## 4. What needs balancing

Several legitimate needs meet in a tantrum:

- The child needs safety, emotional acceptance, and help with a difficult experience.

- The parent must protect people, meet practical obligations, and decide which limits are necessary.

- The child needs opportunities to influence their life and gradually develop more effective ways to respond.

**Accepting anger does not require granting the request that triggered it.** You can acknowledge disappointment about leaving while continuing to leave.

Supporting autonomy means taking the child’s perspective seriously, explaining reasons they can understand, and offering meaningful influence where possible. Research on autonomy-supportive parenting treats this as compatible with clear structure. [Parenting and self-determination theory](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2008_JoussemetLandryKoestner_CanPsych.pdf)

The parent’s judgment also remains relevant. If you discover that shoes hurt or an instruction was misunderstood, changing the plan can be appropriate. Explain what changed. Consistency should make your responses understandable; it should leave room for new information.

## 5. Possible responses

The following approaches address different parts of the problem. The practical suggestions synthesize research and professional guidance; individual phrases and sequences have generally not been tested as separate interventions.

During an outburst, start with B. Once everyone is settled, use A to examine predictable pressures and C to teach an alternative. D helps review recurring interactions; it does not replace comfort or practical support.

### A. Change the conditions that repeatedly precede the outburst

This fits when episodes cluster around predictable situations.

For an exhausted toddler, a shorter shopping trip and earlier meal may be more useful than another explanation about behavior. For transitions, prepare what comes next, use a cue the child understands, and avoid unnecessary waiting. NAEYC recommends these adjustments for early-childhood transitions. [NAEYC guidance](https://www.naeyc.org/resources/pubs/yc/sep2018/reducing-challenging-behaviors-during-transitions/)

A warning is a possible support, not a guarantee. Research on transition difficulties has mixed findings: advance notice may address uncertainty but do little about an unpleasant destination or the loss of a highly preferred activity. Much of that research concerns people with intellectual or developmental disabilities, limiting generalization. [Review of advance-notice interventions](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4711751/)

The useful question is what the preparation changes for this child. Repeating a countdown more firmly adds little if unpredictability was never the main difficulty.

### B. During intense distress, make safety and recovery manageable

Move dangerous objects or other children away. Reduce your own volume and the amount of language. State the immediate limit briefly: “I won’t let you hit.” Offer your presence without requiring conversation.

Some children welcome closeness; others need less interaction. You can remain available while giving space. Once they can engage, acknowledge what happened and discuss alternatives. The CDC recommends waiting until a distressed toddler or preschooler is calm before attempting emotion coaching. This is professional guidance, not proof that a particular comforting phrase shortens tantrums. [CDC emotion-coaching guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/parenting-toddlers/noticing-and-naming/emotion-coaching.html)

The boundary may still need to be carried out. Emotional recovery and agreement with the decision are separate outcomes.

### C. Teach a small alternative outside the difficult moment

Choose something that addresses the recurring problem:

- A child frustrated by a difficult toy might practice signaling “help.”

- A preschooler might rehearse asking for another turn.

- An older child might help agree on how to request a brief break and return to a task.

The alternative must be within the child’s abilities and useful to them. A request for help should reliably bring assistance; it need not guarantee that every demand disappears.

There is evidence beyond immediate compliance for broader emotion-focused parenting programs. A 2023 meta-analysis covering 15 randomized trials, with children aged 18 months to nearly seven years, found improvements in emotional competence, parenting practices, and behavioral adjustment. Those findings concern programs containing multiple elements. They do not establish that naming feelings alone prevents tantrums. [England-Mason and colleagues, 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36706555/)

### D. Examine what happens after the behavior—and get coaching when needed

When the same interaction persists, consider whether adult responses inadvertently make the unwanted behavior more effective than an acceptable alternative.

If a reasonable purchase limit repeatedly changes only after prolonged screaming, that pattern may encourage escalation. A plan could preserve the purchasing decision, reduce repetitive argument, and respond promptly when the child communicates more effectively.

This does **not** justify blanket ignoring. The CDC limits planned ignoring to minor behaviors suited to that approach and advises against it for danger, unmet physical needs, or distress requiring support. [CDC guidance on redirecting attention](https://www.cdc.gov/parenting-toddlers/responding-to-behavior/ignoring.html)

Some experimental evidence supports brief ignoring and time-out for increasing compliance, but compliance is a narrower outcome than emotional regulation or trust. Those studies cannot answer every question about responding to distress. [Meta-analysis of discrete parenting behaviors](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30289928/)

For persistent difficulties, structured parent coaching has stronger evidence than any single script. A 2026 review found that parent-focused and multicomponent interventions reduced disruptive behavior immediately after treatment in preschool and school-age children. Longer-term findings were less consistent, and the trials primarily concerned substantial disruptive behavior rather than ordinary occasional tantrums. [Selph and colleagues, 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41525996/)

## 6. Examples in practice

**The playground departure**

A two-year-old screams when it is time to leave. The parent says once, “You wanted more swings. We’re going home.” They focus on leaving safely and remain available for comfort. The child continues crying.

Later, the parent notices that departures are much harder when lunch is late. They move the visit earlier and introduce a simple, repeatable ending. The first attempt did not produce calm cooperation; it still allowed the parent to maintain the necessary decision and learn something about the conditions.

**The timer that makes things worse**

A five-year-old explodes whenever a tablet timer sounds. Adding more warnings has not helped. During a calm conversation, the parent discovers that stopping mid-level feels especially frustrating.

They try starting only an activity that can reasonably finish within the available period and turning off automatic continuation. The child still protests sometimes. The parent keeps the stopping arrangement while helping them through disappointment. This is an illustrative environmental adjustment, not evidence that a particular timer or app setting treats tantrums.

**The “homework tantrum”**

An eight-year-old shouts and tears a worksheet most evenings. The parent initially treats it as refusal. Looking at the work together later reveals that reading the instructions is difficult and embarrassing.

They involve the teacher, adjust the task support, and agree on a way to signal difficulty before tearing begins. They also address the damage. The interpretation changed because the parent investigated the task, not because one universal response finally worked.

## 7. Common mistakes and limitations

**Turning validation into a demand for calm.** Repeating “I understand you’re upset” while waiting impatiently for the feeling to stop can become another argument. Recognition can be brief; the child may remain disappointed.

**Making every outburst a lesson.** Explanations, questions, apologies, and breathing instructions can all become additional demands during distress. Skills need rehearsal when the child can participate.

**Assuming a single cause.** Exhaustion and a learned interaction pattern can coexist. Addressing one may leave the other unchanged.

**Treating prevention as avoiding every frustration.** Preparation should make ordinary demands more manageable. It need not remove every limit or disappointing experience.

**Escalating a method that is not helping.** If a strategy repeatedly produces more distress or danger, reconsider the explanation and seek guidance rather than assuming greater intensity will make it work.

**Ignoring family constraints.** A plan that requires unlimited time, a quiet room, or two available adults may be unusable. School routines, work schedules, crowded housing, and digital products can contribute to the difficulty. A useful plan identifies feasible changes beyond the parent’s wording.

These are practical cautions drawn from the combined evidence, rather than independently established rules.

## 8. What to observe over time

Look beyond whether the child obeyed immediately. Useful observations include:

- How often episodes happen and whether they involve injury or destruction.

- How recovery happens and how much help is needed.

- Whether the child increasingly signals difficulty or uses an alternative.

- Whether family activities remain possible.

- Whether connection resumes afterward without requiring the child to deny their feelings.

Compare similar situations over time. A quieter child who has stopped asking for help is not sufficient evidence of improved regulation.

Seek assessment when outbursts repeatedly endanger anyone, include self-injury, are prolonged and difficult to recover from, substantially disrupt school or friendships, or are recurrent and severe in a school-age child. Persistent irritability between episodes also matters. These signs invite assessment; they do not identify a diagnosis. If immediate safety cannot be maintained, seek urgent local help. [AACAP guidance](https://www.aacap.org/AACAP/Families_and_Youth/Facts_for_Families/FFF-Guide/Temper_Tantrums-136.aspx)

A sudden change should also prompt attention to new physical, emotional, or environmental difficulties. Parents can seek support because the situation has become unmanageable, without first exhausting every home strategy.

**Useful response concepts**

- **Emotion coaching:** addresses emotional understanding and expression.

- **Predictable routines:** helps with recurring situations and transitions.

- **Collaborative problem-solving:** becomes useful when a child can help explain a specific difficulty.

- **Repair after conflict:** applies when either person’s response has damaged the interaction.

The evidence supports several complementary routes, with different degrees of certainty: developmental studies describe patterns and associations; professional guidance helps translate knowledge into immediate care; randomized trials support structured parenting interventions. Evidence for a program does not validate every component separately, and fewer outbursts do not, by themselves, demonstrate gains in trust or independence.
