**Pattern 14 of 49 · Part 3: Notes, Keys, and Chords**

**Use when:** You can find individual notes, but choosing the next notes of a melody still feels like guessing across the whole keyboard.

A writer finds a short phrase on the white keys, then moves it to start on D. Following the next white keys changes the sound of the phrase. Keeping the original distances instead introduces F-sharp and C-sharp. The useful thing to carry is a pattern of steps, rather than a particular set of keyboard colors.

## The move

Choose a repeatable pattern of pitch distances to give a melody a coherent note palette.

Begin with C and follow the major-scale steps: whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half. The result is C–D–E–F–G–A–B–C. Starting the same steps on D produces D–E–F-sharp–G–A–B–C-sharp–D. These are two pitch levels of the same scale type. A short melody can use just a few of those notes; it does not need to run through the complete scale.

A scale supplies a starting palette, not a rule that every note outside it is wrong or every note inside it will work equally well.

## One step means a distance

A half step, also called a semitone, moves to the immediately neighboring keyboard pitch. C to C-sharp is one half step. E to F is also one half step, even though both keys are white. A whole step spans two half steps: C to D, or E to F-sharp. [[songwriting-pitch-and-intervals|Intervals and Melodic Shape]] explains how to locate and count these distances.

A scale arranges a collection of notes in pitch order. For the scales here, the upper note repeats the starting note one octave higher. The octave spans twelve semitones. The particular order of whole and half steps determines where the notes fall between those two versions of the starting pitch.

Major has seven distinct notes before that octave repeat. Its seven gaps add up to twelve semitones: 2 + 2 + 1 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 1. Memorizing all the note names can wait; understanding that the gaps form a repeatable shape is the first useful discovery.

## Build one major scale, then move its shape

Starting on C makes the whole pattern visible on the white keys. Starting on D reveals why that is a special convenience of C major rather than a definition of major.

| Step from the previous note | C-major note | D-major note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Starting note | C | D |
| Whole | D | E |
| Whole | E | F-sharp |
| Half | F | G |
| Whole | G | A |
| Whole | A | B |
| Whole | B | C-sharp |
| Half | C, one octave higher | D, one octave higher |

![Two major scales use the same whole- and half-step pattern, starting on C and D.](/diagrams/songwriting/scale-steps.svg 'The distances stay the same; F-sharp and C-sharp keep the D-major shape intact.')

In the player, **Major scale · same shape on C and D** gives each note one beat. The first eight beats ascend through C major; the next eight ascend through D major. The second run moves every note up two semitones. Nothing changes in its rhythm or step pattern. This is an original construction comparison, separate from the complete Porch Light song.

## Turn the collection into a phrase

Porch Light's seed E4–G4–A4 uses three notes from C major. Its durations are one, one, and two beats. E4 to G4 skips over F4, so a melody drawn from a scale does not have to move through adjacent scale notes.

Moving the complete seed up two semitones gives F-sharp4–A4–B4, now drawn from D major. F-sharp belongs there because it preserves the scale and melodic relationships. Using F natural would make a different gesture. Either could be a deliberate choice, but they should not be mistaken for the same move.

A palette helps narrow the search for a next note. Rhythm, repetition, register, and the ending still give the phrase its character. Two writers can use the same scale and produce very different melodies.

## Compare a second step pattern

Natural minor uses whole, half, whole, whole, half, whole, whole: 2–1–2–2–1–2–2. Starting on C gives C–D–E-flat–F–G–A-flat–B-flat–C. Compared with C major, three notes have changed: E, A, and B become E-flat, A-flat, and B-flat.

The player comparison **Scale color · C major and C natural minor** keeps the starting pitch, one-beat rhythm, and ascending direction fixed. Hear which gaps move. This is a comparison of two collections around C, not a claim that a scale name fixes the emotion of a song. Tempo, words, delivery, and accompaniment also matter.

For now, major and natural minor provide two useful starting shapes. Other minor forms and modes belong in later comparisons, once the difference between a note collection and its musical center is clear.

In Porch Light, the E–G–A seed leaves most of that collection unplayed and gives its last note more time. A useful listening question is which relationships survive when the phrase moves. ## A palette still needs a home

Using only C-major notes does not prove that C feels like home. The same collection also supplies A natural minor. Musical emphasis determines which note organizes a passage. The next pattern, [[songwriting-scales-and-tonal-home|Establish a Tonal Home]], isolates that decision through bass references and phrase endings.

Borrowing a chord or emphasizing a note outside the starting scale comes later. A scale is useful when it gives a writer an understandable starting relationship, and less useful when it becomes a reason to reject an intentional sound without hearing it.
