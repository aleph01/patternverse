*An editorial pattern drawn from Nonviolent Communication. The examples and dialogue are fictional teaching illustrations.*

“Please take more responsibility.”

The person hearing this may sincerely agree. Tomorrow they check the bills, tidy the hallway, and arrange a repair. You remain frustrated because what you wanted was for them to plan dinner without prompting.

A request can express something important and still leave the action almost entirely unspecified. Making it concrete closes the gap between a desired improvement and a response someone can recognize.

## Give the other person something answerable

NVC describes requests in terms of specific actions, expressed as what someone is being asked to do. Alex Censor’s guidance on effective requests distinguishes concrete action language from broad wishes, while stressing that wording alone cannot establish a cooperative intention. [Alex Censor, “Making Effective Requests”](https://www.nonviolentcommunication.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/MAKING-EFFECTIVE-REQUESTS.pdf)

A useful request usually identifies the action, the relevant situation or timing, and enough detail to tell whether you understood one another. It should be possible to answer, “Yes,” “No,” or “I could do this different version.”

“Be more considerate” leaves considerable interpretation. “Would you message me if you expect to be more than fifteen minutes late?” offers an action connected to a recognizable situation.

## Turning a recurring complaint into an agreement

Two housemates keep having the same argument about cleaning. One says the kitchen is constantly filthy; the other says they clean it all the time.

When they look at the actual disagreement, they discover different standards. One counts cooking surfaces and dishes. The other includes the floor and rubbish. Neither has knowingly agreed to the other’s full definition.

One housemate proposes:

> “On the nights you cook, would you wash the pans and wipe the hob before going to bed? I’m happy to take the bins out and sweep on Sundays.”

The other replies:

> “I can do the pans and hob. On Wednesdays I get home very late. Could I do those before leaving the next morning?”

The concrete proposal exposes the exact point that needs adjustment. A vague appeal to responsibility could have produced another week of mutual disappointment.

The agreement may still fail. If it does, both people can examine an identifiable commitment rather than reopen the question of who is fundamentally tidy or considerate.

## Ask for the next decision, not the entire future

At work, a project lead says, “I need everyone to communicate proactively.” Some people send daily summaries; others copy the whole team into every message. The additional communication creates noise without ensuring that risks arrive in time.

A more useful request might be:

> “If you think a task will miss its agreed date, would you flag it in the project channel as soon as you know, with the task name and what help you need?”

The lead can also ask for a decision now about a future action: “Can we agree today to use that approach for the next two weeks?” A request does not have to concern an action completed this minute. The immediate response can be a clear commitment about what happens later.

This distinction matters in long projects. “Promise never to surprise me again” is impossible to guarantee. “Will you tell me by Thursday whether the estimate has changed?” creates a specific checkpoint.

## Some requests seek understanding

Action does not always mean performing a task. Sometimes the next useful action is answering a question or clarifying what was heard.

After discussing an upsetting incident, someone might ask:

> “Before we decide what to do, would you tell me what you understood was difficult for me?”

That can expose a misunderstanding early. It can also become patronizing if delivered as an examination. An ordinary alternative is, “I’m not sure I explained the issue well. What’s your understanding of it?”

The intended action is clarification. The wording should fit the relationship and the moment.

## Common mistakes

**Asking someone to produce an internal state.** “Be interested,” “trust me,” or “stop being anxious” asks for something that may not be available on demand. Ask for a behavior that could support the desired experience, such as setting aside time to talk or sharing the information needed for a decision.

**Specifying everything except the important part.** A detailed request can still miss the actual concern. Asking for a nightly message will not necessarily address uncertainty about the relationship. Concrete action is useful when it serves a clearly understood purpose.

**Turning specificity into control.** There is a difference between agreeing on a result and prescribing every movement someone must make. Detail should establish shared understanding while leaving reasonable discretion over execution.

**Assuming silence means agreement.** A clear request still needs a response. If the answer is vague, clarify the commitment rather than treating politeness as acceptance.

## Clear requests reveal real constraints

This pattern helps with repeated disappointments, handovers, shared responsibilities, and conversations where someone says, “I don’t know what you want from me.”

It may also reveal that the requested action is too costly, impractical, or outside someone’s authority. That is useful information. A specific refusal often provides a better basis for planning than an enthusiastic agreement to an undefined wish.
