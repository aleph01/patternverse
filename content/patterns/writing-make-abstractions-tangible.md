Readers can agree with a general statement and still understand it in different ways. A proposal promises flexibility, a service offers independence, or a team values accountability. The words may be familiar, yet readers cannot tell what would happen differently if the promise were kept.

This pattern helps when a definition is easy to repeat but difficult to recognize in practice, when people appear to agree while imagining different arrangements, or when an explanation never reaches a situation the reader can inspect. **Connect the abstract idea to a concrete instance, consequence, or contrast, and make the connection explicit.**

The aim is movement between levels. The general idea tells readers what the particulars have in common. The particulars show what that idea includes, excludes, or changes. Neither level has to replace the other.

## Give the general claim a situation to work in

An abstract term gathers many possible cases under one label. That economy becomes a problem when the differences among those cases matter to the explanation.

Imagine an essay about an invented workplace's promise of “flexible scheduling.” Its first draft says:

> Flexible scheduling allows working arrangements to adapt to changing needs. It replaces rigid patterns with a more responsive approach to organizing time.

The second sentence mostly redescribes the first. Readers still do not know whose needs can change, who can alter the schedule, or how much notice anyone receives.

The writer could introduce two possible arrangements:

> In one arrangement, staff choose their shifts a month ahead, and a coordinator confirms the rota before the month begins. A worker can arrange a regular afternoon course around the chosen hours. In another, a manager can change the following week's shifts at short notice to match bookings. The organization can adapt its staffing, while workers may have to rearrange plans already made.

Both arrangements permit some change. They distribute control over that change differently. The example makes it possible to explain the distinction:

> “Flexible” can describe a worker's ability to choose hours or an organization's ability to vary them. An account of the policy needs to say who can make which changes, and with what notice.

The paragraph now gives the reader a way to inspect the promise. It does not establish which arrangement is best for every workplace, nor assume that all workers want the same thing. It identifies a question hidden inside an apparently shared term.

This is more than replacing a difficult word with a simpler one. _Flexible_ is ordinary vocabulary. The obstacle was the missing connection between the label and an arrangement of decisions and consequences.

## Explain why the example belongs

A concrete story can be vivid and still leave its conceptual work undone. Readers may remember a person or incident without understanding what it demonstrates.

In a separate hypothetical essay, a writer wants to explain “invisible work” in a volunteer film club. The club's annual screening appears to depend on the person who introduces the film. But the writer follows a coordinator who confirms the hall booking, checks that the captions display properly, sends reminders, and arranges for someone to return the borrowed projector.

Listing those actions gives the idea substance. The explanation still needs to identify what makes the work invisible in this account: the audience sees the screening, while much of the preparation leaves little trace when it succeeds. The concept concerns the relation between contribution and recognition, not merely whether a task happens out of sight.

A description of the coordinator's coat, crowded desk, or hurried lunch might make the person more tangible without clarifying that relation. A relevant example needs the features that carry the idea. Other details can remain if they serve the essay, but concreteness alone does not make them explanatory.

The writer can then return to the general term with a more usable meaning. Later references to invisible work no longer need to repeat the whole evening's preparation.

## Use a contrast to expose the boundary

An example is especially useful when readers can see why a nearby case differs. The distinction can prevent a broad concept from absorbing everything around it.

For the film-club essay, consider two further situations. The coordinator tests the captions before doors open; most audience members never learn that the check occurred. The projectionist receives a public thank-you and is named in the programme. Both contribute to the event. The difference, for this explanation, is how the contribution becomes visible and receives acknowledgment.

The contrast also reveals a limit. Being named in a programme does not prove that someone feels appreciated or receives fair treatment. The example isolates one aspect of recognition; it does not settle every question about the work.

The scheduling example uses the same explanatory move differently. Its two cases share a label but put control in different hands. The film-club cases share a purpose but differ in visibility. In each, the writer identifies the relationship that makes the comparison useful, rather than trusting an anecdote to explain itself.

## Keep illustration separate from proof

A plausible case can show how something might work. It cannot establish how often it happens. A film-club coordinator's experience does not show that every volunteer organization overlooks preparation, and a made-up rota cannot establish the effects of a real employment policy.

An explanatory example should therefore be identified according to its status. A hypothetical case explores a possibility. A documented case describes an occurrence. A representative claim needs a reason to regard the case as representative, and a broader causal claim needs appropriate supporting evidence.

This distinction matters because a vivid case can carry more emotional force than its evidential reach warrants. A single frustrating schedule might make an entire policy seem unreasonable before readers have seen its alternatives or constraints. The writer can preserve the example's clarity while stating what remains unestablished.

## Return to the idea when detail has done its work

Trying to make every sentence concrete can strand an explanation among particulars. Readers need general terms to compare situations, remember a distinction, and apply it elsewhere.

Once the scheduling arrangements are clear, a phrase such as _control over changes_ can efficiently name their central difference. Once the film-club work has been described, _recognition_ can connect the account to other settings. These abstractions now gather relationships the reader has seen rather than substituting for them.

Nor must an explanation begin with a story. A direct statement of the concept or problem may be the best orientation, followed by the example that makes its significance available. The useful sequence is the one that lets readers connect the particular to the intended idea without guessing why it was introduced.
