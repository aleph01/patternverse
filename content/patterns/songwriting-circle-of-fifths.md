**Pattern 18 of 49 · Part 3: Notes, Keys, and Chords**

**Use when:** The chorus is uncomfortable to sing even though its melodic shape and harmonic relationships already work.

A singer likes a song, but its verse sits too low to project clearly. The guitarist suggests comparing a version in D major with the original C-major setting. Nothing about the song's identity needs to disappear: the melody can keep its intervals, the chords can keep their relationships, and every note can move upward by two semitones. What changes is the pitch level. The circle of fifths helps organize that change, and explains why the new key brings two sharps with it.

## The move

Move melody and harmony together to a pitch level that suits the intended performer.

Find the phrase that is hardest in the actual voice. Move Porch Light from C to D as a controlled comparison: C–Am–F–G becomes D–Bm–G–A, and E4–G4–A4 becomes F-sharp4–A4–B4. Keep durations and relationships fixed. Sing both extremes of the song before committing. The circle below helps locate and spell related keys; it does not choose a key for the singer or prescribe the next chord. A comfortable chorus that makes the verse inaudibly low is still an unfinished decision.

Transposing the whole song does not solve an excessive internal range; check the low verse as well as the high chorus.

## Read the circle in two directions

The circle is a map of relationships between pitch centers and key signatures. It is useful for orientation, transposition, and planning some harmonic movements. It is not a device that chooses a good next chord on your behalf.

A perfect fifth spans seven semitones in the tuning used here. Starting at C and repeatedly moving up a fifth gives C, G, D, A, E, B, and F-sharp. Continue through the remaining pitch classes and, allowing enharmonic respelling, the sequence returns to C. Enharmonic means two names can refer to the same keyboard pitch: F-sharp and G-flat are the central example on our diagram.

Moving clockwise through the sharp major keys adds one sharp at each step. C major has none; G major has F-sharp; D major has F-sharp and C-sharp. Moving the other way from C introduces flats: F major has B-flat; B-flat major has B-flat and E-flat. Neighboring signatures differ by one accidental. They do not contain entirely different notes.

![A circle with major keys on the outside and relative minor keys inside, highlighting C, F, and G.](/diagrams/songwriting/circle-of-fifths.svg 'Neighboring key signatures differ by one accidental. Inner labels show relative minor keys, not a mandatory chord sequence.')

The inner ring pairs each major key with its relative minor. C major and A minor share a key signature. G major and E minor share one sharp. Relative does not mean identical: the sense of home and the emphasis placed on notes differ. Minor-key songs may also alter their sixth and seventh degrees. The shared signature is a starting description, not an exhaustive inventory of every note a song may use.

## Transpose Porch Light

The recurring example song's verse is eight bars long:

| Bars               | 1–2 | 3–4 | 5–6 | 7–8 |
| ------------------ | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| In C major         | C   | Am  | F   | G   |
| Chord relationship | I   | vi  | IV  | V   |
| In D major         | D   | Bm  | G   | A   |

Each chord lasts two bars. Roman numerals describe relationships to a key: I is built on its first scale degree, vi on its sixth, and so on. Lowercase indicates a minor triad. The new progression retains those relationships even though its chord names change.

The seed E4–G4–A4 becomes F-sharp4–A4–B4. All three notes move by the same distance. Merely replacing the opening C chord with D while leaving everything else untouched would be a reharmonization or alteration, not a complete transposition.

For a singer, upward may be the wrong direction. If the chorus is already too high, moving the whole song down can help. An instrument's convenient chord shapes do not determine the singer's comfortable key. Choose using the actual highest, lowest, and sustained notes of the song; the circle supplies a map, not a vocal-range judgment.

## Find a neighborhood without treating it as a rule

Around C major, F and G are adjacent major keys. Their tonic chords also happen to be IV and V in C. That makes the neighborhood easy to remember, but it does not mean all useful chords sit directly beside C, or that traveling clockwise automatically makes a coherent song.

For a complete diatonic chord palette, build a triad on every note of the scale: C, Dm, Em, F, G, Am, and B diminished. The circle can organize some relationships among them; it does not replace the construction. A borrowed F minor chord, introduced later, sits outside this basic palette and may still be exactly right for a particular line.

Another useful distinction concerns root motion. Dm–G–C moves through descending fifth relationships between roots, D to G to C when each move is heard as down a fifth or its octave equivalent. This is a harmonic pattern. Reading key signatures around a circle is a related map activity. A diagram alone does not tell us whether we are changing keys or moving chords within one key.

## When the map earns its place

The circle is particularly useful when a song needs transposition, when a key signature is unfamiliar, or when you want to understand why two keys share much of their material. It becomes less useful when the immediate problem is a flat lyric, a crowded rhythm, or a chorus without a distinctive melodic gesture. Those require different decisions.

It also reflects a particular musical system. This course uses twelve-tone equal temperament and common major/minor spellings; the map does not describe every tuning or musical tradition. Even within these styles, many songs create continuity through a repeating groove, a drone, timbre, or a strong melodic return rather than a tour of related keys.

The practical achievement is being able to explain the move. “We raised the song by a whole step and preserved its relationships” is more informative than “the circle says D comes next.”

For the scale and key-signature conventions behind this map, see Hamm and Hughes, [Major Scales, Scale Degrees, and Key Signatures](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/major-scales/). ## An die Musik: a song need not have one compulsory vocal key

Schubert's **An die Musik**, D. 547, is included in both the publisher's [high-voice](https://www.halleonard.com/product/1143/franz-schubert-15-selected-songs-high-voice) and [low-voice](https://www.halleonard.com/product/1144/franz-schubert-15-selected-songs-low-voice) collections. These are score editions with accompaniment resources, not a matched pair of sung performances. They make a useful practical point: the singer's comfortable range is part of preparing a song, rather than a test of loyalty to one pitch level.

The [Mutopia score and MIDI edition](https://www.mutopiaproject.org/cgibin/piece-info.cgi?id=1001) supplies a D-major reference. As an illustrative transposition, moving that setting down a whole tone would put its tonal home on C; both the voice and accompaniment must move by the same distance. This does not identify the key of the publisher's low-voice edition, which must be checked in that edition. In Porch Light, the corresponding task is to move the whole song until the repeated high notes and the low verse both speak comfortably. Lowering only the troublesome peak changes the melody instead. A key label helps coordinate the move, but singing the complete phrase is the test of fit.
