## “Could you talk to him for me?”

Nora’s mother, Helen, phones after another unanswered call to Nora’s brother, Ben.

“Tell Ben he’s being selfish. He listens to you.”

Nora knows what usually happens next. She calls Ben, explains how hurt their mother feels, and asks him to make an effort. Ben becomes irritated with Nora. Nora reports back to Helen, and the two discuss how difficult Ben has become.

Helen feels less alone. Nora feels responsible for fixing something that now involves two strained relationships.

**Detriangling** is an established Bowenian clinical process: changing one’s participation in a relationship triangle while maintaining appropriate emotional contact. **Triangles** is one of Bowen’s eight core concepts; detriangling describes work with that process, rather than an additional core concept. [Bowen Center: Eight Concepts](https://www.thebowencenter.org/introduction-eight-concepts), [Michael E. Kerr: “Chronic Anxiety and Defining a Self”](https://s3.amazonaws.com/media.cloversites.com/ae/ae4d98d9-4f73-46c4-a095-c1a61c9a949a/documents/CHRONIC_ANXIETY_AND_DEFINING_A_SELF_-_Kerr_Alantic_Monthly_Article.pdf)

## When helping becomes participation in the conflict

The recurring difficulty is more specific than discussing someone who is absent. A third person becomes involved in managing tension between two others: carrying grievances, applying pressure, forming an alliance, or repeatedly arranging reconciliation.

In Bowen’s account, involving a third person can make tension more manageable without resolving the original difficulty. The third person may subsequently become the focus of conflict. This formulation is useful when relief in one relationship repeatedly coincides with pressure or distance elsewhere. [Bowen Center: Triangles](https://www.thebowencenter.org/triangles)

However, seeking support, reporting mistreatment, requesting practical assistance, and using an agreed mediator should not automatically be classified as problematic triangling. A helpful third person can remain involved without assuming responsibility for making the other two agree. Bowenian clinician Daniel Papero describes how a sufficiently objective third person can support a two-person relationship without taking over its work. [Papero, “Assisting the Two-person System”](https://www.thefsi.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2023/04/Assiting-the-Two-person-System.pdf)

## How the tension travels

Bowen proposed that a triangle is the smallest stable relationship system. “Stable” here means able to distribute tension; it does not mean harmonious or resolved. As anxiety rises, patterns of closeness, conflict, and exclusion can shift among its three relationships. Additional people may become involved through interlocking triangles. [Bowen Center: Triangles](https://www.thebowencenter.org/triangles)

In Nora’s situation, the proposed process is reciprocal:

Helen’s complaint prompts Nora to intervene. Nora’s intervention gives Helen companionship and directs pressure toward Ben. Ben’s objection draws Nora into an argument. Nora then returns to Helen, strengthening their connection around a shared complaint.

Helen might be seeking reassurance; Nora might be trying to end an uncomfortable conversation. Those motives remain hypotheses. The observable sequence—who contacts whom, what each does next, and where conflict moves—is the firmer starting point.

Bowen described changing one’s position in important triangles through greater awareness of one’s automatic responsiveness and contribution to the process. The focus includes one’s own recruiting of allies, as well as being recruited. [Bowen Archives: Bowen Theory](https://murraybowenarchives.org/bowen-theory/), [Kerr](https://s3.amazonaws.com/media.cloversites.com/ae/ae4d98d9-4f73-46c4-a095-c1a61c9a949a/documents/CHRONIC_ANXIETY_AND_DEFINING_A_SELF_-_Kerr_Alantic_Monthly_Article.pdf)

## Changing a position while preserving contact

Detriangling involves learning to notice the pull to react and deciding how to participate. Simply memorizing a refusal can leave the same emotional struggle intact. Kerr explicitly cautioned against treating detriangling as a technique detached from a systems understanding; tone and nonverbal responses also matter. [Kerr](https://s3.amazonaws.com/media.cloversites.com/ae/ae4d98d9-4f73-46c4-a095-c1a61c9a949a/documents/CHRONIC_ANXIETY_AND_DEFINING_A_SELF_-_Kerr_Alantic_Monthly_Article.pdf)

For Nora, useful change could include listening to Helen without campaigning against Ben, maintaining her own relationship with Ben, and tolerating Helen’s disappointment without immediately trying to remove it.

An **I-position** can support this work: stating what one thinks and will do, then making conduct consistent with that position. Papero connects such self-definition with developing person-to-person relationships in which people discuss their own lives and thinking, rather than relating mainly through concerns about someone else. [Papero](https://www.thefsi.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2023/04/Assiting-the-Two-person-System.pdf)

Preparation matters. In a March 1977 letter, Bowen described rehearsing possible responses before a professional gathering where he anticipated becoming caught in polarized exchanges. This was his account of his own efforts, not an intervention trial. [Bowen, *Commitment to Principles*, pp. 175–176](https://murraybowenarchives.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/10/Commitment-To-Principles.pdf)

Nora can influence her messages, availability, and follow-through. She cannot determine whether Helen feels understood or whether Ben calls.

## Worked example: two possible continuations

*The people, dialogue, and outcomes below are invented teaching examples.*

### The familiar response

“Tell Ben he’s being selfish,” Helen says.

Nora calls him: “You should call Mum. You’re upsetting her.”

“Why are you speaking for her?” Ben asks.

Nora defends Helen. Ben defends himself. Their conversation becomes an argument about whether Nora always takes their mother’s side.

Later, Nora tells Helen what happened. They complain about Ben together. Nora contacts him less often afterward. Helen still has no call from him, but Nora has become more involved in the dispute.

### Different participation

Nora notices her familiar urge to make the problem disappear.

“I can listen,” she tells Helen, “but I won’t ask Ben to call for you.”

“So you’re taking his side.”

“I care about you both. I’m not going to carry messages.”

Nora allows a pause. She listens to Helen’s disappointment without adding accusations against Ben or promising to change his behavior.

Afterward, she does not relay the complaint. During her next conversation with Ben, she asks about his work and talks about her own week. She continues her ordinary contact with Helen, too.

Helen may remain upset. Ben has not called her. Nora’s change concerns her participation; the mother–son issue remains unresolved.

If Nora subsequently has her own disagreement with Ben, the same principle also applies to her: she can address her concern without recruiting Helen to argue her case.

## Limits and common mistakes

**Withdrawal can resemble detriangling.** Refusing all contact may reduce immediate discomfort while leaving emotional issues unresolved. Bowen’s concept of **emotional cutoff** addresses managing relationship intensity through distance or avoidance. Protective separation should not, however, be judged from that resemblance alone. [Bowen Center: Emotional Cutoff](https://www.thebowencenter.org/emotional-cutoff)

**Neutrality does not require moral indifference.** Kerr distinguished emotional neutrality from having no position or refusing to judge conduct. Understanding reciprocal responses does not establish equal power, equal responsibility, or equal freedom to act. [Kerr](https://s3.amazonaws.com/media.cloversites.com/ae/ae4d98d9-4f73-46c4-a095-c1a61c9a949a/documents/CHRONIC_ANXIETY_AND_DEFINING_A_SELF_-_Kerr_Alantic_Monthly_Article.pdf)

Applying this formulation responsibly also requires attention to safety and actual duties. Children are not responsible for stabilizing their parents’ relationship. Threats, coercion, dependency, disability, and caregiving obligations can constrain someone’s choices. Necessary protection, advocacy, or professional assistance should not be dismissed as “getting triangulated.”

Nor does one difficult conversation establish a person’s level of differentiation. These sources present theoretical propositions and clinical interpretations, not controlled evidence that Nora’s particular response will improve this family. A triangle formulation is also insufficient to explain an illness.

## Related Bowen concepts

**Differentiation of self** provides the broader frame: developing clearer thinking and principled conduct while remaining meaningfully connected under emotional pressure. One successful exchange does not establish a lasting change in differentiation. [Bowen Center: Differentiation of Self](https://www.thebowencenter.org/differentiation-of-self)

**Triangles** describes the relationship process; **detriangling** concerns changing one’s participation in it. **I-position** concerns self-definition, while **emotional cutoff** helps distinguish reduced reactivity from managing intensity through avoidance. Interlocking triangles explain why an apparently local change may involve other relatives or relationships. [Bowen Center: Triangles](https://www.thebowencenter.org/triangles), [Papero](https://www.thefsi.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2023/04/Assiting-the-Two-person-System.pdf)
