An invitation shows tangled cord and says “Find your thread.” Is it sewing, writing or project planning? The picture is coherent, but its comparison lacks a clear subject.

Choose the subject being explained, the image supplying the comparison and the relevant relationship between them. A metaphor borrows selected qualities; it also risks importing associations the designer did not intend.

## What to notice

All wording and layout remain fixed.](../assets/graphic-design/metaphor-book-growth.svg "Only the separately generated seedling appears in the fixed book photograph. [Full-size comparison](../assets/graphic-design/metaphor-book-growth.svg) · [Stacked narrow-screen view](../assets/graphic-design/metaphor-book-growth-mobile.svg)

Add a seedling at the open book’s gutter, keeping the book, “Ideas take root” headline and event details fixed. The apparent growing medium connects reading with development; a plant elsewhere on the table might simply be a prop.

The image now offers a relationship rather than merely identifying books. It also suggests gardening, ecology or slow change. A catalogue or opening-hours notice might prefer the literal original. This aspiration is not evidence that attending produces intellectual growth.

Forceville distinguishes source, target and transferred features; Gentner’s analogy framework emphasizes selective relationships. These theories help describe the intended mapping, not guarantee its interpretation.

## Two variations

### Give the picture a subject through words

![Two matching Find your thread invitations reuse an identical photograph of tangled cord with a straight tail. Before, the explanatory line reads Make room for something new. After, it reads Plan the next steps of your project. The image, headline, dates, price, typography and positions remain unchanged.](../assets/graphic-design/metaphor-cord-anchor.svg "Only one sentence changes to identify the metaphor’s subject. Its typography, baseline, photograph, headline and event details stay fixed.")

[Full-size comparison](../assets/graphic-design/metaphor-cord-anchor.svg) · [Stacked narrow-screen view](../assets/graphic-design/metaphor-cord-anchor-mobile.svg)

Replace “Make room for something new” with “Plan the next steps of your project.” Keep the cord, “Find your thread” headline, styling and £18 session details.

The line establishes planning as the target, letting untangling supply a way to understand it. Naming the subject does not eliminate the metaphor. The revision is less open-ended, suitable for deciding whether to book; an exploratory exhibition might value ambiguity. Real planning can branch rather than follow one continuous thread.

### Change an unhelpful borrowed relationship

![Two matching Learn together invitations retain identical copy and layout. Before, a photographic wooden ladder supplies the metaphor. After, a photographic wooden bridge spanning a gap replaces it in the same image frame. Both objects use a pale wood appearance on the same neutral ground.](../assets/graphic-design/metaphor-peer-bridge.svg "Only the pictured object changes, ladder→bridge. Their geometry, texture and lighting necessarily differ; this compares metaphors, not isolated shapes or measured responses.")

[Full-size comparison](../assets/graphic-design/metaphor-peer-bridge.svg) · [Stacked narrow-screen view](../assets/graphic-design/metaphor-peer-bridge-mobile.svg)

Replace a ladder with a bridge model for a peer skill-sharing circle. Words and presentation remain fixed. The ladder’s stages and upward ranking give way to a proposed connection across separation.

A bridge is an editorial fit for exchanging methods, not a universal meaning: it can also imply travel or a difficult gap. A structured course could use the ladder more appropriately. Neither object guarantees equal participation or successful connection.

## Useful when

Use metaphor when a concrete relationship can introduce an abstract subject, aspiration or experience. State the intended comparison plainly before investing in its execution, then examine what else the image might imply.

Interpretation requires audience knowledge. Petridis and Chilton document interpretation errors and relationships with message familiarity; a clever construction is still a candidate to test. Words can supply one side of a visual/multimodal metaphor when the image alone leaves the subject open.

## Watch out for

Growth can imply inevitable improvement, ladders hierarchy and clocks urgency. Shared associations differ across cultures, languages and professions. Test what viewers infer rather than only whether they like the image.

Keep dates, prices and instructions explicit. A metaphor can frame the message without carrying every fact. When people need literal evidence—a product view, measured relationship or exact specification—use an appropriate representation instead. Figurative imagery must not disguise an unsupported performance claim.
