## What it is for

Use this pattern when customers can easily compare your product with alternatives, but the encounter around it could give them a stronger reason to choose you.

A pottery studio can sell a finished bowl. It can also offer an evening in which customers shape their own bowls, learn from a potter, and spend time with friends. The object remains useful, while the making becomes part of what people buy.

Experience Selling builds value into what customers feel, do, and remember during an encounter with the business. That experience becomes a meaningful part of the paid offering.

It can support differentiation, a higher price, a separate revenue stream, or a stronger reason to return.

## The core idea

Customers sometimes want more than the functional result. They want the pleasure of participating, the feeling of belonging, the excitement of discovery, or an occasion worth remembering.

The business deliberately organizes its setting, interactions, activities, and pacing around that value.

This connects with Pine and Gilmore’s experience-economy framework: businesses can create economic value through personally engaging, memorable encounters. Their work also emphasizes that customers commit scarce time and attention alongside money. [Pine and Gilmore: The Experience Economy](https://strategichorizons.com/learn-and-engage/speaking/)

An experience can be spectacular, like entering a fictional world. It can also be quiet: a carefully hosted tasting, a welcoming café, or an appointment in which the customer feels understood.

The defining feature is that the encounter itself helps explain the purchase.

## How it creates value

**Participation gives the customer a role.** Choosing, making, tasting, exploring, or performing can turn a transaction into something personally meaningful. The customer helps shape what happens.

**A coherent setting supports the promise.** The space, language, sound, materials, and staff behavior work together. A calm retreat and an energetic workshop need different environments, but both benefit from consistency.

**Ritual gives ordinary actions significance.** A welcome, a naming ceremony, a first taste, or a final reveal can mark a moment. The ritual works when it feels appropriate to the occasion and the customer.

**Human interaction makes the encounter responsive.** A capable host can notice uncertainty, adapt an explanation, or make somebody feel included. That requires judgment as well as a script.

These elements give customers more dimensions on which to value the offering. They can compare the occasion, atmosphere, participation, and personal meaning alongside the underlying product.

## Famous example: Disney’s Savi’s Workshop

At Savi’s Workshop in Walt Disney World, customers assemble a lightsaber within the fictional setting of _Star Wars_.

Disney describes a guided encounter in a concealed workshop. Participants choose a hilt theme, select a crystal, and assemble their lightsaber with help from the workshop’s “Gatherers.” The purchase combines the building experience with the finished object; separate park admission is required. [Disney: Savi’s Workshop](https://disneyworld.disney.go.com/shops/hollywood-studios/savis-workshop-handbuilt-lightsabers/)

The business-model mechanism is visible: the customer purchases an object through an experience that gives its creation a story.

The lightsaber can then serve as a reminder of that participation. Its meaning may include who accompanied the customer and what happened during the visit.

The dependency is equally important. The experience relies on an established fictional world, trained hosts, a controlled environment, and limited session capacity. A similar assembly process without those supporting elements would offer a different proposition.

## Famous example: Build-A-Bear Workshop

Build-A-Bear turns buying a stuffed animal into a guided creation process.

Customers choose an animal, participate in stuffing it, add a heart, check its softness with a hug, select accessories, and give it a name and birth certificate. Staff guide the sequence. [Build-A-Bear: The Workshop Experience](https://www.buildabear.com/brand-about-story-experience.html)

The choices personalize the product. The sequence also gives its creation emotional significance: the customer has helped bring this particular companion into being.

This illustrates how Experience Selling can be bundled into merchandise. The experience supports the product purchase without requiring a separate admission ticket.

The operating challenge is to preserve personal attention while running a repeatable retail process. An overcrowded workshop or a hurried interaction can weaken the very experience that distinguishes the offering.

## Famous example: Starbucks

Starbucks illustrates a more everyday form of Experience Selling.

Its classic café proposition includes a place to sit, meet, and stay. The company explicitly describes that format as a “third place,” while also offering drive-through, delivery, and other formats built around different needs. [Starbucks: Store Formats](https://www.starbucks.com/stores-and-ordering)

In its 2025 coffeehouse-design announcement, Starbucks described investments in softer seating, ceramic cups, more personal service, and spaces intended to feel welcoming. [Starbucks: Coffeehouse Design](https://about.starbucks.com/stories/2025/starbucks-coffeehouse-designs-enter-a-new-era-take-a-look/)

Here, the experience is embedded in the beverage purchase. The commercial proposition can include somewhere to pause, a familiar routine, or a comfortable meeting place.

This also shows the pattern’s limits within one brand. A customer collecting a mobile order may primarily value speed. A customer meeting a friend may value atmosphere. Experience design has to fit the visit’s purpose.

## The economics

Experience Selling can earn money through several arrangements:

- **Paid access:** customers buy a ticket, session, workshop, or event.
- **A combined purchase:** the product and experience are sold together.
- **A price premium:** customers value the overall offering enough to pay more.
- **Repeat occasions:** changing activities, seasonal events, or recurring rituals provide reasons to return.

These are possible mechanisms, rather than automatic outcomes.

The costs often include staff time, training, space, maintenance, preparation, and lower throughput. A longer encounter may increase customer value while reducing the number of customers served per hour.

Capacity therefore matters. A memorable workshop with too few paying participants may lose money. Filling every available place may also damage the experience if customers receive too little attention.

The relevant test is whether the additional revenue and repeat business justify the additional delivery cost.

## Useful when

This pattern is especially useful when the product naturally connects with an occasion, interest, identity, or shared activity.

It fits businesses where customers enjoy learning, exploring, making choices, meeting others, or taking time over the purchase. It can also help when the functional offering is widely available and difficult to distinguish through specifications alone.

A strong fit requires both customer interest and operational ability. The business needs something worth experiencing, plus the people and processes to deliver it consistently.

For customers pursuing a quick, routine task, a clear and efficient transaction may provide greater value.

## Common mistakes and limits

**Adding decoration without changing the encounter.** Attractive surroundings can help, but the experience also depends on what customers actually do and how they are treated.

**Confusing attention with willingness to pay.** A popular photo opportunity may attract visitors without producing enough purchases or bookings to cover its cost.

**Making participation compulsory.** Some customers enjoy performing, sharing, or being celebrated. Others prefer to observe. Good hosting allows different levels of involvement.

**Neglecting the functional promise.** Food still needs to taste good. Products still need to work. The experience raises expectations for the whole offering.

**Underinvesting in staff.** Warmth and responsiveness are difficult to sustain when employees lack time, training, or authority to solve problems.

**Depending entirely on novelty.** Surprise can attract a first visit. Repeat business may require changing content, deepening participation, or an everyday experience people continue to value.
