**Pattern 46 of 49 · Part 8: Arrange, Revise, and Finish**

**Use when:** Later returns get louder but the whole song still lacks an arc.

Every chorus becomes louder, but the final return still feels less consequential than the first. The arrangement has increased level without changing the listener's relationship to the material. Energy is shaped by more than loudness, and a trajectory needs contrasts that remain audible across time.

Shape Energy over Time means planning changes in activity, density, register, performance intensity, and space so that returns have a purpose. A drawing can help expose the plan, but the recording determines whether the intended arc is actually heard.

## Working choice

Plan contrasting entrances, exits, density, register, and space so each return has a purpose. A drawn energy curve is a hypothesis; the actual recording may communicate a different trajectory.

## Separate dimensions of energy

A passage can become more rhythmically active while remaining quiet. It can become louder with fewer notes. A wider register can feel expansive; a narrow exposed voice can feel intense. A bass entrance can change the apparent weight without a new melody.

Porch Light's chorus uses a slower syllabic pace than its verses. Its focal quality does not require more vocal notes. Longer words, a broader accompaniment, and a clearer bass pulse can create a different kind of attention.

Platform Three can keep the same harmonic center while adding a higher repeated figure. Later, removing the bass can expose that figure and create a sense of suspension. The eventual low return becomes an energy event through contrast rather than a key change.

## Sketch a trajectory with actual events

The original Porch Light arrangement begins with seed and chord instrument. Verse one adds voice and sparse bass. Chorus one introduces the clearer groove and a high response. Verse two retains the bass but leaves space beneath the note's important line.

The bridge reduces the texture, placing the admission closer to the foreground. The final chorus returns the groove and adds a limited backing voice. The outro removes the voice and settles the seed. This is a sequence of concrete changes, not merely a curve labeled “more emotion.”

The curve can look similar for two arrangements that sound different. One grows through percussion; another through harmonic density. The diagram must remain subordinate to its underlying events.

## Build by layered entry

When a repeating idea becomes static but should remain recognizable, reserve supporting parts for later entrances. This is a technique within the present pattern: the figure supplies continuity, while each entrance changes the combined texture. Assign those parts clear jobs with [[songwriting-give-each-part-a-job]] before deciding when to introduce them.

The original eight-bar player above keeps a D–F–A–F piano figure unchanged. In **Constant texture**, bass, sustained D–A tones and a pulse begin with it. In **Layered entry**, the bass enters at bar 3, sustained tones at bar 5, and pulse at bar 7. The tempo, sounds, velocities and overall length remain fixed. After each part enters, its notes match the constant version. The comparison changes the schedule of entrances rather than pretending that an entirely different melody proves a point about arrangement.

Open **What changes in A/B?** for the four comparison questions, then **Show layers and activity** for the evidence. The line counts note onsets in each bar, so it describes activity. Perceived intensity also depends on register, timbre, dynamics, harmony, duration and context. A sparse exposed line can feel more intense than a busy accompaniment.

Accumulation needs a next decision. **Build → climax → release** reserves an arrival and then changes what follows it. **Build → release → rebuild** gives a later growth its own starting point. **Gradual transformation** can replace an accumulation of parts with a changing sound or register. None requires the final span to be the loudest. The miniature demonstrates the entrance technique only; its last bar is a stopping point, not evidence of a fully earned dramatic resolution.

## Reserve contrast

If the intro presents every instrument at its fullest activity, later growth may require disproportionate additions. A simpler opening can preserve an entrance that matters later. That is a practical compositional decision, not a rule against immediate impact.

A song can also begin at full intensity and develop by withdrawing. The important reserve might be silence rather than another instrument. A final unaccompanied line can be the most exposed point in a previously dense song.

For Porch Light, a more intimate alternative removes most backing at the final title. The same lyric, now heard after the bridge, may gain force from the reduced setting. Whether it succeeds depends on the performance and the preceding trajectory.

## Make successive returns different for a reason

A first chorus establishes the hook. A second can retain that identity while the new verse changes its meaning. A final return may shorten, extend, add a voice, remove accompaniment, or alter one line. The change should serve the song's movement rather than satisfy a requirement that every chorus be bigger.

A useful comparison preserves the vocal and harmonic structure while changing only the entrance of a backing part. This helps reveal whether the extra layer actually contributes. If the return works without it, the simpler version may be clearer.

Conversely, a fuller setting can support a collective or emphatic ending. Density is not inherently clutter. The roles need to remain intentional and the focal material audible.

## Says: intensity grows around a persistent figure

Nils Frahm's **Says**, on [Spaces](https://nilsfrahm.bandcamp.com/album/spaces), gives gradual growth a long span. A recurring electronic figure remains a reference while sustained layers, upper-register activity, and the breadth of the texture increase. Comparing the early figure with a much fuller later passage makes it possible to hear development without requiring a new tune at every stage.

“More energy” is still too vague to be a complete arranging instruction. In Platform Three, the same bass pattern might gain an upper octave, a sustained layer, and denser percussion at different stages. Each change should have a place in the trajectory; introducing all three at the beginning leaves less contrast available later. Says is useful as an instrumental example of accumulated weight, not evidence that energy is only loudness or that a gradual build must reach its maximum at a prescribed minute. Its longer span also differs from the course's compact thirty-two-bar track. The transferable decision is which dimensions to reserve, and which recognizable feature lets the listener follow their growth.

## Notice fatigue as well as excitement

Continuous high activity can reduce the significance of local peaks. Long gaps can lose momentum when no compelling material fills them. Energy planning therefore includes duration: a texture that works for four bars may not sustain sixteen unchanged bars in this particular song.

A songwriter can identify the moment attention weakens and ask which dimension has become static. The answer may be lyrical information, rhythmic activity, harmonic pace, or a part that has remained present too long. Adding loudness indiscriminately does not diagnose it.

This pattern helps when sections work individually but the whole recording feels level, when the final chorus has nowhere to go, or when every build produces fatigue. It does not require a single rising arc. Cycles, plateaus, and abrupt changes can be convincing when their relationships are clear.

The arrangement and curve here are original. DeSantis's [Dramatic Arc](https://makingmusic.ableton.com/dramatic-arc) offers a complementary creative reference.
