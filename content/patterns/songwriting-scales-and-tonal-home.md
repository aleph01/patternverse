**Pattern 15 of 49 · Part 3: Notes, Keys, and Chords**

**Use when:** A loop uses a familiar set of notes but its center is unclear or differs from the key label on the chart.

A person plays only the white keys of a piano and assumes the music must be in C major. Then a repeated low A makes the same notes seem to gather around a different home. The inventory of pitches has not changed. Their emphasis and relationship to a center have.

## The move

Make a pitch feel like home through bass, repetition, and phrase endings, then choose the surrounding palette.

Hold the E–G–A–G phrase fixed and compare a sustained C bass with an A bass. End the first version on C and the second on A. Listen for the changed point of rest before adding more chords. Both versions use notes from the same collection; the bass reference and final note give them different points of orientation. Establish that relationship before choosing a label for it.

A scale inventory or first note does not by itself establish a center; ambiguity can also be intentional.

## Give the collection a point of rest

A scale is an ordered collection of pitches. A tonal center is the note heard as a point of orientation or home. A key includes that center and a broader major or minor organization. These ideas are related, but one cannot always identify a song's key simply by listing its notes.

[[songwriting-build-a-scale|Build a Scale from Steps]] supplies the construction: C major uses C–D–E–F–G–A–B. This pattern asks a different question: which of those notes feels like a place to return? That pitch is the tonic. It may appear in the melody, the bass, or both, and repeated returns can make its role easier to hear.

Compare a sentence paused in the middle with one that reaches a full stop. Musical endings can also leave a sense of continuation or rest. The comparison is an aid to listening, not a rule that every phrase must finish on its tonic. A phrase may deliberately stop elsewhere while the larger passage keeps its home clear.

A useful starting question is which note a listener would sing to complete the passage. That answer can change with the bass, earlier phrases, or later continuation. Naming the first note on a page does not settle the question. Neither does finding the most frequent note in a short fragment without hearing its setting.

## Compare two homes with the same opening

The player offers two original eight-beat sketches, **Tonal home · settle on C** and **Tonal home · settle on A**. Their first four beats are identical: E4, G4, A4, G4, one beat each. The final note lasts four beats. A low reference pitch lasts through the complete sketch.

| Version | Opening, four beats | Ending, four beats | Bass reference |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| C-centered | E4–G4–A4–G4 | C4 | C3 |
| A-centered | E4–G4–A4–G4 | A4 | A2 |

Both sketches use only white-key notes. What changes is the bass reference together with the ending, not the complete collection. This is a comparison of two ways to establish home; it does not isolate the effect of the bass alone. Muting the Bass part makes it possible to hear how much the ending supplies by itself. Muting Melody leaves the reference without a phrase around it.

These are teaching variations around Porch Light's seed, not replacements for its canonical verse. The full song remains in its original C-major setting. A small comparison can reveal a relationship without rewriting the reference song each time.

## Separate a note from its role

In a C-centered passage, E is the third scale degree: count C as 1, D as 2, and E as 3. In an A-centered passage, the same E is degree 5: A, B, C, D, E. The pitch has not changed, but its relationship to home has. Scale degrees name these single-note relationships. Chord numbers will name whole chords in a later pattern.

A key combines a center with a broader musical organization, such as major or minor. C major and A minor share a key signature and are called relative keys. That is a useful name for the shared material, but the immediate listening task is simpler: hear C or A as the point of rest. Parallel scales, modes, and altered minor notes get their own later comparisons in [[songwriting-modes-drones-and-key-changes|Change the Tonal World Deliberately]].

## Establish home across a longer phrase

A low reference can help orient a fragment, but a whole song usually supplies more evidence. Porch Light returns to C through its phrase endings, repeated harmonic relationships, and final arrival. A G chord near the end of a verse can leave the thought open without changing the song's home to G.

The same distinction matters for a loop. Beginning on Am does not automatically make every later chord part of an A-minor song. The melody may keep resolving toward C, or later passages may make a different interpretation more convincing. Beginning, ending, bass, and repetition should be heard together rather than turning one visible detail into an automatic answer.

An ambiguous center can also be intentional. If a loop avoids a clear resting point, more than one interpretation can be reasonable. A useful explanation describes what supports each hearing. It does not force a definitive key label before the musical setting supplies enough evidence.

## Do not assign a fixed emotion to a scale

Major and minor have familiar expressive associations in many listening cultures, but tempo, register, words, rhythm, timbre, and context all affect an experience. A major-key song can feel grieving or uneasy. A minor-key groove can feel celebratory or forceful.

An original major-key version of Porch Light with a sparse low voice and long pauses may feel more unsettled than a brisk minor-key dance arrangement. That observation does not erase harmonic differences. It places them among other musical choices.

This vocabulary helps when choosing material or describing why a repeated note feels stable. Ableton's [Keys and Scales](https://learningmusic.ableton.com/notes-and-scales/keys-and-scales.html) provides an introductory reference. Scale construction is covered in the preceding pattern; the circle-of-fifths pattern later maps the relationships between key signatures.

## A center can be stronger than a cadence

Miles Davis's **So What**, on [Kind of Blue](https://www.milesdavis.com/albums/kind-of-blue/), establishes a tonal world through the bass theme, chordal responses, and the space given to improvisation. Its main D-centered area contrasts with an E-flat-centered area before returning. The movement between areas matters because the first center has become familiar; this is more than an undirected list of notes.

The comparison illuminates Platform Three. Its recurring low D, phrases returning toward D, and stable bass setting can organize the upper notes around D. Moving the starting point of a scale exercise alone would not establish that relationship. The fuller modal explanation belongs to the later tonal-world pattern. Here, listen for the persistence and return of a center. So What is a jazz performance with its own form, not a model that every songwriter must copy. It gives an audible reason to ask where a phrase feels oriented before deciding that a collection of pitches determines its home. The album page identifies the recording; the tonal account here is a listening interpretation, not a full solo transcription.
