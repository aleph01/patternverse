### The border around an event

A presentation goes badly, and for the rest of the evening it seems to define the presenter's professional life. A question was misunderstood, an important point was rushed, and the discomfort of the room remains vivid. From the perspective of that evening, the presentation fills almost the whole field of attention. A colleague who has watched the person work for three years sees something else: an awkward event within a longer record of capable work. The two views concern the same presentation, but the border around it changes its significance.

In Sleight of Mouth, Robert Dilts calls this movement Change Frame Size. The frame can expand or contract across time, the number of people considered, or the scope of a situation. A local event can be placed within a project, an individual choice within a group, or an immediate benefit within a sequence of later costs. Meaning changes because the event is now compared with different surroundings.

Neither a large frame nor a small frame is inherently more accurate. A wider frame can reveal consequences that a local view misses, while a narrower frame can expose details that a grand summary obscures. The question is whether the current boundary fits the judgment being made. A presentation needs evaluation as a presentation, but one presentation provides a thin basis for evaluating an entire career.

### Following the effects beyond the immediate view

A company postpones maintenance to improve this quarter's financial result. Within the quarterly frame, the decision looks efficient: costs fall and the service continues to operate. Extending the time horizon brings future repairs, interruptions, and emergency work into the account. The longer frame may show that some savings were borrowed from the future. It may also show that postponement was reasonable because equipment will soon be replaced. Widening the frame supplies a fuller comparison rather than a predetermined verdict.

The boundary can also move across people. A department introduces a process that reduces its own administrative work but requires every customer to complete another form. The department's measure of efficiency improves. Across the whole transaction, effort may have increased and simply moved elsewhere. Looking at the experience of customers and support staff reveals costs that the original boundary excluded. The organization can then decide whether the local saving justifies the wider burden.

Narrowing the frame is equally important. A person who wants to write a book may find the whole undertaking impossible to contemplate. Considering the work available in a quiet hour reveals a scene to revise or an argument to clarify. That smaller frame does not guarantee completion of the book, but it changes the immediate decision from judging an entire project to identifying work that can be done now. The wider ambition gives direction; the smaller interval makes action legible.

### Perspective without dismissal

Changing a frame can become an easy way to minimize another person's experience. Telling someone that their difficulty will not matter in ten years may be an invitation to perspective, or it may be a refusal to acknowledge what matters today. A larger time horizon does not make immediate pain imaginary. The event can be both significant now and limited within a longer life. Holding both descriptions is often more useful than asking one to cancel the other.

There is a corresponding danger of making every choice impossibly large. An ordinary decision can be placed within the future of humanity, global systems, or an entire life story until no proportionate action seems adequate. Widening the frame should reveal relevant relationships, rather than attach unlimited significance to a small event. The appropriate boundary depends partly on the decision's reach and reversibility. Choosing lunch and designing a public service do not require the same scope of analysis.

Frames also deserve scrutiny when they are selected to favor a conclusion. A policy can look attractive over one year and costly over five, or beneficial to one group and harmful to another. Showing only the convenient view conceals part of the argument. A fair comparison explains which boundary was chosen and why it matters, while acknowledging material effects outside it. Perspective is strongest when the movement between frames is visible.

### Choosing the scale of the question

Change the Frame Size is useful when an event has become overwhelming, a local improvement hides broader costs, or a large abstraction prevents useful action. Follow the Consequences supplies connections across the expanded frame. Chunk Down examines components inside the event, while Chunk Up changes the category through which an element is understood. These movements can support one another without becoming interchangeable.

The presentation can be evaluated honestly while remaining one event in a longer history. The maintenance decision can include future work without pretending every future cost is known. The book can remain a substantial ambition while today's task stays small. The pattern makes these combinations possible by showing that the importance of an event partly depends on the context chosen for judging it, and that this context can be reconsidered.

## Pattern details

### Use when

- One event overwhelms a longer history.
- A local saving may transfer costs elsewhere.
- A large ambition obscures a manageable next decision.

### Goals

- Choose a context proportionate to the judgment.
- Reveal costs and options outside the current boundary.

### Mechanisms

- temporal framing
- system boundary selection

### Not ideal when

- Wider perspective is being used to dismiss a present difficulty.

### Difficulty

easy
