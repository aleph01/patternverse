*Keep the action, change the person we see.*

A character needs to survive a violent collision, appear at an impossible age, or stand beside another character played by the same actor.

Each situation separates two things we normally experience as inseparable: **what a performer does and whose face or body appears to do it**.

A stunt performer may supply the movement. Another actor may supply the expression. A digital model can supply the appearance. Visual-effects artists combine those contributions into one apparently continuous person.

The motivation is practical as well as spectacular: preserve convincing physical action and interaction while changing the identity, age, or anatomy visible to the camera.

## The opening puzzle: why does the stunt performer look exactly like Hugh Jackman?
In *Logan* (2017), a limousine is struck while Logan grips the steering wheel. The photographed body supplies the strain and movement, but the finished head belongs to Jackman’s character.

![The matching car shot shows Logan’s head joined to the photographed body and hands.](https://image-engine.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/07/WOL14.jpg)

*Logan (2017) — Finished VFX shot.* The corresponding finished shot replaces the head with Logan’s likeness. The hands, costume, and bodily action connect the new head to the performance. [Image source](https://image-engine.com/case-studies/logan/). Credit: © 2017 Marvel / Twentieth Century Fox; published by Image Engine.


Image Engine’s production breakdown identifies this as a head replacement over a stunt performer. The studio also built full digital versions of Logan and Laura, choosing different amounts of replacement for different shots. [Image Engine’s case study](https://image-engine.com/case-studies/logan/).

The important question is how much needs to change. If the photographed shoulders, hands, costume, and movement already work, those elements can remain. The replacement must join them convincingly.

These approaches overlap, but their scope differs:

| Approach | What changes | What can remain photographed |
|---|---|---|
| Face replacement | The visible facial region | Head outline, hair, body, surroundings |
| Head replacement | Face, skull outline, often hair and neck | Body performance and environment |
| Full digital double | The entire character | Other performers and the surrounding scene |

A digital double is a reusable representation of someone. Face replacement describes a job within a shot. A production can use its digital double to replace only a head in one scene and the whole body in another.

Nor does every replacement require a fully computer-generated face. Filmed facial material can be tracked, reshaped, projected onto geometry, and combined with the original photography.

## How the replacement becomes part of the shot
**1. Record the appearance.** The production needs a useful record of the person’s appearance. Photographs and scans can describe facial shape, proportions, skin detail, hair, and expressions. Lighting reference helps artists understand how those surfaces respond under different conditions.

That creates the ingredients. It does not yet create a performance.

**2. Match the movement.** The replacement must follow the photographed action. Artists establish the camera’s movement and the performer’s position, then coordinate the digital head or body with them. This is often called **[matchmoving](/pattern/film-matchmoving-and-camera-tracking)**.

The attachment is especially revealing. A head that follows the face perfectly but disagrees with the shoulders can seem to slide or stretch at the neck. Body proportions matter alongside facial likeness.

**3. Match the light.** Lighting supplies another test. If someone passes beneath a tree, the face must enter shade with the body. Reflections in the eyes, light through the ears, and shadows beneath the chin need to belong to the same environment.

**4. Combine the images.** Compositing brings the replacement into the photographed image by combining image layers. Hair edges, foreground objects, focus, motion blur, grain, and color all contribute to the connection.

The viewer should perceive one event. The construction may involve several performances and several kinds of image.

### Puzzle two: how can one actor play twins who interact naturally?

In *The Social Network* (2010), Armie Hammer appears as both Winklevoss brothers. Filming Hammer twice can solve some compositions, but another approach lets two performers act together during the take.


![Josh Pence, left, performs beside Armie Hammer.](https://www.awn.com/sites/default/files/styles/inline/public/image/featured/44980-helping-fincher-build-his-social-network.jpg?itok=AH-NQGoW)

*The Social Network (2010) — Original production image.* Josh Pence, left, performs beside Armie Hammer. This image shows the original performers before Pence’s face is replaced in the relevant shots. [Image source](https://www.awn.com/vfxworld/helping-fincher-build-his-social-network). Credit: Sony Pictures; published by Animation World Network.


For selected shots, Josh Pence performed alongside Hammer. Lola Visual Effects tracked Pence’s face, while Hammer separately performed facial material matched to the selected take. Filmed imagery was projected onto a scanned facial model and composited into the scene. The production also used split-screen work. [Interview with Lola supervisor Edson Williams](https://www.awn.com/vfxworld/helping-fincher-build-his-social-network).

The physical interaction and the final facial identity came from different recordings. An apparent twin therefore need not be a second copy of the same complete photographed performance.

### Puzzle three: how can Brad Pitt act with a body of a different age and size?

In *The Curious Case of Benjamin Button* (2008), the character’s unusual aging demands more than wrinkles. His face, head proportions, body size, and performance must agree across changing stages of life.


![A production comparison shows Pitt recording facial acting, a body actor on set, and the integrated digital Benjamin head.](https://www.cgw.com/images/Media/PublicationsArticle/p10_3.jpg)

*The Curious Case of Benjamin Button (2008) — Published production comparison.* Left: Pitt records facial performance. Upper right: the body actor during filming. Lower right: the digital Benjamin head integrated into the scene. [Image source](https://www.cgw.com/Publications/CGW/2009/Volume-32-Issue-1-Jan-2009-/Whats-Old-is-New-Again.aspx). Credit: Digital Domain production imagery; published by Computer Graphics World.


For the early Benjamin, other actors supplied the photographed bodies while Digital Domain created a digital head carrying Pitt’s performance. Pitt recorded the character’s scenes separately; artists developed and adjusted the facial animation to work with the earlier photography.

The result was a constructed performance combining body acting, facial acting, modeling, animation, and lighting. [Digital Domain’s artists explain the process in Computer Graphics World](https://www.cgw.com/Publications/CGW/2009/Volume-32-Issue-1-Jan-2009-/Whats-Old-is-New-Again.aspx).

This is why “they pasted his face on” is an incomplete explanation. The new head must inhabit the body’s movements and the scene’s light while expressing the intended character.

## The same pattern can complete an interrupted production
After Paul Walker’s death during the making of *Furious 7* (2015), Wētā Digital helped complete his remaining appearances using several methods. Its account identifies earlier footage, scans of Walker’s brothers, and detailed compositing among the resources used. [Wētā FX’s production summary](https://www.wetafx.co.nz/films/filmography/furious-7).

This example shows why a finished appearance rarely identifies its method by itself. Photographed material, another performer, digital reconstruction, and editorial choices can contribute in different proportions.

## Why a convincing still can fail in motion
Likeness is only one requirement. A face can resemble the actor in a single frame and feel unfamiliar when it smiles.

Small discrepancies accumulate: an eye moves before the head, a blink interrupts attention, the jaw opens with the wrong rhythm, or the replacement appears unusually steady while the body jolts.

Contact is another demanding boundary. A hand crossing the face must pass in front of the correct surfaces. A collar must meet the neck. Skin and cloth must exchange believable shadows.

Replacing the whole body removes some attachment problems but introduces others: weight, balance, clothing, hair, and contact with the ground all need convincing movement.

The amount of reconstruction should follow the shot’s needs. More replacement means more of the event must be recreated successfully.

## When the pattern earns its complexity
Digital doubles and face replacement are useful when the desired action and desired appearance cannot conveniently come from the same recording: stunt substitution, twin roles, age changes, altered proportions, and transitions between human performance and impossible movement.

They work especially well when the production plans the relationship between those contributions before filming.

The neighboring patterns divide the work differently. **[Performance capture](/pattern/film-performance-capture)** supplies movement or expression for animation. **[Wire-assisted stunts](/pattern/film-wire-assisted-stunts)** extend a real performer’s motion. **[Motion control](/pattern/film-motion-control-and-repeatable-camera-movements)** helps separate recordings share a camera move. **[Prosthetic makeup](/pattern/film-prosthetic-makeup)** changes the face before photography.

Here, the defining move is to preserve the parts of a photographed performance that serve the scene, while reconstructing the person the audience believes it is watching.
