A leaf that looked ordinary from the path glows when you view it with the sun behind it.
Its veins become dark lines inside a luminous green surface. Nearby, fine fibers on a wool
mitten catch a bright outline, while a person at a window becomes mostly a dark shape.
These appearances share a direction of light but depend on different materials and exposure choices.

Use light coming from behind the subject when that relationship helps the photograph. Thin
materials may transmit it. Hair, fur, and fibers may catch it along edges. Haze can become
visible. An opaque subject can become a silhouette against a brighter background. Decide which
effect matters before trying to make every part of the image bright.

## Separate the possibilities

Think of backlight as a relationship, not a preset look. The source lies beyond the subject
from the camera's viewpoint. Whether you see glowing material, a rim, or a dark shape depends
on source position, material, background, other illumination, and how you expose and process
the image. Merely putting the sun behind something does not guarantee a bright outline.

A luminous edge needs a useful contrast with what sits behind it. Pale fibers against a
bright sky may disappear; the same fibers against a dark wall can be clear. Translucency
requires material thin enough for light to pass through. A thick wooden door will not glow
like a leaf. A silhouette needs a readable outline as well as a brightness difference.

[[follow-the-light|Follow the Light]] introduces direction more generally. [[separate-subject-and-background|Separate Subject and Background]] explains why an outline must remain distinguishable. Backlight can
provide that distinction, but it can also make an important face difficult to see.

## A leaf with visible veins

Choose a leaf whose surface you can view without pointing directly at the sun. A small
change in angle may reveal its color and veins while keeping the source outside the frame.
If the leaf becomes a featureless bright patch, reduce the exposure target and check the
result. If you make everything very dark to protect an irrelevant piece of sky, the leaf
may lose the luminous quality that attracted you.

The background matters. A gap of bright sky behind the leaf may compete with it; darker
foliage can make its edge easier to read. Moving the camera can solve that problem without
changing the leaf. Wind may change both the leaf's angle and its focus distance, so check
that the chosen detail remains technically adequate. [[focus-on-what-matters|Focus on What Matters]]
and [[freeze-or-show-motion|Freeze or Show Motion]] address those separate controls.

Never look directly at the sun through an optical viewfinder or aim a magnifying optical
system at it for this effect. A bright window or a source safely outside the frame can
teach the same principle without needing a solar subject.

## A rim around an opaque subject

A dark knitted mitten can retain surface texture while individual fibers catch light from
behind. Place it visually against something darker than the edge. If the source moves too
far sideways, the effect may become ordinary side lighting; if it is directly behind a
thick object, some edges may receive little visible light from the camera's position.

For a portrait, backlight can outline hair while a pale wall or open sky in front supplies
gentler light to the face. Without that front illumination, exposing for the background may
leave the face dark. Raising the exposure for the face can brighten or clip the background.
You can accept that tradeoff, change the location, add reflected light, or choose a silhouette.
There is no obligation to recover detailed tone everywhere.

Phones may automatically combine exposures to lift the face and retain the sky. That can
be useful, but moving hair or hands can produce artifacts. Some cameras offer similar HDR
features. Inspect the actual image rather than assuming the preview represents one ordinary
exposure. [[photography-foundations|Photography Foundations]] explains computational capture.

## Make a silhouette readable

At a bright window, turn a consenting person's profile until the nose, lips, and chin separate
from the background. If they face the camera squarely, their outline may communicate less
about their expression. Keep an arm or hand from merging ambiguously with the body when
the gesture matters. Shape is doing work that surface detail would otherwise perform.

Expose so the bright background remains useful and the figure stays sufficiently dark for
your intention. A partly detailed face may feel different from a nearly black silhouette.
Neither requires a specific universal compensation value. Camera metering, background brightness,
and processing differ. [[expose-for-what-matters|Expose for What Matters]] explains how to choose
the priority and assess clipping.

## Watch what backlight conceals

Backlight helps when material, edge, atmosphere, or shape is the subject. It is less useful
when a record depends on accurate readable front-surface detail and there is no adequate
light there. Flare can reduce contrast or create colored shapes; shade the lens without
covering the view, or adjust the angle if those effects distract.

Strong backlight can also mislead autofocus, and tiny bright highlights may exceed what the
camera records. Darkening later cannot restore details that were never captured. Keep the
light relationship, focus choice, and exposure compromise distinct. Understanding those choices
makes backlight a reusable possibility rather than an accidental silhouette you were trying
to turn into a conventional portrait.
