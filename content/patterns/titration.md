A client says that they want to discuss a frightening encounter. They begin with the first few seconds and quickly lose track of the therapist's questions. Another week, the same material is approached through a single manageable detail: the moment they noticed someone raising their voice. This time they can describe what happened while remaining aware of the room and their choice to stop.

## Core idea

Titration concerns the amount and intensity of experience approached at once. In Somatic Experiencing, it describes working with charged material in small enough increments that the person can remain engaged and has room to settle before another increment is considered.

A brief image may be more evocative than a long factual account. A single word, a change in proximity, or a therapist's tone can alter intensity. Conversely, someone may discuss a difficult event at length while feeling little immediate activation. The clock alone does not tell the practitioner how much experience is being approached.

The defining move is adjustment of the encounter to the person's present capacity for meaningful participation. Smaller is a relative term: a detail that seems minor to the therapist may carry the entire threat for the client. The unit of work is therefore discovered through feedback rather than assigned by the apparent seriousness of the story.

## What changes in the experience

The relevant observations include the person's own report, capacity to notice what is happening, ability to respond, and availability of choices. Visible quietness does not necessarily indicate comfort. Someone may have become disconnected, compliant, exhausted, or absorbed in a different experience.

In the opening example, the smaller detail is useful only if the person can stay in meaningful contact with it. If it still overwhelms, the work changes. If it feels too remote to matter, a different entry may be considered. A predetermined amount is less informative than ongoing collaboration.

Similarly, practicing a boundary might begin with a small hand movement rather than a forceful push. The smaller action provides information about readiness and conflict. Its value is not that it is easier in an abstract sense, but that it permits observation and choice.

Adjustment may concern content, proximity, sensory detail, duration, or how much interpersonal attention accompanies it. After an adjustment, the practitioner looks for renewed participation: the person can describe a response, disagree with a suggestion, notice another option, or choose to continue. Quietness alone cannot distinguish steadiness from shutting down, and intensity alone cannot distinguish engagement from overwhelm.

## A worked example

The same principle can apply to wanted experience. Imagine someone who longs for recognition but becomes uncomfortable when praised. A therapist's extended appreciation may evoke embarrassment and an urgent need to change the subject. One specific acknowledgment, followed by time to notice the reaction, may be more accessible.

This does not mean the person should eventually tolerate unlimited praise. They may prefer a different form of recognition. Titration helps explore the encounter without overruling that preference.

Suppose the client can receive one specific acknowledgment about a difficult conversation but recoils when it becomes a description of their character. The therapist checks the difference. The client says, 'If I'm the brave one, people expect me to do the next hard thing too.' The issue is now more precise than a general intolerance of praise.

They remain with the acknowledgment of that one action and with the client's right not to repeat it. The client notices pride and then worry. Rather than increasing the dose of appreciation, the therapist makes room for the worry about future demands. The outcome is a more usable form of recognition and clearer knowledge of its conditions. Titration has enabled contact with meaning, not trained the person to endure unlimited admiration.

## Why the move may help

A plausible contribution is that the person retains enough capacity to process what is happening instead of merely surviving the intervention. When an encounter becomes too large, nuanced information may be replaced by urgency, blankness, automatic agreement, or an impulse to escape. An adjustment can restore conditions in which the experience can be investigated.

Somatic Experiencing gives titration a specific place in its clinical model. General support for an entire therapy does not establish which component produced an outcome or the correct dose for a given person. Here the immediate test is functional: has the change made contact more workable, with the person still able to influence it?

## When it helps—and what can get in the way

A manageable increment can support contact with something important. It can also be used to postpone all contact indefinitely. The distinction is functional: does the pacing allow the person to engage with the chosen work, or has regulation become the only acceptable activity?

There is no universal rule that more activation produces more progress. Nor is remaining calm throughout a session necessarily the aim. Emotion can be intense and workable. The pattern asks whether the amount fits this person, this moment, and the purpose of the work.

Titration is particularly relevant when a person wants to approach something but repeatedly loses access to choice once it begins. It may be less useful when the difficulty is uncertainty about whether they want that work at all. Reducing the size of an unwanted intervention does not resolve disagreement about its purpose. Consent to the direction and capacity for the present increment are separate matters.

## Boundaries and common misunderstandings

The most obvious mistake is assuming that willingness implies unlimited capacity. A person can consent to a topic and still need adjustments once its effects become apparent. Consent is revisable.

Another mistake is using titration to override real-world urgency. If someone needs protection from ongoing harm, attending to small sensations cannot substitute for action. Physical symptoms may also require medical attention rather than interpretation as therapeutic activation.

A series of tiny increments can become an endless preparatory project. Conversely, a predetermined progression can become pressure to escalate. Neither the therapist's schedule nor comparison with another client's response should decide when to increase intensity. Meaningful participation may justify staying at the same scale, changing direction, or ending the exploration.
