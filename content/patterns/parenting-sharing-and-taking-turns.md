## 1. A recognizable situation

Your three-year-old has finally worked out how to use the playground digger. Another child approaches and asks for a turn. You say, “Let’s share,” and reach for the handles.

Your child grips harder and shouts, “Mine!” The waiting child starts crying. You feel pressure to demonstrate that your child can be considerate.

But “sharing” is an ambiguous instruction. Are you asking your child to stop immediately, lend something temporarily, divide materials, or include someone in their game?

**Children need help understanding both sides of the arrangement: how to let someone else participate, and how to trust that their own access and boundaries will receive protection.** Getting an object handed over is only one part of that learning.

## 2. What might be happening

Several different difficulties can produce the same refusal.

**The child wants to continue something meaningful.** Another child’s arrival does not make an unfinished activity feel finished. Notice whether disputes happen just as your child becomes engaged, or when someone interrupts a construction or pretend-play story.

**The arrangement is unclear.** “You can have it later” leaves unanswered questions: When? Will someone remember? Will it come back? Watch whether concrete arrangements work better than general requests.

**Waiting exceeds the child’s current abilities.** They may understand whose turn it is and still struggle to remain nearby without grabbing. Compare short, supported waits with long queues or situations after a tiring school day.

**The child is protecting something important.** A favorite possession, carefully arranged collection, or object previously damaged by another child may call for a different response from communal equipment. Ask what they think will happen if they lend it.

**The difficulty involves the social situation.** Perhaps another child repeatedly takes things, refuses to return them, or controls every game. Reluctance with one particular person deserves investigation.

**Giving something up feels costly.** A child can understand fairness while still strongly preferring to keep a desirable resource. In a study of three- to eight-year-olds, younger children endorsed equal sharing well before their actual sticker allocations consistently matched that principle. This was a laboratory finding, not a test of playground behavior, but it cautions against assuming that another explanation of fairness will solve the problem. [Smith, Blake, and Harris, 2013](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0059510)

These are possibilities to investigate. Refusing one request does not reveal a child’s overall generosity or empathy.

## 3. How development changes the picture

### Toddlers: adults organize much of the exchange

A toddler may offer you something willingly and protest when a peer touches it moments later. Consistent sharing is an unrealistic expectation at this stage; guidance and opportunities to participate matter more than reprimands for failing to perform reliably. [Raising Children Network’s developmental guidance](https://raisingchildren.net.au/school-age/behaviour/friends-siblings/sharing)

Use short, visible sequences: “One roll to me, then back to you.” Help the child who is waiting find another activity. Asking two toddlers to negotiate independently adds language and planning demands to an already difficult moment.

Avoid the opposite assumption that toddlers cannot be considerate. Their abilities are emerging and sensitive to the situation.

### Preschoolers: understanding develops faster than reliable performance

Many preschoolers can ask, offer, trade, and follow simple turn-taking arrangements. They still need assistance when the item matters greatly or the wait is uncertain.

Research also distinguishes **following an adult-organized exchange** from **inventing a fair arrangement independently**. In one small experiment, five-year-olds were more successful than three-and-a-half-year-olds at spontaneously alternating access to rewards requiring cooperation. That particular task does not establish a universal age at which children become capable of taking turns. [Melis and colleagues, 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27225221/)

Give preschoolers more responsibility, while retaining help with remembering agreements and carrying out handovers.

### School-age children: investigate fairness and relationships more closely

Children can increasingly plan rotations, discuss ownership, and negotiate exceptions. Ask them to help design arrangements before conflict begins.

A refusal to lend a personal possession is not equivalent to monopolizing shared classroom equipment. With older children, the central issue may be reliability, unequal contributions, or pressure from peers rather than a basic failure to understand turns.

Individual differences matter throughout. A child may need a picture showing the order, a gesture for requesting access, shorter waits, or help shifting between activities. Support should match the specific difficulty rather than chronological age alone.

## 4. What needs balancing

There are several legitimate interests:

- **Time to become absorbed in play.** Constantly interrupting an activity can prevent children from doing anything substantial.

- **Fair access to common resources.** One child’s wish to continue cannot always determine everyone else’s access.

- **Ownership and autonomy.** Lending a treasured possession involves a different obligation from taking turns with a nursery bicycle.

- **Generosity and self-protection.** Children need opportunities to contribute voluntarily and permission to refuse unreasonable demands.

- **Adult practicality.** Parents and educators cannot supervise every exchange indefinitely. Arrangements need to work with the available space, materials, and staffing.

These are partly value judgments. Research can inform how children learn; it cannot determine every family’s rules about lending possessions or every playground’s acceptable turn length.

## 5. Possible responses

### A. Clarify the arrangement before asking for cooperation

**Fits:** recurring disputes about ownership, communal materials, or visitors using toys.

Distinguish three situations:

- **Giving:** the other person keeps what is offered.

- **Lending:** the object returns under an agreed arrangement.

- **Taking turns:** access alternates, usually with shared equipment or materials.

Before a visit, let your child put away a few personal items they do not want used. Agree which remaining materials are available for shared play. Professional guidance supports allowing children some possessions they keep for themselves. [Raising Children Network](https://raisingchildren.net.au/school-age/behaviour/friends-siblings/sharing)

For communal materials, explain the limit concretely: “These markers are for the table. You can use some, and other people need some too.”

My practical recommendation is to examine the setup as well. One highly desirable item among many children, insufficient drawing space, or a game with only one active role creates predictable competition. Redistributing existing materials or offering parallel activities may help without requiring purchases.

Putting away a treasured toy protects a boundary. Removing every opportunity for shared activity would provide little opportunity to learn cooperation.

### B. Make access predictable and support the child who waits

**Fits:** two children want the same shared item now.

First stop grabbing or hurting. Identify the current arrangement briefly: “She is using it. You want a turn.”

Then choose a workable endpoint. Sometimes the current child can finish their activity while the other does something else. When access is limited, a timer or clearly bounded action—such as completing one circuit—may be more practical. ZERO TO THREE recommends both longer uninterrupted turns and timed turns, depending on the circumstances. These are professional recommendations, not evidence that one method is universally superior. [Helping Young Children With Sharing](https://www.zerotothree.org/resource/helping-young-children-with-sharing/)

Stay available for the handover. A timer communicates an endpoint; it cannot carry out the transition for a distressed preschooler.

Help the waiting child act on something specific: choose another activity, wait beside you, or return when called. Follow through when their turn arrives.

Avoid promising access you cannot guarantee. At a public playground, another family may leave before your child receives a turn. Acknowledge that disappointment honestly.

### C. Leave room for genuine choices about giving

**Fits:** lending personal possessions or offering something beyond required access to common resources.

Invite a contribution the child can actually choose:

> “Would you like to offer one of your stickers, or keep them today?”

If refusal is allowed, accept it. If the item is communal and everyone requires access, explain that arrangement directly rather than presenting a compulsory handover as a choice.

In experiments involving three- and four-year-olds, children who chose to give up something valuable subsequently shared more in another task than children in instructed-sharing or less costly conditions. This supports a short-term effect of meaningful choice in that setting. It does **not** show that requiring any turn-taking damages generosity, or that voluntary choices produce lasting changes at home. The proposed explanation—that children infer something about themselves from choosing to give—remains a theoretical interpretation. [Chernyak and Kushnir, 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23955355/)

Related findings vary by age and task. A separate helping experiment found a benefit of choice for five-year-olds but not three-year-olds. Helping and sharing are different behaviors, so that study provides a qualification rather than a direct replication. [Rapp and colleagues, 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28209413/)

### D. Teach the sequence during easier play

**Fits:** a child repeatedly struggles with requesting, waiting, remembering the order, or releasing an item.

Practice the specific missing step when the stakes are low. Roll a ball back and forth, alternate adding pieces to a shared construction, or use a simple game with brief turns. Start with exchanges the child can manage rather than their most treasured possession.

For preschoolers, a visible cue can show whose turn it is. For school-age children, agree on responsibilities: who tracks the order, what counts as a completed turn, and what happens if someone needs help. NAEYC recommends visual reminders and supported problem-solving in preschool and kindergarten settings; these are practice recommendations rather than independently proven remedies for sharing difficulties. [NAEYC guidance](https://www.naeyc.org/node/6065)

The stronger intervention evidence concerns **complete social and emotional learning curricula**. A systematic review identified 79 controlled experimental or quasi-experimental studies involving children aged two to six. Programs improved broad social competence and other outcomes on average. They combined multiple activities and teaching practices; the findings cannot establish that a particular ball game, praise statement, or visual cue works alone. [Blewitt and colleagues, 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30646283/)

For persistent nursery difficulties, ask what skill is being taught and how adults support it during real disputes—not simply whether your child “shared well.”

## 6. Examples in practice

**A two-year-old wants another child’s shovel.**

You prevent grabbing and say, “You want that shovel. He is digging with it.” You offer another way to dig and remain nearby.

Your child cries and rejects the alternative. The other child keeps using the shovel. You support the disappointment without repeatedly asking them to surrender it. The immediate achievement is getting through an unavailable-object situation safely; willing waiting may come later.

**A timer fails with a four-year-old.**

You introduce a timer for a shared toy camera. At the bell, your child screams: they were halfway through photographing a scene.

Later, you discover that the timer repeatedly interrupts a sequence requiring several steps. Next time, you agree on one completed photograph within a reasonable time, help the waiting child choose something else, and supervise the exchange.

Your child may still protest. Changing an impractical endpoint improves the arrangement; it does not guarantee a cheerful handover.

**An eight-year-old refuses to share a game device.**

Initially, this looks like monopolizing. In discussion, you learn that the other child regularly overwrites saved progress.

You establish separate profiles if the device permits, agree on access times, and clarify which settings nobody may change. If the product cannot protect separate progress, choose another arrangement. Cooperation here depends partly on whether the equipment allows fair use without destroying someone’s work.

These examples illustrate how to apply the principles. Their exact procedures have not been tested as individual interventions.

## 7. Common mistakes and limitations

**Using “share” when you mean “stop immediately.”** Name the actual request and explain why it is needed. A child cannot learn a stable arrangement if another person’s request always changes it without warning.

**Treating asking politely as a guarantee of access.** Asking is a useful skill. Accepting “not now” or “that belongs to me” is another.

**Letting the strongest protest determine the outcome.** Notice whether quieter children repeatedly lose access or whether the current user can indefinitely block everyone else. Adults remain responsible for the arrangement.

**Making the timer the authority.** Adults choose the duration and must judge whether it fits the activity. A bell does not make an arbitrary rule fair.

**Interpreting every refusal as a lack of empathy.** A child may understand another person’s wishes while valuing their own unfinished activity more. The learning task includes reconciling competing interests.

**Assuming one developmental timetable is universal.** Cross-cultural research involving children aged three to fourteen found that personally costly prosocial behavior developed differently across societies. Those behavioral tasks do not establish a parenting method, but they caution against treating findings from one population as a universal generosity milestone. [House and colleagues, 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23959869/)

Evidence remains limited on which everyday sharing procedures best protect trust, friendship, and voluntary generosity over years. A successful immediate handover does not establish those longer-term outcomes.

## 8. What to observe over time

Look for a broader set of changes than “gave the toy when told”:

- Can the child request access without grabbing?

- Can they complete a supported handover, even while disappointed?

- Do they increasingly remember the order without adult reminders?

- Can they lend selectively and refuse appropriately?

- Do other children receive fair access?

- Is shared play becoming more enjoyable and less dependent on constant adult intervention?

Distinguish willingness from endurance. A child who silently gives everything away to avoid conflict may need help protecting their access.

If difficulties persist, compare situations. Does the problem concern all exchanges, one treasured item, long waits, a particular peer, or transitions away from absorbing activities? Ask educators whether the child understands the arrangement and what support changes the outcome.

Occasional reluctance alone is not a reason to suspect a disorder. Additional support is reasonable when disputes repeatedly cause injuries, prevent participation or friendships, or accompany substantial communication or broader developmental concerns. Discuss those concrete difficulties with the child’s educator and healthcare professional. Physical aggression requires immediate protection while the underlying sharing problem is investigated. [American Academy of Pediatrics guidance](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/preschool/pages/Social-Development-in-Preschoolers.aspx)
