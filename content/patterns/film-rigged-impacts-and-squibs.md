*Create the visible consequence at the target, then connect it to the apparent cause.*

A character runs down a corridor. Gunfire follows them, chipping the wall just behind their shoulder. Another character takes a hit: their jacket suddenly changes, their body reacts, and they collapse.

The filmmaker needs those events to happen at precise places and moments. The audience needs to understand where the danger is, who has been hit, and how close an escape was.

That creates a useful opportunity. A camera does not need to photograph a projectile travelling from a weapon to its destination. It can photograph a carefully designed disturbance at the destination—and let timing, performance, editing, and sound supply the connection.

## The opening puzzle: how does a wall suddenly appear to be struck?

Imagine a sequence of impact evidence. First there is an intact surface. Then a brief burst of dust and fragments. Finally, a damaged patch remains.

Those three states establish an impact without showing anything arriving.

The reusable pattern is to **prepare the visible result and reveal it on cue**. The same principle works for scenery, props, costumes, and digitally created damage.

## What is a squib?
In film-effects usage, a squib commonly means a small pyrotechnic device used to produce a brief, localized effect, including a simulated bullet hit. It is one tool within the broader family of rigged impacts.

That broader family also includes air-driven effects, mechanically produced damage, breakaway materials, and digital additions. The terminology can be loose: some non-pyrotechnic hit systems are also marketed as “squib” effects. Professional suppliers distinguish several approaches according to the shot’s requirements. [Reference: BloodyStuff’s overview of theatrical hit effects](https://www.bloodystuff.co.uk/hitfx).

The important distinction for understanding the illusion is between **the device that creates an event** and **the finished event the audience sees**. A dust burst, a costume change, and a performer’s fall may involve different techniques, even when they appear to be one instantaneous occurrence.

### Example: *Bonnie and Clyde*—how do dozens of separate effects become one overwhelming ambush?

The ending of *Bonnie and Clyde* (1967) combines visible hits on the characters and car with tightly coordinated performances and changes in screen time.

Director Arthur Penn described three days of filming, carefully arranged cues, squibs, and several cameras operating at different speeds. Warren Beatty and Faye Dunaway supplied the physical reactions. The effects did not perform the scene for them.

Penn also photographed action from closely grouped cameras using different lenses and speeds. Editing could therefore move between immediate violence and prolonged, almost suspended movement. The apparent continuity of the ambush emerged from several coordinated contributions.

The general lesson is that an impact is a dramatic beat. Its timing, the actor’s response, and how long the audience experiences it matter as much as its visible appearance. [Reference and illustrated breakdown: Arthur Penn’s account for the Directors Guild of America](https://www.dga.org/craft/dgaq/issues/0804-winter-2009/shot-to-remember-bonnie-and-clyde).

### Example: *The Matrix*—how does destruction seem to follow a moving stream of gunfire?

During the helicopter rescue in *The Matrix* (1999), windows break, impacts appear inside the office, and water pours through the damaged façade.

Cinematographer Bill Pope described a predetermined pattern of effects, with hits across the interior coordinated with the window destruction. Seen over Neo’s shoulder, the sequence reads as a weapon sweeping across a building.

The production also encountered a revealing problem: the first take exposed part of the mechanism producing the window breakage. The lighting was adjusted before another take.

That is a valuable filmmaking principle. A successful practical effect must produce convincing evidence while keeping its actual cause out of the image. The camera position and illumination are part of the illusion. [Reference and production photographs: *American Cinematographer*, “The Matrix: Welcome to the Machine”](https://theasc.com/article/flashback-the-matrix-cinematography/).

## The effect has three jobs: anticipation, event, and aftermath

An impact often lasts only a moment, but its surrounding states determine whether it feels convincing.

- **Before:** the surface or costume must look ordinary, without advertising the coming effect.
- **During:** the disturbance must register clearly enough to explain what happened.
- **After:** the scene must retain the appropriate damage, debris, or costume change.

The aftermath is especially easy to overlook. A spectacular burst followed by a pristine wall can make the event feel disconnected from its surroundings. Conversely, a modest burst with convincing residual damage can feel substantial.

This also explains why a finished hit may combine practical and digital work. A photographed disturbance can provide texture and interaction, while additional damage is created or maintained afterward.

### Example: *John Wick: Chapter 4*—can the same pattern work when the hits are added later?

Yes. Visual-effects supervisor Janelle Croshaw Ralla reported that the film’s muzzle flashes, bullet hits, and blood hits were added in visual effects.

The production photographed practical effect elements for the visual-effects teams to use, while vendors shared material to maintain a consistent appearance. The filmmakers paid attention to subtle light and reflections that helped integrate the additions.

Crucially, the work included the damage associated with a hit. A momentary spray alone was insufficient; fabric damage also needed to register, even briefly.

This is the same underlying pattern executed through compositing: coordinate a localized event with the photographed action, then make its consequences belong to the scene. [Reference: interviews with supervisors Jonathan Rothbart and Janelle Croshaw Ralla in *befores & afters*](https://beforesandafters.com/2023/05/04/inside-john-wick-4s-insane-arc-de-triomphe-scene-and-the-top-down-dragons-breath-gunfight/).

## Why does a small effect sometimes feel enormous?
Because the audience experiences several signals together.

A localized burst establishes where something happened. A performer’s movement establishes its consequence. A sharp sound establishes its suddenness. A cut or camera movement directs attention toward it.

These signals can reinforce one another without each being spectacular. Their agreement gives the event weight.

The reverse is also true. An early reaction, a late sound, or damage appearing in the wrong place can make an elaborate effect feel strangely unconvincing.

The visible hit and the performer’s movement therefore need separate consideration. A dramatic backward flight belongs to stunt choreography and, where appropriate, separate movement equipment. Effects companies list hit effects and performer-launching systems as distinct capabilities. [Reference: FilmSFX’s overview of hits and stunt effects](https://www.filmsfx.co.uk/).

## Where the pattern becomes difficult
Practical impacts can change the set or costume after every take. Subsequent angles must preserve the correct stage of damage. Multiple cameras can help capture a fleeting event, but each camera also creates another viewpoint from which equipment might become visible.

The photographed appearance matters too. Dust needs separation from its background. A brief event may disappear in motion blur or become unexpectedly conspicuous in slow motion. A burst that dominates a close-up may barely register in a wide shot.

Professional effects, stunt, costume, camera, and visual-effects teams coordinate these requirements; pyrotechnic effects require specialist handling.

## Connections to other patterns

Rigged impacts often work alongside **[breakaway props](/pattern/film-breakaway-props)**, which determine how an object comes apart; **[wire-assisted stunts](/pattern/film-wire-assisted-stunts)**, which shape a performer’s movement; **[overcranking](/pattern/film-overcranking-and-undercranking)**, which extends a brief event; and **compositing**, which adds or adjusts visible consequences.

The distinctive contribution of rigged impacts is precise, localized change: **something happens here, at this moment, and the scene bears the evidence afterward.**
