A therapist notices that every pause has become a problem to solve. While the client searches for words, the therapist searches for the right intervention. The conversation remains courteous, but both people seem to be working hard at being a successful patient and a successful professional. There is little room simply to encounter one another.

## Core idea

Loving presence names a different quality of attention in Hakomi. The practitioner allows something about this particular person to evoke appreciation: their carefulness, humor, determination, tenderness, or willingness to remain in a difficult conversation. Attention becomes receptive and personally engaged. This is a relational stance, rather than a statement delivered to make someone feel better.

Ron Kurtz describes loving presence as a combination of presence and compassion. The educational pattern here is to let appreciative contact influence how the practitioner listens, waits, and responds. The client need not display admirable qualities on request or reciprocate the practitioner's warmth. Appreciation is the practitioner's responsibility to cultivate, not another task for the client.

The defining move happens first in the practitioner: attention returns from an abstract problem to the person encountering it. Appreciation concerns something real and particular, including an effort whose costs are now apparent. It need not endorse the person's actions or assume that every protective strategy remains helpful.

## What changes in the experience

The important difference is responsiveness. There is no prescribed expression of warmth. Some people welcome a gentle smile; others experience it as pity, seduction, or an invitation to please. An appreciative stance remains answerable to the person's actual response.

A practitioner who is less occupied with proving usefulness may notice more: the client's hesitation, their preference for less eye contact, the moment a question becomes intrusive. The intended contribution is an atmosphere in which exploration need not be performed for approval. That is a clinical rationale, not proof that adopting a particular feeling state reliably produces an outcome.

This can change the timing of an encounter. A pause becomes time in which the client may find words, rather than a gap the therapist must fill. A correction becomes information about the person, rather than a threat to professional competence. The therapist still thinks clinically, but that thinking remains responsive to what is actually happening between them.

## A worked example

Consider a client who apologizes whenever they become tearful. A therapist preoccupied with progress might immediately explain why crying is healthy. Another might reassure so quickly that the client never discovers what the apology means. In loving presence, the therapist notices the person's effort to protect the relationship and remains interested in how that effort works. A response can then emerge from contact: the apology may be acknowledged, a pause allowed, or the client asked whether the therapist's attention feels welcome.

Suppose the client replies to a warm reassurance, 'You say that, but you keep handing me tissues before I even cry.' The therapist recognizes an attempt to help that has also communicated discomfort. They stop reaching for the box and ask what the client noticed. The client says that being watched for tears makes them feel fragile and conspicuous.

Appreciative contact now takes a less expressive form: the therapist gives more space, stays interested in the story, and accepts the preference without explaining it away. The client eventually cries and then becomes annoyed. Neither response needs to confirm that the therapist has achieved loving presence. The useful change is that the client has more room to be specific and less work managing the encounter.

## Why the move may help

One plausible contribution is a change in the therapist's own reactivity. When the client is experienced only as resistant, demanding, or difficult to help, interventions can become corrective or defensive. Remembering the person's efforts and individuality may widen the practitioner's responses. Whether this feels respectful is a separate question answered through the encounter.

Hakomi presents loving presence as a foundational attitude. The visible observations are smaller: fewer interruptions, a more accurate response, a repair that is accepted, or greater freedom to disagree. Those observations do not establish an isolated causal effect of this stance, and a therapist's private feeling of compassion is not a measure of the client's outcome.

## When it helps—and what can get in the way

The stance is especially relevant when the relationship has become mechanical, when silence triggers pressure to intervene, or when a person's distress has eclipsed their individuality. It also matters during ordinary, undramatic moments. A person can experience interest without disclosing increasingly painful material.

A client arrives angry about a scheduling mistake. The therapist feels defensive and starts mentally explaining the calendar. Loving presence does not require pretending to enjoy the complaint. It can begin with recognizing the client's effort to speak directly about something that mattered, while also acknowledging the therapist's irritation.

The therapist takes responsibility for the mistake and listens to its impact. If appreciation helps the therapist stay curious rather than retaliate or become overly accommodating, it supports the encounter. If it becomes a sentimental story about how courageous the client is, it may obscure the concrete repair that is needed.

This illustrates a useful boundary: caring attention does not replace accountability. It should make accountability easier to bear.

## Boundaries and common misunderstandings

Loving presence is easily confused with constant positivity, personal affection, or an obligation to remain endlessly available. Professional limits remain necessary. A clear ending time, an acknowledged misunderstanding, or a firm boundary may be the most caring response available.

It can also become a demand that clients recognize the therapist's good intentions. Someone who feels watched, patronized, or uncomfortable is providing relevant information. Their disagreement does not demonstrate an inability to receive love. Cultural expectations, prior experiences, disability, and differences in power influence how attention is experienced.

An important misunderstanding is that appreciation must be announced. Naming a quality can be helpful, but it can also put the client in the position of accepting a flattering description. Quietly allowing appreciation to change listening may be more appropriate. Cultural expectations, power differences, and the person's preferences all shape how warmth is received.

## Lineage and sources

The source tradition is [Ron Kurtz's account of Loving Presence](https://bti.edu/pdfs/Kurtz_Loving-Presence.pdf). This article offers original examples of a professional relational stance, without claiming that the stance is an independently validated treatment.

The cases here are fictional illustrations. Loving presence is retained as Hakomi's term; the account describes a professional stance rather than a stand-alone treatment protocol.
