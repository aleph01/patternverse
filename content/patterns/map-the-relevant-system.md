## Purpose and problem

Fourteen-year-old Sam keeps missing the first lesson. School describes an attendance problem. His mother, Jo, describes a daily battle. Sam says he gets headaches and wishes everyone would stop asking. Each account points toward a different person who needs to change.

Before choosing an explanation, it helps to ask what the first account leaves outside the picture. Who is involved in getting Sam to school? What changed before the absences began? Which arrangements make attendance harder, and which relationships make school more bearable?

Sam and Jo are fictional. Their situation introduces a practical systemic move: expand the field of attention until the difficulty can be understood in its relevant setting.

## Core idea

**Map the Relevant System means drawing the boundary around the question, then revising that boundary as people explain their lives.**

The person experiencing distress remains in view. Alongside them come significant relationships, histories, services, resources, and constraints. A boundary is useful when it includes information that could change the understanding or the available response.

UCL's systemic competence framework places individuals within significant relationships, communities, professional networks, cultural settings, and wider political circumstances. It also recognizes physical health and development. Contextual understanding therefore leaves room for both relational and individual explanations. [UCL: Basic systemic competences](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/brain-sciences/sites/brain_sciences/files/migrated-files/Systemic_basic_systemic_competences.pdf)

The map answers **what needs to be considered**. It does not, by itself, establish **what caused the difficulty**. Putting two things on the same page creates a question about their connection, not proof of one.

## How it works

### Choose a question before choosing a diagram

A map for understanding difficult mornings will differ from one about loneliness after retirement. Starting with a question gives a reason to include a person, institution, event, or practical condition. Without that purpose, mapping can become an exhausting inventory.

Useful information may concern who shares daily life, who makes decisions, who provides support, what happened over time, and which circumstances restrict choice. Rent, transport, shift patterns, accessible buildings, and eligibility rules may matter as much as relationships inside the home.

### Choose a form that serves the conversation

A **genogram** represents family relationships across generations and can include significant events and relational histories. An **ecomap** places a person or household alongside connections with the surrounding environment. A timeline may better reveal a move, illness, separation, or service change. UCL includes genograms and ecomaps among systemic mapping techniques. [UCL: Specific systemic techniques](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/brain-sciences/sites/brain_sciences/files/migrated-files/Systemic_specific_systemic_techniques.pdf)

These tools have overlapping histories. Ann Hartman's ecological social-work account describes diagramming family and community relationships; the emphasis includes connections with resources and environmental demands. This article brings such mapping ideas together as an editorial pattern, rather than presenting one standardized clinical method. [Hartman: Diagrammatic Assessment of Family Relationships](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/104438947805900803)

A conversation or a few words on paper may be sufficient. A technically elaborate diagram can be less useful than a simple representation someone can recognize and correct.

### Keep different accounts visible

One person may describe a relative as reliable support while another experiences that relative's involvement as intrusive. Record whose account a description represents. An absent person's intentions remain unknown unless there is evidence for them.

Distinguish reported circumstances, interpretations, and questions. “The bus arrives after registration” differs from “School does not care.” Both may matter, but they need different kinds of inquiry. This distinction makes correction easier and helps prevent a professional's first interpretation from becoming the official story.

## Worked examples and case studies

### Sam's mornings: widening an attendance problem

*Fictional teaching scenario. All dialogue and outcomes are invented.*

Sam wants to attend without feeling sick. Jo wants him in school without risking her job. School staff want more consistent attendance. Initially, their discussion concentrates on Sam's motivation and Jo's ability to enforce routines.

The therapist asks, “Could we put a difficult morning on the page, including the things none of you gets to choose?” Jo explains that her employer recently moved her to an early shift. Following a house move, Sam now uses a bus that reaches school after the first lesson starts. Sam adds that arriving late means walking into class while everyone watches. A pastoral lead he trusts is available later in the morning. His headaches have not yet been medically assessed.

The map now includes Sam, Jo, school, transport, work, and a health question. It also reveals an existing supportive relationship. Nobody has established a single cause of the absences. Several concrete obstacles and questions have become harder to overlook.

Jo feels less personally accused but points out that she cannot simply change shifts. School staff initially worry that an adjusted arrival arrangement will lower expectations. Sam is willing to speak with the pastoral lead but does not want the whole class told about his situation.

In this invented continuation, the school agrees to a temporary arrival arrangement while transport options are reviewed, and Jo arranges a health appointment. Sam misses fewer first lessons over the next fortnight, but still feels anxious about entering class. The map has helped identify possible responses; it has neither explained away his symptoms nor resolved the remaining distress.

The important change in understanding is that “make Sam comply” no longer describes the whole task. Some relevant decisions belong to adults and institutions around him.

### Caregiving beyond one person's coping: a published case

*Published exploratory single-case study in family-systems nursing, summarized from Wildhaber and colleagues (2025).*

At a Swiss Family Systems Care Unit, a single mother took part in three therapeutic conversations. She had a 12-year-old son with intellectual disability, a nine-year-old daughter, and disabling health problems of her own. A nearby grandmother helped; relationships with the children's father were strained.

The counselor began drawing a combined genogram and ecomap while exploring everyday life. The conversations included the mother's wish for peace, the children's different needs, and the support available around them. Her daughter sometimes helped her brother while also wanting more attention from her mother. Rather than treating this only as a problem of the mother's coping, the discussion made the distribution of care and opportunities for personal space visible. The paper also describes her distress about political injustice and feeling misunderstood by people around her, bringing the wider social context into the account.

One possibility discussed was time for mother and daughter together while the grandmother cared for the son. The paper describes this as an option explored, not a verified arrangement with a demonstrated outcome. The counselor also acknowledged the mother's efforts; the mother was open to considering possible changes.

The authors interpreted the conversations as helping her experience greater ease and develop new perspectives while substantial burdens remained. This account concerns the whole sequence of conversations, which included several interventions. It cannot isolate an effect of drawing the map, establish causation, or demonstrate general effectiveness. [Wildhaber and colleagues: exploratory single-case study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12623666/)

For this field guide, the case illustrates a specific distinction: identifying a resource means exploring its actual availability and the needs of everyone involved. A grandmother appearing on a map does not make her unlimited capacity an established fact.

### When a family-centered map misses the relevant support

*Fictional teaching scenario. All dialogue and outcomes are invented.*

Leila seeks help with panic and disrupted sleep after leaving a controlling partner. She wants to keep working and manage contact safely. The therapist begins with relatives, assuming they will be the main source of support, and asks about involving her mother.

Leila becomes guarded. Her mother remains in touch with the former partner; Leila does not want her current address passed on. Meanwhile, a housemate accompanies her to appointments, and a friend helps her manage unwanted messages. Neither appears on the first map. Unpredictable work shifts are also making appointments difficult.

The initial attempt has mistaken biological connection for available support. The therapist acknowledges the assumption and asks which connections Leila wants represented, and which information requires particular care. They revise the map to include her housemate, friend, workplace arrangements, and relevant services. Discussing someone on the map is explicitly separated from permission to contact them.

Leila supplies more detail once that distinction is clear, although she remains cautious about what enters the record. The revised understanding suggests practical questions about appointment access and specialist support. It does not establish that the former partner is safe, or resolve the housing and employment constraints.

Here, mapping helps by correcting the professional's boundary. Expanding a map does not require expanding attendance at a session.

## Conditions for useful application

People need to understand why information is being requested and how it may be recorded or shared. They should have meaningful opportunities to correct descriptions, decline unnecessary detail, and discuss concerns about involving others, within the service's responsibilities.

The representation also needs to be accessible. Drawing, speaking, translated conversation, or supported communication may suit different people. When a child or someone needing communication support is involved, an adult's account should not silently become their only voice.

Safety can change how inquiry is conducted. NICE recommends private, one-to-one enquiry about domestic abuse in a setting where the person feels safe. A joint discussion with someone who intimidates them may conceal information and increase risk. [NICE: Domestic violence and abuse, recommendation 6](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ph50/chapter/Recommendations#recommendation-6-ensure-trained-staff-ask-people-about-domestic-violence-and-abuse)

## When to use it

This pattern is useful when accounts repeatedly locate the difficulty in one person, when several services are involved, when a major transition has changed daily arrangements, or when an apparently reasonable plan is impractical in the person's actual circumstances.

Two brief fictional contrasts show how the boundary depends on the question. An older adult's missed appointments may involve a distant clinic, hearing difficulties, and a neighbor who can sometimes provide transport. A student living abroad may have substantial emotional support from relatives overseas but nobody locally who can help during an illness. “Has support” is too coarse a description in either case.

Mapping need not precede every response. Immediate danger, urgent medical needs, or a straightforward practical obstacle may call for action before a broader account is developed.

## Tradeoffs

Widening attention takes time and may uncover needs that the therapist or service cannot meet. It can also create additional disclosure and coordination burdens. An institution belongs in the picture when it matters, but its presence does not give the person control over its decisions.

A useful stopping point is reached when further detail would not presently change the understanding or next decision. The map can remain incomplete. Sometimes acknowledging a missing resource is more accurate than producing a longer list of people who might help.

## Common mistakes and limitations

**Turning context into blame.** A family's responses may be relevant without causing a condition. Physical illness, disability, individual experience, and relational circumstances can require attention together.

**Mistaking connections for causes.** A line between two people records a relationship. A history of repeated events suggests questions. Neither establishes a motive, diagnosis, or causal explanation.

**Assuming family means support.** Kinship, co-residence, and legal responsibility do not establish safety, willingness, or capacity. Chosen relationships may be central; formally available services may be inaccessible.

**Confusing influence with responsibility.** Someone's efforts to survive coercion do not make them equally responsible for it. Mapping the context must preserve accountability for harmful choices.

**Completing the diagram instead of listening.** Categories and symbols are aids. If the map repeatedly needs to overrule someone's account to remain tidy, the representation needs revision.

The proposed value of this pattern is a more adequate basis for inquiry and decisions. A competence framework describes practice, and a case study illustrates it; neither establishes that mapping alone improves clinical outcomes.
