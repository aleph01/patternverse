A person says that they are afraid to show unfinished work. The explanation is sensible but distant, like a report about somebody else. Then, imagining the colleague whose judgment matters, they say, "I need you to see me as capable. If you see me struggle, I expect you to stop trusting me." The same concern is now expressed from within its emotional perspective.

## Core idea

An Overt Statement, in Coherence Therapy, is a candid, present-tense expression of emotional knowledge. It may name an expectation, a purpose, a feared consequence, or what feels necessary to do. The statement is shaped and revised until it fits the person's experience.

The difference is not simply between silent thought and spoken words. Someone can recite a sentence without contacting it. The clinical intention is to bring felt meaning and language together, so the person recognizes the perspective as something operating in their experience.

The sentence need not fit a formula. Strong emotion is not required. A quiet recognition may be sufficient, and a dramatic statement may be inaccurate. The person can soften, strengthen, or reject the therapist's suggested wording.

The defining move is ownership of a particular emotional position in the present encounter. A person may acknowledge an expectation they do not endorse intellectually: 'I know you may not do this, and this is what I expect.' That distinction allows the learning to be contacted without asking the person to adopt it as a considered belief.

## What changes in the experience

In the work example, "I have perfectionism" is a broad description. "I cannot let you see uncertainty because I expect you to withdraw your trust" specifies the relationship and consequence. That specificity creates more to explore: whether the expectation applies to every colleague, what counts as uncertainty, and what the person knows about this relationship now.

Distinguishing an emotional expectation from an external fact allows the emotional position to be acknowledged without endorsing its prediction. Immediately replacing it with reassurance may prevent the person from fully recognizing it. Treating it as objective truth is equally unhelpful.

An overt statement also differs from an affirmation. It is not a preferred belief repeated to create confidence. It may be uncomfortable because it names a position the person dislikes or considers unreasonable. Its usefulness comes from accurate articulation, not positivity.

Attention shifts from a category such as perfectionism to a relationship between action and anticipated consequence. The wording can be revised as it becomes clearer who is being addressed, what is feared, and what the response is trying to prevent. Stronger language is not necessarily more accurate; a conditional or partial statement may fit better.

## A worked example

A person repeatedly declines assistance. Through exploration, a statement emerges: "I want your help, but if I accept it, I expect you to decide what happens next." This makes both the wish and the objection visible. Practical negotiation about the terms of assistance may now be possible.

The statement does not prove that declining help has only one cause. Health, workload, previous experiences, and the actual helper's behavior remain relevant. The emotional logic is a contribution to understanding, not a complete theory of the person.

Suppose the therapist hears this as unwillingness to depend on anyone. The client corrects that account: they are comfortable depending on a friend for practical help, but not on a parent who links assistance to advice about every subsequent choice. The statement becomes more specific: accepting help from this person feels like surrendering the right to make an unpopular decision.

Speaking that position brings sadness rather than relief. The client wanted help without having to protect autonomy at the same time. The therapist stays with the meaning and the actual relationship instead of replacing it with a positive affirmation. The outcome is a clearer position from which to evaluate the offer, including whether its conditions really are acceptable.

## Why the move may help

An emotional expectation may remain difficult to work with while it is described only from outside. A first-person statement can make its logic and stakes more available: this is what seems likely, and this is why the response makes sense from that perspective. It can also reveal where a general explanation has been too broad.

Coherence Therapy uses overt statements to help integrate discovered emotional knowledge and support later work with it. The presence of a candid sentence does not establish that a memory has been reconsolidated or that the symptom will change. Immediate recognition, later recall, and durable behavioral change are different observations.

## When it helps—and what can get in the way

The pattern is useful when someone can discuss a difficulty without recognizing the particular expectation involved, or when a newly discovered meaning needs to become more explicit and memorable. In Coherence Therapy it can support discovery and integration before further work with contradiction.

Suppose someone says, "If I become independent, my family will have no use for me." The statement can accurately convey an expectation without establishing that the family will actually respond that way. It may reflect a current relational condition, an older learning, or a mixture of both.

The pattern fits a newly discovered meaning that is difficult to hold onto after the session or remains an abstract theory about oneself. It may be unnecessary when the person already has clear access to the expectation. In that case, repeating it can become rehearsal without new learning, and practical or relational work may be more useful.

## Boundaries and common misunderstandings

It should not become a confession extracted by the therapist. A person may feel pressured to repeat words that sound psychologically sophisticated. A sentence that does not fit is not evidence of resistance. Memory-related statements require particular care: felt conviction cannot establish the occurrence or details of an event.

A therapist can make an overt statement sound like a confession of a secret motive. That can create shame or encourage assent to an account the person does not recognize. The statement should remain voluntary and corrigible. Its emotional truth concerns the person's current expectation, not proof that the predicted outcome is inevitable or deserved.
