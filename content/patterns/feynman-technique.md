Some ideas feel clear until you try to explain them. Familiar terminology can create an impression of understanding while quietly carrying most of the meaning for you. You recognize the words, follow the explanation, and feel that the concept makes sense—until you attempt to reconstruct it yourself.

The Feynman Technique tests this by asking you to explain an idea in your own words. Whenever the explanation becomes vague, circular, or dependent on a term you cannot unpack, you have found a place where your understanding may still be thin.

Suppose you say that inflation happens because “the purchasing power of money decreases.” The sentence may be correct, but it does not explain much. Why does purchasing power decrease? Why do prices rise? What role do supply, demand, wages, expectations, or monetary conditions play? Each attempt to unpack the explanation reveals whether the words are carrying genuine understanding or merely familiarity.

The technique is often summarized as “explain it to a child,” but simplicity is not really the point. Some topics genuinely require technical language. A musician may need terms such as tonic and dominant; a biologist may need to talk about alleles; a psychologist may need the language of reinforcement or working memory.

The stronger principle is explanatory accountability. If you use a term, you should have some grasp of what work that term is doing. Technical vocabulary should compress understanding rather than replace it.

The technique also works well outside academic subjects. Try explaining why a scene in a film feels tense, what makes a particular painting visually balanced, why one joke works better than another, or why a conflict between two people keeps escalating. The moment your explanation turns into “it just does” or repeats the original claim in different words, you have found something worth examining.

A good explanation does not need to be elegant on the first attempt. In fact, roughness is useful. The point is to expose where the explanatory chain breaks, return to the source or examples, repair that gap, and try again.

The Feynman Technique is therefore less about sounding simple than about discovering where your own understanding stops.
