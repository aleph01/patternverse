## A familiar moment

A friend says, “I spent the whole afternoon preparing, and they cancelled five minutes before we were supposed to meet.” You want to help. “Maybe something came up,” you reply. “At least you have a free evening.” Your friend becomes quiet.

Your explanation may be possible, but it has moved past the disappointment before acknowledging it. Another beginning could be: “You put a lot into getting ready, and the cancellation came at the last minute. I can understand feeling let down.” The facts about the cancellation remain incomplete. The experience has become more understandable.

## Understand the skill

In Dialectical Behavior Therapy, or DBT, **validation** means recognizing what is accurate or understandable in a person's experience. **Self-validation** applies the same stance to your own experience. It can involve acknowledging a feeling, reflecting what was said, or recognizing relevant circumstances.

Validation does not establish that every interpretation is true or that every action is effective. You can understand anger while setting a limit on shouting. You can acknowledge fear while checking whether the predicted danger is present. The useful question is what can be recognized honestly, rather than what reassuring phrase will make the feeling disappear.

[DBT Self Help's validation guide](https://dbtselfhelp.com/validation/) describes several levels of validation, from paying attention and accurate reflection to understanding context and relating as equals. These are available ways of responding, not a checklist to perform in every conversation. They share attention, accuracy, and respect.

## When to use it

Try validation when someone seems unheard, when you rush to advice, or when disagreement is making it difficult to acknowledge any understandable experience. Try self-validation when a feeling is followed by “I shouldn't feel this” or a sweeping judgment about yourself.

It helps to distinguish listening from problem solving. Someone may want practical help; ask rather than assuming either way. Validation alone cannot resolve an unfair workload, repair harm, or ensure safety. If a person pressures you to validate an accusation as fact or accept mistreatment, you can acknowledge what they feel while keeping your own account and limits. You do not have to continue a threatening conversation to demonstrate understanding.

## How to practice

This short sequence adapts established validation principles for everyday use. There is no requirement to guess a person's history or discover an explanation for every feeling.

1. **Pay attention.** Listen to the words and notice your urge to correct, compare, reassure, or give advice. For yourself, pause long enough to notice what is present.
2. **Describe accurately.** Reflect an event or stated feeling without exaggerating it. “You said the last-minute change was disappointing” stays close to what you know.
3. **Check any inference.** If you name something unspoken, make it tentative: “Was part of it feeling overlooked?” Accept a correction.
4. **Recognize relevant context.** Use circumstances or history the person has actually shared. You might recognize the effort invested, an important value, or how the present situation would affect many people. Do not invent a cause.
5. **Respond as an equal.** Use language you mean. Understanding can include uncertainty, disagreement, and boundaries without talking down to the person.
6. **Choose whether anything else is wanted.** Ask about listening, practical help, or space. For yourself, consider what support or next action fits without making it a condition of acknowledging the feeling.

[This self-validation lesson](https://dialecticalbehaviortherapy.com/emotion-regulation/self-validation/) similarly emphasizes acknowledging experience and understanding its context.

## See it in action

After the cancellation, you try again: “I went straight to explaining it. You spent the afternoon preparing, and then the plan changed at the last minute.” Your friend says, “Yes. I feel as though my time doesn't matter.”

You can reflect the experience without endorsing a claim about the other person's intention: “It left you feeling that your effort wasn't being considered. Have I understood?” Your friend corrects you: “More that I didn't get any warning.” You adjust: “The lack of warning is the part that stings.”

You then ask, “Would it help to think about what to say, or would you rather I just listen?” They want to talk for a few minutes before deciding. You do not need to produce a solution immediately.

Self-validation might later sound like: “I'm uncomfortable that I missed what they needed. I was trying to help, and I can listen more carefully now.” That account acknowledges your intention and the effect of the first response. It does not require either blaming yourself or insisting that your friend should have appreciated your advice.

## Try it yourself

**Find Words That Acknowledge** takes about 6–10 minutes. Choose self-validation, validating another person, or a fictional example. You will separate what is known from what you are guessing, identify relevant context, and draft a response you can honestly stand behind.

You can rehearse the wording aloud or silently and revise it. You will leave with a short validation statement, an accuracy check where relevant, and any boundary or next action you choose. This interactive sequence is an original adaptation and cannot determine another person's actual feelings.

## When it feels difficult

**“Validating means agreeing.”** Identify one part you can recognize without accepting the whole account. “That uncertainty sounds difficult” does not mean “Your prediction is certain.” Start by reflecting only the feeling or event the person has stated.

**“I don't know why this reaction makes sense.”** Avoid manufacturing a history or motive. Say what you do know and ask a respectful question. For yourself, “This feeling is here, even though I don't understand it yet” can be a smaller first step.

**“The kind words sound false.”** Replace generic reassurance with a specific observation. “You spent hours preparing” may be easier to mean than “Anyone would be devastated.” Start with one sentence and check its accuracy. You do not need to praise, intensify, or soften every experience to recognize it.

## Bring it into daily life

During one ordinary conversation, reflect what you heard before offering an explanation. Let the person's correction change your understanding. During self-criticism, try one accurate description of experience before deciding what to change.

What can you acknowledge honestly, and what do you still need to ask rather than assume?

“When I notice myself rushing to correct a feeling, I can try acknowledging the experience and checking what I understood.”
