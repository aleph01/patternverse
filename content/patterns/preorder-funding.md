## What it is for

A business may have customers ready to buy before it has the cash to manufacture their orders. Components, tooling, and factory deposits need paying now; the finished product will arrive months later.

Preorder Funding brings customer payments forward so they can help finance the work required to deliver the product.

It is useful when demand exists, delivery takes time, and the business can make a credible promise about what customers will receive. It can reduce the money tied up in inventory and the amount of outside capital needed for a launch or production run.

## The core idea

Sell a defined product before it is ready for delivery, collect some or all of the payment, and use the available proceeds to help produce and fulfil those orders.

The sequence becomes:

**Receive orders and payments → make or procure the product → deliver to customers.**

The timing of payment matters. Preorders can involve full payment, a deposit, or no payment until later. Only money collected and made available early can fund production. An unpaid reservation can inform planning without supplying cash. [Shopify: Preorders](https://help.shopify.com/en/manual/products/purchase-options/pre-orders)

Customers receive a promise of future delivery. They do not acquire ownership in the business simply by paying early.

## How it works

![Preorder Funding: 100 customers pay $100 upfront, providing $10,000 before production. After $8,000 in production and fulfilment costs, $2,000 remains before other costs. Deposits provide less early funding, while charging at shipment provides none before production.](../assets/business/preorder-funding.svg)

A business presents a product with a price, specifications, expected delivery period, and clear payment and cancellation terms.

It may collect orders during a limited window, produce once a threshold is reached, or accept preorders continuously for scheduled batches. A minimum threshold is useful when manufacturing requires a certain quantity, but it is not essential to the pattern.

In the hypothetical example above, 100 customers each pay $100 before production. The business receives $10,000 and spends $8,000 making and delivering their orders.

With a $25 deposit instead, those same orders provide only $2,500 upfront. Another source must cover the remaining spending before the balances arrive.

The financing benefit therefore depends on **how much cash arrives, when it becomes available, and when costs fall due**.

## Why it works

**It reduces the gap between spending and collection.** Customer payments can cover materials, supplier deposits, or manufacturing bills that would otherwise require the business’s own money or borrowing.

**It connects production with demonstrated demand.** Paid orders provide information about quantities, sizes, colors, and configurations before the business commits to a batch.

**It can reduce unsold inventory.** Producing against orders reduces dependence on forecasting what customers might buy. The benefit is strongest when the business keeps speculative production beyond those orders under control.

**It gives customers a reason to commit early.** Customers may value securing availability, receiving an early price, or helping a product they want reach production. The exchange must justify their wait.

These benefits rely on trust. The longer customers must wait, and the less established the product or company, the more reassurance they need about delivery.

## Famous examples

### Fairphone: customer payments enabled the first production run

Fairphone’s first phone provides a direct example. In 2013, the company reached a milestone of **5,000 preorders at €325 each**. Fairphone explained that this enabled a substantial down payment toward development and production, allowing the project to proceed toward a larger batch. [Fairphone’s announcement](https://www.fairphone.com/nl/stories/5000-fairphones-sold-lets-start-production)

Customers’ early payments helped turn interest into manufacturing activity. They were also accepting the uncertainty of buying from a company that had never delivered a phone.

Fairphone subsequently described continuing to finance production through preorders. This shows how the pattern can extend beyond a single launch into successive product runs. [Fairphone’s account of its early growth](https://www.fairphone.com/stories/weve-reached-100000-fairphone-owners)

### Tesla Model 3: refundable reservations supplied partial funding

Tesla’s Model 3 launch illustrates the deposit-based version—and its limits.

In its report for the first nine months of 2016, Tesla recorded a **$409.1 million increase in customer deposits**, primarily resulting from Model 3 reservations. Those deposits contributed to operating cash flow before the corresponding vehicles were delivered. Tesla also warned that Model 3 reservations were refundable and might never become vehicle sales. [Tesla’s September 2016 quarterly report](https://ir.tesla.com/_flysystem/s3/sec/000156459016026820/tsla-10q_20160930.html)

The deposits supplied cash and indicated interest, while creating a potential refund obligation. They covered only part of the eventual purchase price and did not establish that customers had committed to completing their purchases.

This is a neighboring form of preorder funding: paid reservations can contribute funding even when they are not completed purchase agreements.

### Taylor Stitch: applying customer funding to recurring clothing releases

In a 2019 interview, Taylor Stitch cofounder Michael Maher described a hybrid approach: essential products remained in stock, while other designs were crowdfunded through the company’s Workshop. He connected this customer participation to funding growth and limiting overproduction. [Interview with Michael Maher](https://www.entrepreneur.com/leadership/taylor-stitch-makes-us-rethink-our-wardrobe/336272)

Taylor Stitch’s sales terms identify Workshop purchases as preorders with estimated shipping periods. [Taylor Stitch’s terms of sale](https://www.taylorstitch.com/pages/terms-of-sale)

The example shows how advance ordering can become part of an established retailer’s operating model. Customers help support upcoming releases, while immediately available staples serve people who prefer to buy from stock.

## The economics

Preorder Funding changes the timing of cash. It does not automatically improve the profit on each order.

An early discount may make customers more willing to wait, but it also reduces the money left after delivery costs. Payment fees, marketing, customer support, returns, warranty claims, and development expenses still matter.

In the graphic’s example, the $2,000 remaining after production and fulfilment is before those other costs, taxes, and refunds. It is not automatically profit.

Access to the money matters too. A payment provider may delay payouts or retain a reserve. Cash held back cannot pay a factory bill today.

The most useful financial question is whether the business can cover its **largest cash shortfall before delivery**, allowing for plausible delays and cancellations. A well-funded order book can still conceal a shortage if major expenses arrive before customer payments become available.

## Useful when

Preorder Funding is particularly useful when:

- A product has credible demand and a reasonably dependable path to delivery.
- Production requires minimum quantities or substantial supplier deposits.
- Customers accept waiting in exchange for availability or another clear benefit.
- Demand varies across versions, making advance orders valuable for planning.
- The business can explain the product and its remaining uncertainties clearly.

It is less suitable when development remains highly speculative, costs cannot yet be estimated reliably, or customers need immediate delivery.

## Common mistakes and limits

**Treating every preorder as funding.** Orders charged only at shipment help forecast demand but leave the production financing problem largely unchanged.

**Spending customer money without protecting delivery.** Early receipts have work attached to them. Using them for unrelated expansion can leave accepted orders underfunded.

**Overpromising the schedule.** Tooling changes, quality problems, and supplier delays can lengthen the wait. For covered US merchandise orders, sellers need a reasonable basis for shipping promises and must follow delay-notification and cancellation requirements when those promises cannot be met. [FTC guidance on delivery obligations](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/business-guide-ftcs-mail-internet-or-telephone-order-merchandise-rule)

**Ignoring cancellation and return exposure.** Refundable deposits can leave quickly. Delivered orders may also generate returns or support costs after the apparent funding benefit has passed.

**Mistaking launch enthusiasm for lasting demand.** Early buyers may be unusually committed or attracted by special pricing. Their orders do not establish that subsequent batches will sell at sustainable prices.

**Depending on the next batch to finish the previous one.** If each release needs fresh preorder receipts to fulfil older commitments, strong sales can obscure an accumulating shortfall. Each batch needs a credible funding and delivery plan.
