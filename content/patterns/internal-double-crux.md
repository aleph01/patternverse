You have agreed to help organize a neighborhood event. Each evening you open the planning document, feel a tightening in your chest, and close it again. One explanation is that you lack discipline. Another is that you should never have volunteered. Both explanations arrive quickly; neither tells you much about the actual disagreement.

Something in you wants to be someone people can rely on. Something else remembers how the last small favor expanded into weeks of unpaid coordination. Taking either concern seriously seems to endanger the other.

Internal Double Crux gives that disagreement a structure in which both perspectives can contribute information. Its useful question is what becomes visible when neither position is dismissed before it has been understood.

## A conversation with room to change

In the CFAR Handbook, the technique begins with an internal disagreement and fair names for its sides. Each perspective speaks from its own position. After the opening contribution, a turn acknowledges something true in the previous contribution and adds one concern. Alternation continues as the disagreement becomes clearer or changes shape. A moderator stance helps keep the exchange fair and manageable.

The established name connects this exercise with Double Crux, a method for investigating disagreement between people. The internal version described here is a structured exchange; it does not require locating a single factual proposition that would settle everything.

Parts language is optional. “Keeping my word” and “protecting my time” can simply name two concerns. They need not become permanent characters or an account of how the mind is literally organized.

## When helping has become an unlimited promise

In the event example, “responsible me” and “selfish me” would load the outcome before the conversation began. More balanced names might be “honor the commitment” and “keep the work bounded.” The following exchange is a fictional illustration:

**Honor the commitment:** “People are relying on us, and another week of silence leaves them guessing.”

**Keep the work bounded:** “Leaving them without an answer does make their job harder. I am worried that answering will be treated as agreeing to manage everything.”

**Honor the commitment:** “Our last message was vague enough that they could expect more than we meant. I want to distinguish changing the agreement from disappearing.”

**Keep the work bounded:** “Those are different. I could imagine offering one defined task, but I do not believe a limit unless we tell someone what happens to the remaining work.”

The conversation has uncovered an unresolved agreement. A pleasant internal compromise would leave that agreement untouched. A practical response might be to tell the organizer exactly what can be done and ask who will take the rest.

The person could still decide to withdraw. They could also discover that the original commitment was already specific and that they misunderstood it. The value lies in making the disagreement answerable to details of the actual situation.

## An acknowledgment has to carry information

“Yes, but” can be a way of waiting for another chance to argue. A substantive acknowledgment identifies something the other perspective has made harder to overlook. It need not endorse the whole position.

For example, “You are right that we need rest” becomes more useful when it changes an assumption: “I was planning as though tomorrow's energy were guaranteed, even though this week has already been exhausting.” The latter gives the next decision something to work with.

One concern per turn helps keep a reply possible. If a perspective presents missed deadlines, disappointed friends, financial worries, and an entire account of personal failure at once, the response may address only the easiest point. Slowing the exchange makes omissions easier to notice.

These are practical reasons the structure might help. They are not evidence that following it will produce agreement or better decisions in every case.

## When the quieter concern has no argument yet

A designer wants to release a portfolio and repeatedly postpones it. The releasing perspective can explain the opportunity in polished sentences. The hesitant response offers only an image: standing on a brightly lit stage with no way to leave.

Treating that image as an inferior argument would miss the question it raises. Given time, the designer recognizes that publicizing the portfolio feels like promising to accept any resulting work. They have not decided what commissions they would actually want.

The concern can now be investigated: does the site make that promise, or could it state a narrower scope? Would a contact form create a choice or an obligation? An inquiry policy might address part of the hesitation; uncertainty about the work itself might remain.

An image or bodily response does not establish an external fact. It can suggest a question that the current explanation has left out. [[contacting-the-felt-sense|Contacting the Felt Sense]] and [[finding-and-resonating-a-handle|Finding and Resonating a Handle]] develop this kind of attention without requiring immediate verbal clarity.

## Agreement can arrive too quickly

Anna Salamon's retrospective on CFAR raises a concern about versions of Internal Double Crux: verbal reasoning can overpower less verbal reservations, producing apparent agreement without adequate understanding. She presents this as a personal interpretation supported by anecdotes, not a formal evaluation. Sabien's discussion of the handbook version distinguishes his teaching from the version she developed and emphasizes prerequisites such as Focusing. The disagreement itself remains relevant to how confidently the technique should be presented.

Imagine someone who has repeatedly overcommitted accepting a clever argument that this new project is different. Their objections become quiet, but the proposed hours have not changed and no existing responsibility has been removed. Relief in the conversation supplies little evidence that the workload is workable.

An educational adaptation should leave room to record a concern that cannot yet be explained, decline a forced acknowledgment, or stop with disagreement intact. Those choices loosen the handbook's prescribed procedure. They are deliberate adaptations, not a claim to reproduce the original exercise exactly.

## Some conflicts involve real costs

Someone wants to stay near close friends and also move abroad for a valued opportunity. They may understand both wishes perfectly. More dialogue cannot make the cities adjacent or recover every missed evening together.

Here the useful outcome could be a clearer loss, a limited trial, or a decision made with mixed feelings. The article does not adopt the stronger suggestion that every conflicting desire must reveal an incorrect model. Limited time, incompatible opportunities, and different values can produce genuine tradeoffs.

This pattern fits recurring ambivalence when there is enough room to consider the perspectives with curiosity. It can become unhelpful when every small decision requires an inner negotiation, or when an urgent practical need keeps being converted into another reflection. Distressing or overwhelming material calls for appropriate support and pacing; completing the dialogue is never an obligation.
