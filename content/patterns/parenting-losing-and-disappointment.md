## 1. A recognizable situation

Your child has been enjoying a board game. Then their sibling wins. They accuse the sibling of cheating, sweep the pieces onto the floor, and announce that they will never play again.

You explain that everyone loses sometimes. They already know that. You remind them that winning is not everything. They still wanted to win.

A similar difficulty might appear when a planned outing is cancelled, another child gets the coveted part in a play, or a carefully practised performance goes badly. The outward response may be anger, tears, withdrawal, or refusing to participate again.

**The aim is to help the child experience disappointment without it determining their behavior, relationships, or sense of worth.** That leaves room for genuinely caring about the outcome. A child can recover well and still wish things had gone differently.

## 2. What might be happening

Losing creates a gap between what the child wanted and what happened. What makes that gap difficult varies.

| Possible difficulty | What might help distinguish it |
|---|---|
| **The immediate frustration is hard to manage** | The child reacts strongly but recovers with support and remains interested in playing afterward. |
| **The result feels like a judgment of their worth** | They say “I’m useless” or “Everyone is better than me,” rather than describing one disappointing event. |
| **The social situation feels humiliating** | Reactions are much stronger around particular siblings, teammates, spectators, or people who tease. |
| **The rules or expectations were unclear** | They dispute how the winner was decided, expected effort to guarantee success, or misunderstood an adult’s promise. |
| **The challenge is poorly matched** | They repeatedly compete against someone much more skilled, or rarely experience meaningful progress. |
| **They have limited capacity left that day** | Reactions intensify after exhausting school days, long competitions, missed meals, or insufficient rest. |

These are possibilities to explore, not conclusions to draw from one episode.

Listen for the meaning the child gives the event. “I wanted another turn,” “They always laugh at me,” and “You’ll think I’m stupid” call for different follow-up conversations.

Fear of failure can also lead a child to avoid trying or pretend not to care. The American Academy of Pediatrics discusses this pattern in its guidance on perfectionism, particularly among adolescents. It does not mean that every child who hates losing is a perfectionist. [AAP guidance on perfectionism](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/young-adult/Pages/What-Fuels-Perfectionism.aspx)

## 3. How development changes the picture

Knowing a rule about losing and being able to manage the associated feeling are different abilities.

**In early childhood**, keep the immediate task small: stop throwing pieces, accept some help, or take a break. A preschooler may be able to repeat “Everyone gets a turn to win” while needing considerable assistance when it happens. Abstract explanations about character or future resilience offer little practical direction in that moment.

**During the primary-school years**, children can increasingly examine what a result means, distinguish luck from skill, and help plan how to respond. They still need concrete examples. “One game tells us who won this round” is easier to work with than “Don’t let failure define you.”

**With older children and adolescents**, explore the actual stakes: selection, belonging, reputation, ambitions, or disappointing someone important. Give them more say over whether they want company, discussion, feedback, or time alone. Being older does not make a meaningful rejection trivial.

Research on *cognitive reappraisal*—changing how an event is interpreted—supports developmental adaptation. A systematic review found that some preschoolers can use simple reframing with adult help, while more independent use develops later. Findings vary substantially with the task and measurement method; laboratory success does not establish that a child can use the skill unaided after a public defeat. [Willner and colleagues, 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9258621/)

These are starting expectations. Language, developmental differences, experience, and current stress also matter.

## 4. What needs balancing

The child needs permission to care, feel disappointed, and retain ownership of their interests. Other people need safety, fair play, and room to enjoy their own successes. The parent needs a response that works within ordinary family life.

It helps to separate three questions:

- **What can the child feel?** Anger, sadness, envy, and disappointment can be acknowledged.

- **What can they do?** Hitting, damaging things, or humiliating another player requires a boundary.

- **What happens next?** The result may stand while the child takes a break, seeks feedback, tries again later, or reconsiders the activity.

“Acceptance” should also leave room for justified disagreement. A child can learn to handle disappointment and still challenge cheating, exclusion, or an unfair decision.

Sometimes the adult has something to repair. If you promised an outing and cancelled it, acknowledging your part is relevant. Your child’s distress is not automatically an opportunity to teach them to expect less.

## 5. Possible responses

The following approaches combine research and professional guidance with practical synthesis. The examples are illustrations, not independently tested scripts.

### A. Acknowledge the specific loss and set the immediate behavioral limit

Start with what mattered: “You really wanted to win that round.” If necessary, add the boundary: “I won’t let you throw the pieces.” Protect people and belongings, then reduce the pressure to discuss.

You can offer a break without promising a different result: “We can sit here or step away for a minute. Your sister won this game.”

Avoid immediately requesting gratitude, perspective, or a lesson learned. Boston Children’s sports specialists recommend giving young athletes time to feel disappointed, allowing them to lead the conversation, and making parental acceptance clear. This is clinical guidance, not evidence that a particular phrase speeds recovery. [Boston Children’s guidance](https://answers.childrenshospital.org/athletes-bad-day/)

Broader emotion-focused parenting programs have stronger evidence than isolated wording. A meta-analysis of 15 randomized trials involving children aged 18 months to nearly seven found improvements in emotional competence and behavioral adjustment. Those results support the overall approach; they do not establish a specific treatment for difficulty losing. [England-Mason and colleagues, 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36706555/)

### B. Make practice manageable and agree on a small recovery plan

For children who struggle with games, start with activities they understand, rounds short enough to repeat, and opponents reasonably matched to their abilities. Before playing, agree on one response to disappointment: put the cards down, ask for a pause, or move away without disturbing someone else’s game.

A parent can model this briefly after their own loss: “I wanted that last card. I’m disappointed. I’m taking a moment.”

Low-stakes games and adult modeling are recommended by early-childhood specialist Shauna Tominey. These are practical recommendations rather than evidence for a prescribed amount of losing. [Oregon State University guidance](https://health.oregonstate.edu/news-and-stories/2020-11/teaching-children-lose-gracefully-so-they-can-lose-dignity-adults)

Cooperative games can offer another way to enjoy play, but claims about their broader benefits need qualification. In a study of 65 four- to six-year-olds who played cooperative or competitive board games over six weeks, children enjoyed cooperative games more, but the conditions did not differ on measured cooperative, prosocial, or antisocial behavior. The study did not establish that cooperative games teach children to tolerate later competitive losses. [Eriksson and colleagues, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/sjop.12708)

There is no evidence here for an ideal win–loss ratio. Openly adjusting rules or using a teaching round is a practical option; repeatedly engineering defeats to “toughen them up” is not a supported conclusion.

### C. Help the child interpret the result accurately

Once the child can engage, explore the conclusion they drew.

“I lost, so I’m terrible” combines an event with a sweeping judgment. You might respond: “You lost today. What does that tell us about this game—and what doesn’t it tell us?”

Distinguish factors the child can influence from those they cannot: preparation, strategy, luck, other people’s performance, selection criteria, or available places. Changing an interpretation can make it more accurate without pretending the outcome was desirable.

For a younger child, offer a simple observation. With an older child, ask what they think before supplying your explanation. If the disappointment concerns a genuine limitation or rejection, acknowledge it; improvement is not guaranteed.

Feedback should be credible and specific. “You changed your approach after the first round” gives information. “You’re amazing at everything” may not fit the child’s experience.

The familiar advice to “praise effort” also deserves restraint. A 2024 experiment with 150 children averaging about eight years found no difference between process praise and person praise in its preregistered tests of persistence and self-evaluation after failure. Exploratory analyses suggested an advantage of process praise over a control condition. This qualifies sweeping claims about praise, while leaving room for useful feedback. It was a puzzle experiment, not a long-term study of parental responses to losing. [Bennett-Pierre and colleagues, 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39111151/)

### D. Examine the environment that makes losing feel so consequential

Consider what adults and peers communicate before and after the result.

Does the family conversation revolve around rankings? Does a sibling gloat? Does the coach publicly shame mistakes? Does the game repeatedly display losses, rankings, or prompts to immediately try again? Does the child have meaningful choices about participation?

Possible changes include stopping ridicule, discussing a coach’s behavior, reducing an exhausting schedule, or letting the child choose a more suitable activity. These are responses to the setting, not evidence that the child should never face competition.

A systematic review of 29 youth-sport studies linked supportive parental involvement and an emphasis on learning with more favorable motivational outcomes. Much of the evidence was observational, often involving older children and adolescents, so it cannot establish that parental pressure caused a particular child’s reaction. It does justify investigating the wider climate. [Gao and colleagues, 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10800670/)

## 6. Examples in practice

**A preschooler loses and still cries**

A four-year-old loses a short matching game and pushes the cards away. Their parent moves the cards out of reach and says, “You wanted to win. You can be upset; the cards stay on the table.”

The child declines a hug and continues crying. The parent stays available without repeating the explanation. Later, they practice asking for a pause.

The response has not produced cheerful sportsmanship. It has preserved the result, prevented damage, and identified a small skill to rehearse. Another game need not begin immediately.

**A school-age child hears failure as a verdict**

A nine-year-old misses a decisive shot and says, “I’m the worst player.” Their parent initially replies, “No, you were brilliant!” The child becomes angrier: “You didn’t even watch.”

The parent changes course: “You’re right that the shot didn’t go how you wanted. I was trying to cheer you up. Do you want me to listen, or talk about it later?”

Later they distinguish the missed shot from the child’s whole contribution and, if wanted, identify one skill to practice. Honest acknowledgment makes room for learning without insisting the performance was good.

**A cancelled plan needs acknowledgment and repair**

A parent cancels a promised weekend activity because work has changed. Their child refuses the substitute activity and says the parent never keeps promises.

The parent acknowledges the broken expectation, explains the constraint without making the child responsible for solving it, and offers realistic alternatives when the child is ready. They also reconsider how firmly they promise plans they cannot guarantee.

The child may remain disappointed. The response addresses both coping and the relationship in which the disappointment occurred.

## 7. Common mistakes and limitations

**“It’s only a game.”** The stakes may be small to an adult and substantial to the child. Find out what losing meant before deciding that their interpretation is unreasonable.

**Turning every loss into immediate coaching.** A detailed performance review can make parental attention feel like another evaluation. Ask whether feedback is wanted and choose the timing.

**Promising compensation or future victory.** “You’ll win next time” may be untrue. Support needs to remain credible when outcomes are uncertain.

**Treating tears as poor sportsmanship.** Address harmful conduct specifically. A child can cry while respecting other players.

**Making effort another perfection standard.** “As long as you always give 100%” can become an exhausting requirement. Discuss reasonable preparation, rest, and whether the activity remains worthwhile.

**Requiring continued participation to prove resilience.** Distinguish a temporary urge to quit from a considered wish to change activities. There may be commitments to discuss, but persistence is not the only legitimate outcome.

These cautions are a synthesis of the evidence and guidance, not separately proven rules.

## 8. What to observe over time

Look for changes in how the child participates and recovers:

- Can they experience a setback without harming people or disrupting others?

- Can they describe what hurt or request support?

- Do they return to activities they value, with or without a break?

- Can they distinguish one result from a judgment of their whole self?

- Does enjoyment remain possible even when success is uncertain?

If recovery is not becoming easier, compare the situations. Difficulty mainly with one opponent or audience is a reason to revisit the social setting in D. A game that repeatedly exceeds the child’s skills calls for the adjustments in B. If losses keep becoming statements such as “I’m useless,” return to the meaning of the result in C rather than simply adding more practice.

Outward composure is an incomplete measure. In a study of 150 preschoolers in China, Japan, and the United States, children displayed more positive expressions around adults than when alone after receiving an unwanted gift. That demonstrates how social context affects expression; a smile does not by itself establish emotional recovery. [Ip and colleagues](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0022096520304264)

Seek additional support when fear of failure substantially restricts school, friendships, or activities; when self-criticism becomes pervasive; or when outbursts involve dangerous aggression or self-injury. Assessment should explore the broader difficulty rather than treating dislike of losing as a diagnosis. [AACAP guidance on seeking help](https://aacap.org/AACAP/Families_and_Youth/Facts_for_Families/FFF-Guide/When-To-Seek-Help-For-Your-Child-024.aspx)

**Useful response concepts**

- **Emotion coaching:** helps with the immediate emotional experience.

- **Help without taking over:** supports learning while preserving the child’s opportunity to act.

- **Collaborative problem-solving:** can clarify recurring disputes about rules or participation.

- **Repair after conflict:** applies when teasing, adult reactions, or broken promises have added to the disappointment.

The evidence is strongest for broader emotional-development approaches and for some specific mechanisms studied under controlled conditions. Direct evidence about everyday parental responses to losing is more limited. Low-stakes practice, honest feedback, and thoughtful game selection are reasonable applications of the available knowledge; they should be evaluated by whether the child gains room to feel, participate, choose, and recover over time.
