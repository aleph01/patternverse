You need a desk for a small room. It must fit the available wall, leave enough space for your legs, and cost no more than your budget. After checking the measurements and condition, you find one that meets those requirements. Then another recommendation appears. Perhaps a different finish would look better. Perhaps next week's listings will offer a bargain. The practical problem is nearly solved, but the search continues.

**Satisficing** gives that search a stopping rule. Establish what would make an option acceptable, examine candidates, and accept one when it meets the standard. A suitable desk can be chosen while other desks remain unexplored.

Herbert Simon developed the idea within an account of decision making that takes limited information and limited capacity to evaluate alternatives seriously. His model includes searching for satisfactory alternatives and adapting aspiration levels through experience. The practical version here makes the acceptance standard explicit so that it can guide an actual choice.

## Give “enough” a useful meaning

A threshold needs to describe what the choice must accomplish. For the desk, dimensions, usable condition, and a spending limit are concrete enough to check. “Something I feel completely certain about” would leave the search open indefinitely. “The perfect desk” supplies no usable stopping point either.

An acceptable standard can be demanding. A venue for a reading group may need step-free access, permission to meet for two hours, enough seating, and a cost the group can cover. A beautiful room that fails the access requirement has not met that standard. The word satisfactory does not decide how high the threshold should be; the purpose and circumstances do.

Preferences can remain preferences. A view, a particular color, or an unusually attractive price can be welcome without becoming another requirement every candidate must satisfy. Writing the distinction down can reveal how an ordinary decision has accumulated conditions it never needed.

This is especially useful when new possibilities repeatedly move the finish line. An option meets yesterday's requirements, but seeing a premium feature elsewhere creates today's additional requirement. Sometimes that feature reveals a genuine need. Sometimes it merely keeps comparison going. An explicit standard makes the change available for examination.

## Include the work of choosing

Searching consumes time and attention. It can also delay the benefit of having made a choice. In the desk example, another evening of browsing is an evening without the work surface the person needs. A possible improvement has to be considered alongside that continuing cost.

Satisficing makes room for a decision to be sufficient for its purpose without establishing that it is the best available anywhere. That is a different task from ranking every candidate. If several known options are easy to compare, a brief comparison may be worthwhile. The stopping rule becomes valuable when each answer produces more candidates and more questions.

Search order matters. Someone who begins with unsuitable listings can take a long time to find an acceptable desk. Basic filters, relevant sources, and enough information to verify the requirements still matter. A clear threshold cannot compensate for looking in the wrong place or accepting an unsupported description.

## A worked example: choosing a meeting place

A reading group has spent three weeks discussing cafés. Members keep discovering somewhere with better food, a quieter atmosphere, or a more convenient location for one person. Meanwhile, no meeting happens.

They agree on a workable standard: the venue must be accessible to the members attending, permit conversation, accommodate the group on the chosen date, and fit the agreed budget. One member checks the venue's access and booking arrangements directly. The first available place that satisfies those conditions is booked for the next meeting.

They may later hear about a nicer café. That possibility was included in the decision to stop searching. It need not invalidate a venue that serves the agreed purpose. If the café turns out to play loud music during their booked hours, however, they have learned something relevant to a requirement. The next meeting's choice can use that information.

The example separates accepting an adequate option from ignoring a material problem. The group releases speculative comparison while remaining attentive to whether the arrangement actually works.

## Use a completion threshold too

The same idea can be adapted to deciding when a task is finished. Someone preparing a short neighborhood notice can specify that it must give the correct date, place, purpose, and contact information, and be readable on a phone. Once those checks pass, another hour spent comparing typefaces may contribute little to that particular notice.

This extension concerns the stopping point for revision. It does not establish that all writing deserves the same standard. A publication, a public sign, and a message to a small group can require different kinds of checking. The threshold should fit the actual use.

## Revise the standard for a reason

No acceptable option may appear. In that case, the standard, search method, or available options need examination. Perhaps the budget excludes what is required, perhaps a preference was mistaken for a necessity, or perhaps a different arrangement would serve the purpose. Simon's account allows aspiration levels to change; a threshold need not be frozen forever.

A useful practical discipline is to identify what new information justifies a revision. Changing a requirement because you learned that it is unnecessary differs from quietly dropping it because an attractive candidate fails it. A planned pause to review an unsuccessful search can help make that distinction visible.

There are also situations where further comparison is easy and valuable. If a small set of options is already known and their consequences are straightforward to compare, selecting the best among them may take little additional work. Research on satisficing distinguishes decision environments rather than establishing one universally superior rule. This pattern offers a way to manage a particular kind of search, not a promise of better outcomes in every choice.

- Herbert A. Simon, [A Behavioral Model of Rational Choice](https://doi.org/10.2307/1884852), *The Quarterly Journal of Economics* 69(1), 1955, pp. 99–118. [Read the original paper](https://cooperative-individualism.org/simon-herbert_a-behavioral-model-of-rational-choice-1955-feb.pdf). Develops satisfactory alternatives, limited evaluation, and aspiration levels that can change during search.
- Florian M. Artinger, Gerd Gigerenzer, and Perke Jacobs, [Satisficing: Integrating Two Traditions](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/jel.20201396), *Journal of Economic Literature* 60(2), 2022, pp. 598–635. Reviews the conditions under which different forms of satisficing can be effective.

The desk, reading-group venue, and neighborhood notice are original illustrations. Defining requirements before browsing and identifying reasons for later review are practical adaptations. The article makes no claim that satisficing guarantees satisfaction, eliminates regret, or identifies an objectively best choice.
