The perfect building has the wrong interior. Its entrance looks magnificent, but the rooms are too small to film. Another location has exactly the staircase the story needs, while a studio offers the best place to build the room at the top.

A filmmaker can use all three.

**Creative geography joins separately filmed places into a single apparent space.** An exterior, corridor and bedroom can occupy different buildings—or different cities—while the finished scene makes them feel connected.

This gives filmmakers considerable freedom. They can choose locations for their appearance, practical access and dramatic purpose, then establish the relationships between them through framing, movement, editing and sound.

The place the audience experiences is assembled during the telling of the story.

Imagine a sequence in which a woman enters a library, crosses a hallway and reaches a reading room.

The street entrance could be filmed at Location A. The hallway could belong to Location B. The reading room could be a set at Location C.

What makes them feel connected?

The woman’s appearance remains consistent. Her movement continues. The entrance door provides a recognisable link between exterior and interior. The next opening gives her a destination.

We follow her action and interpret the changes of view as different parts of the same journey.

A particularly effective connection is a **match on action**: the character begins opening a door in one shot and completes the movement in another. The action carries our attention across the cut while the location changes. [Film-editing explanation: University of Georgia](https://open.online.uga.edu/latinxmedia/chapter/film/).

In Joe Wright’s *Pride & Prejudice*, Darcy’s estate appears to be a single magnificent home. Its grandeur remains consistent as Elizabeth encounters different parts of it.

The production assembled that home from more than one property:

| Part of Pemberley | Filming location |
|---|---|
| Exterior | Chatsworth House |
| Painted Hall staircase and Sculpture Gallery | Chatsworth House |
| Drawing room where Elizabeth meets Georgiana | Wilton House’s Double Cube Room |

The rooms do not need to share a real address. They need to belong convincingly to Darcy’s world.

Architecture, decoration and the continuity of the characters’ visit establish that relationship. The audience experiences one estate, even though the production selected its components separately. [Official location account: Focus Features](https://www.focusfeatures.com/article/pride___prejudice__the_locations?film=pride_and_prejudice).

## The connection matters more than the complete floor plan
A film rarely shows every passage between its rooms. It provides selected relationships: this door leads inside; that staircase goes upstairs; the person approaching from the left is coming toward us.

Those relationships can be enough to make a place understandable.

Matching details help at the joins. A distinctive doorway, similar stonework or a continuing movement can connect otherwise separate spaces. Costume and props maintain the character’s continuity through them.

Sound can contribute another connection. Imagine footsteps continuing across a cut, or a clock heard in the entrance and then more clearly in the next room. The sound suggests a shared environment while the pictures supply different pieces of it.

The production needs to decide which connections the audience must understand. A suspense scene may require precise knowledge of the distance between a bedroom and an exit. A leisurely house tour can leave much more of the layout unspecified.

## The puzzle: where does Hogwarts’ staircase actually lead?
The staircase at Christ Church in Oxford appears in the *Harry Potter* films. Hogwarts’ Great Hall, however, was built as a set at Leavesden.

The college’s real hall inspired the studio design. That architectural relationship helps the filmed locations feel like parts of the same institution.

On screen, an actual Oxford staircase and a purpose-built dining hall belong to Hogwarts. Their connection is part of the school’s fictional geography.

This approach gives the production the character of an existing building alongside the control of a studio set. Different spaces contribute what they do best to one imagined place. [Christ Church’s location account](https://www.chch.ox.ac.uk/visit/things-to-see), [Warner Bros.’ Great Hall account and photographs](https://www.wbstudiotour.co.uk/the-experience/explore-the-tour/sets/the-great-hall/).

## The puzzle: does that street entrance really lead into those tunnels?
In *The Third Man*, Harry Lime disappears from Vienna’s streets into its underground world.

The kiosk placed in Am Hof square for his disappearance was a prop. Portions of the sewer environment were reconstructed at Shepperton Studios, alongside other studio-built elements used with the Vienna locations.

The film connects the street, entrance and underground passages into an apparently continuous environment. The entrance supplies the crucial promise: there is somewhere beneath this city for Lime to go.

Once that connection is established, subsequent shots can develop the underground space through selected passages, shadows and movements. [Location analysis and images: BFI](https://www.bfi.org.uk/features/location-location-location-vienna-third-man).

## The puzzle: could you actually drive that chase route?
The chase in *Bullitt* offers a larger-scale version of the same pattern. Separate stretches of San Francisco become an apparently continuous pursuit.

In his study of the sequence, André Lima de Alvarenga identifies several ways the editing connects its locations. Views of the drivers provide linking shots with relatively little geographical information. Elsewhere, cars disappear around a corner or over a hill, then continue their movement in the next shot.

The continuity of the pursuit makes the connections persuasive even where the actual streets do not form the route implied by the edit.

The result is a city organised around the chase’s momentum. [Published analysis: *Creative Geography of Film*](https://revistas.ufrj.br/index.php/EspacoAberto/article/view/2055).

## What keeps the geography believable?
The strongest joins preserve the information the viewer is using to follow the scene.

Movement should make sense across the transition. Characters should arrive with compatible speed, posture and intention. If a person looks toward an entrance, the following shot should make the relationship to that entrance understandable.

Light and weather also need to cooperate. A bright exterior can connect naturally to a darker interior, but the change should feel motivated by entering the building. Unexplained shifts in sunlight, wetness or time of day can expose the separate filming.

Framing controls what must match. A tight shot of a hand turning a handle asks less of two locations than a wide view through the entire doorway.

These decisions begin during planning. Editing has much more freedom when the footage contains usable connections.

## Where the trick breaks
A real-map discrepancy may pass unnoticed, while a small contradiction in the scene’s own geography causes confusion.

If a character has just barricaded the only exit, an unexplained second door can weaken the suspense. If a chase repeatedly reverses apparent direction without giving us a way to understand the turns, we may lose the relationship between pursuer and target.

Familiar landmarks can also expose distant locations being presented as neighbours. Whether that matters depends on the scene: local viewers may notice an impossible route while still following the action comfortably.

The essential requirement is that the film’s own spatial relationships remain useful and convincing.

## The neighbouring patterns
**Location doubling** supplies a place that stands in for another. **Creative geography** connects places into an environment. **Continuity editing** makes those connections readable, while **set extensions** can complete the portions shown within an individual image.

Together, these techniques let a story inhabit a place that exists fully only on screen.
