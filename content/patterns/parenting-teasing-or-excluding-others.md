## 1. A recognizable situation

A teacher tells you that your child has been encouraging classmates to leave another child out. There have also been jokes about the child’s accent.

At home, your child is affectionate and considerate. When you ask what happened, they say, “Everyone was doing it. It was just a joke.”

You may feel defensive, embarrassed, or angry. You want your child to understand the harm, but a harsh confrontation could make them more concerned with denying it than changing it.

**The task is to stop the behavior, understand what sustains it, and help your child act differently.** That requires accountability and continued support. The child who was targeted should receive protection throughout that process.

## 2. What might be happening

Teasing and exclusion can serve different purposes. Several explanations may fit, and none should be assumed without investigation.

**Misjudged joking.** Your child may believe the interaction is enjoyable for everyone. Ask what the other child did, whether they tried to stop it, and what happened afterward. Laughter alone does not establish consent.

**An awkward attempt to set a boundary.** “I want to play alone” and “Nobody is allowed to play with you” have different meanings. Find out whether your child wanted space, was protecting an activity, or was organizing others against someone.

**Seeking approval or status.** A joke may produce laughter; excluding someone may demonstrate influence. Notice whether the behavior happens mainly with an audience or particular friends.

**Fear of becoming the target.** A child may join in because refusing feels socially dangerous. Ask privately: “What do you think would happen if you didn’t join?” Their fear needs attention alongside responsibility for their actions.

**Retaliation or an unresolved grievance.** Your child may feel wronged and use humiliation or exclusion to punish someone. Investigate the original problem without accepting retaliation as its solution.

**Learned prejudice or group rules.** Comments about disability, race, gender, appearance, or poverty may reflect ideas circulating among peers, adults, or media. Ask where the idea came from and what the child believes it means.

Professional guidance recognizes peer status, belonging, emotional difficulties, family experiences, and school conditions as possible contributors. These are possibilities, not a diagnostic checklist or proof of the cause in your family. [StopBullying.gov: Why Some Youth Bully](https://www.stopbullying.gov/bullying/why-some-youth-bully)

Bullying involves unwanted aggression, unequal power, and behavior that is repeated or has the potential to recur. Relational bullying includes recruiting others to reject someone, spreading rumors, and public humiliation. Harmful incidents deserve intervention even before a repeated pattern is established. [StopBullying.gov’s definition](https://www.stopbullying.gov/bullying/what-is-bullying)

## 3. How development changes the picture

### Preschool: interrupt the action and teach a concrete alternative

Young children can use friendship and group membership to influence others: “You can’t come to my party unless you give me that.” They may be copying language, pursuing an immediate goal, or beginning to understand its social power.

Adults need to respond to the action rather than wait for a sophisticated explanation of intent. Use brief language and help enact an alternative: asking for space, choosing another activity, or requesting adult assistance.

A systematic review documents relational aggression in early childhood, including direct exclusion and threats to end relationships. Studies varied considerably in measurement and informants; there is no simple age threshold separating innocent behavior from meaningful harm. [Swit and Slater, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.avb.2021.101556)

### Primary school: examine the child’s role in the group

Children can increasingly discuss fairness, motives, and consequences. Understanding that exclusion hurts does not guarantee they will resist a group that rewards it.

Ask about specific roles: who starts the joke, who repeats it, who controls participation, and what happens to someone who objects. Help your child plan an alternative they can realistically carry out.

### Adolescence: address reputation, audience, and responsibility

Teasing can become more indirect, coordinated, or persistent through group chats and social media. Creating a humiliating post, forwarding it, and pressuring others to react are different actions, each requiring attention.

Involve the teenager in planning change, while maintaining clear limits on targeting others. Discuss how private messages can acquire wider audiences and how an online action may continue circulating after deletion.

Across ages, communication and learning differences may affect understanding or the support needed. They do not remove another child’s need for protection. Avoid assuming a diagnosis—or a lack of empathy—from the behavior alone.

## 4. What needs balancing

**Friendship choice and fair treatment.** Children do not have to become close friends with everyone, accept unwanted touch, or agree to every invitation. Adults can still stop discriminatory exclusion, humiliation, and attempts to control other children’s friendships.

**Belonging and responsibility.** Wanting approval is understandable. Another child should not have to absorb harm so your child can keep their position in a group.

**Accountability and continued connection.** Your child needs to know that you will address what they did and remain available to help them change.

**Repair and the targeted child’s autonomy.** An apology should not purchase forgiveness, access, or a renewed friendship.

**Individual change and environmental change.** A child may need new skills. The classroom, team, or online group may also need different supervision and expectations.

These distinctions matter in practice. A child declining a private playdate is different from persuading classmates to exclude someone from shared school activities.

## 5. Possible responses

### A. Stop the behavior and establish the facts

**Fits:** you witness an incident or receive a credible report.

Interrupt clearly:

> “Stop commenting on his voice. That is hurting him.”

If several children are involved, stop the exchange and organize supervision. Attend to the targeted child’s needs without making them explain their distress in front of the group.

Speak with your child privately afterward. Describe the reported actions and invite their account:

> “The teacher says you told others not to sit with her. Tell me what happened.”

Separate established facts, disputed details, and explanations. “I was joking” may explain intention; it does not settle whether the behavior was acceptable.

State the immediate limit concretely: no repeating the comment, recruiting others, threatening someone for reporting, or continuing through another account.

Professional guidance recommends clear limits, nonphysical discipline, and close follow-up when children hurt, shame, or exclude peers. It does not establish that one particular reprimand changes the pattern. [American Academy of Pediatrics guidance](https://www.healthychildren.org/english/safety-prevention/at-play/pages/bullying-its-not-ok.aspx)

### B. Teach an alternative that meets the legitimate need

**Fits:** your child needs space, influence, companionship, or a way to handle pressure.

Match the alternative to the situation.

If they want space:

> “I want to finish this on my own.”

If they dislike someone’s behavior:

> “Please stop taking my things. I’m asking the teacher for help.”

If friends invite them to mock someone, possible responses include declining to forward the material, changing the subject, leaving the exchange, or approaching an adult privately. A frightened child may need help choosing the least risky option.

Practice briefly. Then ask what would make the alternative difficult in the real setting. If refusing means becoming a target, adults need to address that pressure.

There is some causal evidence for structured teaching about exclusion. A randomized trial involving 983 children aged eight to eleven evaluated a classroom program combining videos and teacher-led discussion. Participants showed more inclusive judgments and reported more play across group backgrounds. These outcomes do not establish lasting reductions in actual targeting, and the complete program cannot be reduced to one parental conversation. [Killen and colleagues, 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9179087/)

### C. Arrange relevant repair and proportionate limits

**Fits:** your child has contributed to identifiable harm.

Help them name their own action:

> “I repeated something about you that wasn’t true. I’m going to correct it with the people I told.”

Repair might involve correcting misinformation, returning something, removing harmful content, or changing an exclusionary arrangement. Coordinate with school staff so repair does not expose the targeted child to more unwanted attention.

The child who was hurt can decline contact. Avoid arranging a surprise apology meeting or requiring them to reassure your child.

If a particular activity or tool was used to target someone, a temporary, relevant restriction may be reasonable—for example, pausing participation in that group chat while adults establish a safer arrangement. Explain what happens next, what behavior is required, and when the restriction will be reviewed.

These are practical options, rather than independently proven consequences. Professional guidance supports responses that teach responsibility and address damage, while cautioning against forced mediation when bullying involves unequal power. [StopBullying.gov: Supporting Children Involved](https://www.stopbullying.gov/prevention/support-the-children-involved)

An apology matters most as part of a changed pattern. Continued access to the targeted child is not a necessary opportunity to demonstrate improvement.

### D. Change the group conditions and coordinate follow-up

**Fits:** repeated targeting, audience-driven behavior, discriminatory exclusion, or limited improvement after individual conversations.

Work with the responsible adults to identify where the behavior happens, who participates, and what sustains it. Ask how they will protect the targeted child, monitor retaliation, and support different behavior from your child.

Possible changes include adult-organized team allocation, supervision during particular transitions, clearer access to shared activities, and group expectations about forwarding humiliating content. Consider school pressures and product design: public rankings, visible reactions, and easily forwarded posts may create opportunities for an audience to reward harm.

Research supports attending to peer norms. In a randomized study across 56 US middle schools, a student-led intervention reduced disciplinary reports of conflict over one school year. The outcome included conflict broadly, not only bullying, and does not prove that assigning an individual child a leadership role will change them. [Paluck, Shepherd, and Aronow, 2016](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1514483113)

A systematic review found the strongest bullying-prevention evidence for interventions working across school, classroom, and individual levels, particularly in primary and middle school settings. Evidence concerned complete programs; individual components were not established as interchangeable remedies. [George and colleagues, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ps.20230541)

The school plan should complement home support. A parent cannot supervise peer interactions throughout the school day.

## 6. Examples in practice

**A four-year-old announces, “Boys can’t play here.”**

You clarify that the nursery building area is available to everyone. You help organize materials and participation rather than requiring instant friendship.

Later, you explore whether your child was copying a phrase, protecting an unfinished construction, or applying a rule about gender. The alternative depends on the explanation: protecting work needs an arrangement; excluding a category of children needs correction and teaching.

**A nine-year-old joins jokes to avoid being excluded.**

Your child admits laughing at a classmate’s reading because their friends would turn on them otherwise.

You address the participation and help arrange repair. You also inform the teacher about the group pressure.

The first plan—saying “That’s not funny”—fails when peers mock your child. You do not conclude that they simply need more courage. The school revises its supervision and response to intimidation while helping them use a more manageable alternative. Your child may remain anxious despite agreeing that the behavior must stop.

**A fourteen-year-old creates a humiliating meme.**

Your teenager insists it was shared only in a private group.

You address their role in creating and distributing it, help stop further circulation where possible, and coordinate an appropriate correction. Deletion cannot guarantee that copies disappear.

The targeted child does not want an apology message. Your teenager feels frustrated that they cannot “make it right.” You explain that repair includes respecting this refusal and changing future conduct. Relief from their guilt is not the other child’s responsibility.

These examples illustrate applications, not separately tested interventions.

## 7. Common mistakes and limitations

**“My child would never do that.”** Behavior at home may differ from behavior with an audience. Investigate without treating a report as either automatically false or complete.

**Making remorse the immediate test.** A frightened or defensive child may deny, laugh, or minimize. Clear limits need not wait for a convincing display of regret.

**Relying only on empathy.** A child may understand that someone is hurt while valuing peer approval more strongly in that moment. Address the social reward and provide workable alternatives.

**Public humiliation as a lesson.** Shaming your child or withdrawing affection introduces another coercive relationship pattern. A recent meta-analysis linked parental psychological control with relational aggression across studies spanning preschool through college. This is correlational evidence, not proof that parents caused a particular incident or that one alternative response prevents it. [Li and colleagues, 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41379706/)

**“You must play with everyone.”** This can override reasonable personal boundaries. Teach the distinction between declining contact and organizing someone’s rejection.

**Treating the child’s victimization as an excuse—or overlooking it.** A child can harm peers and be harmed elsewhere. Both situations require attention.

**Assuming a kindness activity proves change.** Improved judgments, apologies, and supervised behavior are useful but incomplete outcomes. Research is less conclusive about whether particular home techniques produce lasting changes in unsupervised teasing and exclusion.

## 8. What to observe over time

Look for behavior across settings:

- Does the teasing stop when adults are absent?

- Does targeting move to subtler actions, another child, or another platform?

- Can your child decline play without humiliating someone or controlling others?

- Do they stop participating when a group begins targeting a peer?

- Can they acknowledge an incident without an escalating cycle of denial and shame?

- Does the targeted child have safer access to school and activities?

Use information from responsible adults and your child. Avoid making the targeted child or their family responsible for repeatedly reporting back to you.

Notice your child’s wellbeing too. Are they afraid of their friends, preoccupied with status, or experiencing mistreatment themselves? Improvement should include safer relationships and better choices, rather than merely avoiding detection.

Seek coordinated professional support when behavior persists despite a clear plan, involves serious threats or discriminatory harassment, or occurs alongside substantial emotional or behavioral difficulties. Assessment should consider the child, peer group, and setting. Serious harm requires prompt protective action while that assessment proceeds.
