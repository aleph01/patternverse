Two people want to feel closer. They decide that every Friday evening should be special. Soon each dinner carries a second conversation beneath the first: Is this meaningful enough? Are we enjoying ourselves? Has the evening improved our relationship?

They try spending some Fridays making a meal neither has cooked before. There are ingredients to find, mistakes to recover from, and small discoveries to share. The evening has something to be about. Closeness may develop through their attention to each other and the task, without every moment having to demonstrate it.

This is an illustration of **obliquity**: some complex goals are approached more effectively through activities and relationships that matter in their own right. John Kay develops the idea for situations where purposes are difficult to specify, knowledge is incomplete, and the results of an action depend on other people's responses. It offers a way to proceed while learning, without assuming that a broad aim can be converted into a complete plan.

## Give your attention somewhere concrete

An aspiration such as a fulfilling life can provide direction while leaving today's choices unclear. It may involve belonging, independence, useful work, curiosity, and rest. Those purposes can pull in different directions, and their importance may change through experience.

Obliquity gives attention to something concrete within that uncertain picture. Someone who wants a richer social life might join a choir because they enjoy singing and want to make music with others. Rehearsals give people a reason to return, listen, cooperate, and gradually become familiar. Friendship is a possible development of that participation, with its own timing and dependence on others.

The distinction matters. Attending while silently ranking everyone as a potential friend can turn the rehearsal into another evaluation exercise. Engaging with the music and the people gives the activity value even before any close friendship forms. Having a hope for connection is compatible with this; every encounter need not carry responsibility for fulfilling it.

## Why the indirect route can help

Kay's argument brings several difficulties together. First, a high-level goal may contain more than we can define in advance. A person can discover what satisfying work means by doing different kinds of work. Planning alone cannot reveal every preference that experience will bring into focus.

Second, other people participate in producing the outcome. A relationship develops through their interests and choices as well as yours. A favor offered primarily to secure approval can have a different character from help offered because a person matters to you. Treating another person's response as a result to manufacture can undermine the relationship you hope to build.

Third, a simple target can leave out important parts of a larger purpose. In an original photography example, counting likes gives a clear measure of response. If that number becomes the sole definition of progress, the photographer may repeatedly choose familiar subjects and abandon experiments that receive little immediate attention. The count answers a narrower question than whether the work is becoming more perceptive, accomplished, or personally meaningful.

These are reasons to consider an oblique approach, not proof that indirect pursuit always succeeds. A choir can be unwelcoming. A shared activity can be frustrating. An absorbing project can fail to provide the livelihood someone needs. Attention to actual consequences remains necessary.

## Learn what you want through involvement

Suppose someone wants meaningful work and believes that means becoming a manager. They begin helping a small volunteer team deliver an event. They enjoy teaching new volunteers, dislike scheduling, and find that solving practical problems together matters more than having authority.

Those observations change the question. Mentoring, a specialist role with collaborative responsibilities, or occasional project leadership might now deserve consideration alongside management. The activity has revealed something about the aspiration itself. There was no fully specified destination waiting for the person to calculate the shortest route.

The experience also has limits. Volunteer work does not establish what a paid management role will be like. The next inquiry could involve talking with working managers or negotiating a bounded responsibility at work. Obliquity supports continued learning and revision, including the discovery that a previously attractive goal no longer fits.

## Keep a direction and allow revision

An indirect approach still needs a plausible connection to what matters. Singing with others creates opportunities for repeated participation and cooperation. Practicing photographic observation develops abilities involved in making photographs. An unrelated diversion has no special claim to be an effective route simply because its effects are uncertain.

It helps to distinguish what can be done deliberately from what can only develop through participation. A person can arrange a rehearsal, invite someone to dinner, practice a skill, and honor a promise. Mutual affection, belonging, and a sense of purpose involve more than executing those actions correctly.

Feedback can be broader than a single score. Over time, someone might notice whether they want to return, whether others participate willingly, whether their abilities are developing, and whether the activity fits alongside other responsibilities. Those observations can support continuing, changing the arrangement, or leaving. An appealing philosophy should not make an unsatisfying commitment impossible to question.

## When a direct approach earns its place

Kay distinguishes complex, changing situations from tasks with clear objectives and understandable means. Booking the room, paying an invoice, and asking whether someone is available are ordinary tasks that can be handled directly. A larger oblique pursuit can contain many such actions.

The couple in the opening example may also need to discuss an unequal division of work or an unresolved disagreement. Cooking together cannot be assumed to resolve either. Direct conversation and shared activity can each contribute, depending on the difficulty in front of them.

Obliquity is especially useful when constant evaluation seems to burden an activity, when a broad aspiration resists precise definition, or when experience could change the goal itself. It is less useful as a reason to avoid a necessary conversation, ignore material constraints, or continue indefinitely without signs that the chosen activity is worthwhile. Meaningful involvement creates possibilities; it does not guarantee the outcome you first imagined.
