### What a large judgment leaves out

A manager says the team is bad at communication. The judgment sounds clear until someone asks what happened. One handover lacked an owner, a decision was made in a private conversation, and a progress update arrived after another team had already planned its work. These events share a broad label, but they involve different failures. More meetings might improve one and make another worse. Until the label is unpacked, the proposed solution has little connection to the actual difficulty.

Chunk Down is Robert Dilts's name for moving from a larger element of a belief to smaller, more specific elements. In Sleight of Mouth, the move changes or clarifies the generalization by examining what it contains. The smaller elements might be behaviors within a skill, moments within an event, features within a category, or observations behind an interpretation. The purpose is to recover distinctions that were lost when several things were treated as one.

This differs from simply making a task smaller. Smaller tasks can be a useful result, but the pattern begins with representation. Communication, confidence, quality, and readiness are all convenient words whose scope can hide variation. A claim about one of them may feel absolute because the relevant parts have not yet been distinguished. Greater detail can reveal that the claim fits some components and fails to describe others.

### Locating the part that matters

Consider someone who believes they cannot give presentations. Looking at a recent presentation reveals a more uneven picture. The explanation of the main idea was clear, the examples held attention, and the person became flustered during unexpected questions. The broad belief treats every part of presenting as equally difficult. A more specific account identifies uncertainty during questions as the main problem. Rehearsing the entire talk again may be less useful than learning to pause, clarify a question, and acknowledge when an answer needs checking.

The distinction can also change an apparently binary decision. A product is described as not ready, but readiness may involve security, billing, onboarding, support, and visual polish. A review might show that billing and security require further work, while onboarding can be tested with a small invited group. The team no longer has to choose between calling everything ready and calling everything unready. It can connect different conditions to different kinds of release and make the remaining uncertainty explicit.

Chunking down is valuable because the parts frequently have different causes. A delayed handover may reflect unclear ownership; an incomplete document may reflect time pressure; a confusing message may reflect incompatible assumptions. A single explanation such as carelessness suppresses those differences. Once they are visible, the response can be narrower and more proportionate. The team might clarify responsibility in one place, simplify the document in another, and establish a shared definition in a third.

### Detail can become an escape

More specificity is not always more useful. A discussion can descend into small distinctions while avoiding the larger issue that made them important. If a workplace repeatedly excludes someone from decisions, examining the wording of each invitation may miss the recurring pattern. Chunk Down should improve the account of what is happening, rather than fragmenting it until no general conclusion seems possible. The larger claim may remain accurate after its components have been examined.

There is also a risk of creating an interrogation. Repeated demands for exact examples can burden someone who is already trying to describe an experience. A listener may need to acknowledge the overall account before exploring one manageable instance. The aim is shared understanding, and that requires attention to the conversation as well as the precision of its content. Specificity obtained through pressure can reduce the willingness to share the very information the listener needs.

The appropriate stopping point depends on the decision. A designer may need to know which screen confuses users, but not every eye movement they make. A team may need an owner for a handover, but not a theory of every interaction that led to the omission. Detail has earned its place when it changes the explanation, the confidence in a claim, or the available response. Additional detail that changes none of these can become delay.

### Returning to the larger picture

Chunk Down works especially well when a sweeping judgment makes a problem appear homogeneous or an intervention appear obvious. Redefine the Terms often becomes easier after the underlying observations are separated. Look for the Exception can discover components or occasions that the broad claim overlooks. Chunk Up provides the complementary movement: after understanding the particulars, it asks which broader category or purpose connects them.

A useful analysis usually travels in both directions. The manager returns from three communication failures to a more informed account of coordination. The presenter returns from a difficult question period to a more accurate account of their capabilities. The larger picture survives, but it contains the variation that was missing before. That variation is often what makes improvement possible, because a person can work with a specific difficulty without treating the whole activity as beyond reach.

## Pattern details

### Use when

- A sweeping claim makes a difficulty appear uniform.
- A proposed solution is poorly connected to specific failures.

### Goals

- Recover variation within a broad claim.
- Identify the component that needs a response.

### Mechanisms

- decomposition
- specificity

### Not ideal when

- Further detail distracts from a demonstrated larger pattern or delays a decision.

### Difficulty

easy
