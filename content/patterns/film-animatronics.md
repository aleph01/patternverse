*Build a physical character that can perform while the camera rolls.*

A dinosaur needs to lean toward a car. An alien must look at a child and seem to understand him. A pig has to hesitate before speaking.

A sculpture can supply the appearance, but these moments need a performance. The creature must move with intention, react at the right time, and share space with actors and scenery.

**Animatronics puts controllable mechanisms inside a physical character so its body or features can move during filming.** The mechanism supplies capabilities; operators turn those capabilities into behavior.

The attraction is immediate physical presence. Light falls on the creature’s actual surfaces. It can cast shadows, disturb water, touch scenery, and give performers something tangible to respond to.

## The opening puzzle: how can a creature this large move beside the actors?

![Stan Winston Studio’s crew stands beneath the exposed T. rex mechanism.](https://de9o6n2ujz7l.cloudfront.net/cache/a1/11/a111ba97a4e473a0a68e3f05b57cce89.jpg)

*Jurassic Park (1993) — Workshop photograph.* Stan Winston Studio’s crew stands beneath the exposed T. rex mechanism. The structure, hoses, and moving assemblies reveal the machinery beneath the animal’s eventual skin. [Image source](https://www.stanwinstonschool.com/blog/building-jurassic-park-t-rex-animatronic-dinosaur). Credit: Stan Winston Studio archives; published by Stan Winston School of Character Arts.


The exposed *Jurassic Park* T. rex looks less like an animal than an extraordinary piece of industrial equipment.

That is the useful revelation. Its apparent bones and muscles do not have to work like a living dinosaur’s. They need to produce the movements the scene requires while supporting a convincing outer body.

Stan Winston Studio’s mechanics describe building a hydraulic metal skeleton for the full-size creature. Its movement came from engineered structure and powered mechanisms, rather than a performer wearing the whole dinosaur. In the archival footage, watch the uncovered skeleton move: the mechanism must carry the eventual skin as well as position the head and body. [The builders’ account and archival footage](https://www.stanwinstonschool.com/blog/building-jurassic-park-t-rex-animatronic-dinosaur).

The audience sees the character. Outside the frame, supports, controls, and a substantial crew make that character possible.

## Three layers have to cooperate

| Layer | Its job |
|---|---|
| Structure | Support the character and establish which parts can move |
| Mechanisms | Turn control inputs into movement |
| Surface | Translate that movement into believable skin, fur, flesh, or other material |

Electric motors, hydraulic systems driven by pressurized liquid, and pneumatic systems driven by compressed air can supply movement. Cables and linkages can transmit it to a jaw, eyelid, ear, or section of neck.

The surface is an active part of the design. A mouth mechanism may move correctly while its skin folds unnaturally. A thick skin can conceal machinery but resist delicate movement. A thin covering may reveal the hard structure underneath.

The mechanical design and the creature’s appearance therefore have to develop together.

### Example: Jurassic Park—make the machine move like flesh


![The completed T. rex on set, with support machinery visible beneath its body.](https://d3dkwn7op408kc.cloudfront.net/ckeditor_assets/2024/06/19/skinning-jurassic-park-t-rex-v07.jpg)

*Jurassic Park (1993) — Behind-the-scenes photograph.* The completed T. rex on set, with the support machinery visible beneath its body. Camera placement can present the animal while excluding the equipment that carries and moves it. [Image source](https://www.stanwinstonschool.com/blog/jurassic-park-t-rex-skinning-animatronic-dinosaur). Credit: Stan Winston Studio archives; published by Stan Winston School of Character Arts.


For *Jurassic Park* (1993), the finished T. rex combined its mechanical structure with a foam-latex skin. The fabrication team had to accommodate shrinkage, join sections, and make areas such as the mouth corners fold convincingly during movement.

The task extended beyond hiding metal. The covering had to preserve the creature’s shape while allowing the underlying mechanism to perform. [Stan Winston Studio’s skinning and movement tests](https://www.stanwinstonschool.com/blog/jurassic-park-t-rex-skinning-animatronic-dinosaur).

Notice the support equipment beneath the animal in the production photograph. A physical creature does not have to stand or travel independently to give a convincing performance within a selected camera view.

A carefully chosen portion of a creature can be enough: a head reaching through a window, a neck rising into frame, or a torso leaning toward someone. Framing determines how much of the machine must become an animal.

## Who is doing the acting?

An animatronic character need not be autonomous. Its movements can be performed live, programmed, or assembled from a combination of operator input and repeatable actions.

Several people may share one creature. One controls broad movement while another handles facial detail.

Coordination matters more than the number of moving parts. A glance should belong to the head turn. A blink should feel connected to attention. A small pause before movement can suggest thought.

A mechanism offers a range of motion. A performance gives that motion a reason.

### Example: E.T.—how do moving features become an emotional response?

In *E.T. the Extra-Terrestrial* (1982), much of the character’s appeal lies in apparently small events: looking, listening, blinking, or preparing to speak.

Carlo Rambaldi’s mechanical head used cables to control features including the eyes, lips, eyebrows, and forehead. These movements gave the filmmakers individual expressive elements to combine into a character’s response. [Description of an original production head, consigned by Rambaldi’s family](https://www.juliensauctions.com/en/items/107863/e-t-the-extra-terrestrial-mechanical-animatronic-head-with-dvd).

Different tasks could also require different versions of E.T. The Museum of the Moving Image identifies one surviving production figure as a static version used among the stuffed animals, distinguishing it from the animatronic versions built for movement and expression. [Museum account](https://movingimage.org/et-at-momi/).

A character can therefore be continuous for the audience while changing physical construction between shots.

### Example: Babe—why build more than one mechanical pig?


![Babe’s uncovered animatronic head exposes the eye, mouth and snout mechanisms.](https://i0.wp.com/beforesandafters.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/08/b_2.jpg?resize=1170%2C780&ssl=1)

*Babe (1995) — Production-mechanism photograph.* Babe’s animatronic head with its facial covering removed, exposing the structures that move the eyes, mouth, and snout. [Image source](https://beforesandafters.com/2020/08/07/neal-scanlan-explains-how-babe-the-pig-was-puppeteered/). Credit: Courtesy Bob Lindenmayer; published by befores & afters.


For *Babe* (1995), Neal Scanlan’s team built separate animatronic pigs for standing, sitting, and lying down. The animal’s body changes shape so substantially between those positions that one mechanism would have struggled to serve them all.

Operators used a control system with mitt-like hand interfaces and joysticks. Their movements drove the puppet’s body, neck, and facial features. A second puppeteer could add smaller signs of life, such as ear flicks or nose movements. [Scanlan’s firsthand explanation](https://beforesandafters.com/2020/08/07/neal-scanlan-explains-how-babe-the-pig-was-puppeteered/).

Those small actions helped the pig seem present even when it was not speaking.

The film also combined animatronics with trained animals and digital alterations, including mouth movement. Each method supplied actions suited to its capabilities. [American Humane’s production account](https://www.americanhumane.org/production/babe/).

The reusable trick is to design around the required performance. One character need not mean one all-purpose machine.

### Example: Jaws—what happens when the environment fights the mechanism?


![Steven Spielberg poses in the mouth of the mechanical shark, Bruce.](https://theasc.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/Jaws-Bruce-and-Steve.jpg)

*Jaws (1975) — Behind-the-scenes photograph.* Steven Spielberg poses in the mouth of the mechanical shark, Bruce. This production photograph shows the physical creature rather than a finished shark-attack shot. [Image source](https://theasc.com/article/on-location-with-jaws/). Credit: Production photograph published by American Cinematographer.


The mechanical shark in *Jaws* (1975) had to perform in seawater, where maintenance became part of the production’s daily reality.

A contemporary report describes corrosion affecting its metal components, damage requiring repairs, and continuing work on its outer skin. The shark’s appearance also depended on surface treatment that made water behave convincingly on it. [American Cinematographer’s report from the production](https://theasc.com/article/on-location-with-jaws/).

The environment is therefore part of an animatronic’s design problem. A mechanism that works in a workshop still has to work at the required speed, under the intended lighting, while wet, covered, or interacting with scenery.

The finished shot depends on the whole arrangement: creature, surface, surroundings, operators, camera, and timing.

## Where the illusion becomes difficult
The skin can bunch at a joint. An eye can remain fixed while the head moves. Repeated motion can start to look like a cycle instead of a response.

Scale introduces further constraints. Large mechanisms have mass and inertia; compact heads have limited room for moving parts. More controls can expand expression while making operation and maintenance harder.

A convincing build also has a useful viewing range. The angle that hides a support or preserves an appealing silhouette may restrict the camera’s freedom.

These limits help explain why productions combine techniques. Animatronics can supply a close physical encounter, while another puppet, a performer, or digital animation supplies movement beyond that construction’s range.

## When the pattern is useful
Animatronics is especially useful when a creature needs a physical presence during photography: close interaction, contact with scenery, expressive detail, or movement that can be achieved within a controlled area.

The neighboring techniques divide those jobs differently. **[Prosthetic makeup](/pattern/film-prosthetic-makeup)** changes a performer’s appearance and can incorporate mechanical features. **Stop motion** creates movement between exposures. **[Performance capture](/pattern/film-performance-capture)** records acting for subsequent animation. **[Digital doubles](/pattern/film-digital-doubles-and-face-replacement)** can continue a character beyond a physical puppet’s capabilities.

Animatronics brings construction and performance together in front of the camera: a fabricated body becomes a character through coordinated movement, surface behavior, and the attention it appears to give the world.
