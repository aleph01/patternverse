*Use small-scale fluid motion to create enormous, moving skies.*

A bank of clouds gathers around a mountain. Something luminous moves inside it. The formation expands, opens, and reveals an approaching spacecraft.

Waiting for real weather would give filmmakers little control over that sequence. A painted sky could provide the shape, but the clouds need to billow and change.

A cloud tank supplies that moving material.

**Cloud tanks create cloud-like formations by introducing opaque liquids into water and photographing their movement.** The resulting footage becomes an effects element: imagery that can be combined with landscapes, buildings, spacecraft, or other separately photographed material.

The tank supplies intricate physical motion. Framing, lighting and playback speed suggest its size; compositing combines that photographed element with the rest of the scene.

## The puzzle: how can an aquarium contain a storm?

![Richard Edlund and another ILM crew member work at the cloud tank used for the Ark effects.](https://www.lucasfilm.com/app/uploads/joe-johnston-indy-interview-bts-smoke.jpg)

*Raiders of the Lost Ark (1981) — Behind the scenes.* Richard Edlund and an ILM crew member work at the cloud tank for the Ark effects. Liquid formations supply organic atmospheric elements for the final composite. [Image source](https://www.lucasfilm.com/news/joe-johnston-indiana-jones-interview/). Credit: Lucasfilm.


Imagine watching a pale liquid spread through clear water. Its edges curl, fold, and break into smaller tendrils. Dense areas conceal what lies behind them; thin edges remain translucent.

Now remove the aquarium from the picture. Place those formations above a landscape, adjust their lighting and color, and give their movement an appropriate rhythm.

Without the tank’s edges or another familiar size reference, the formation can read as something enormous.

The useful resemblance comes from fluid behavior: expanding fronts, rolling curls, irregular boundaries, and layered translucency. Filmmakers select and present that behavior so we interpret it as atmosphere.

## The classic trick: give the cloud somewhere to spread

An ordinary drop of paint may descend through water and disperse. The classic cloud-tank arrangement adds a useful control: layers of different density.

Effects artist Scott Squires describes placing fresh water above denser salt water. Paint introduced into the upper layer billowed outward and tended to settle along the boundary, producing a flatter base and a spreading cloud bank.

That boundary helped turn a descending plume into a more recognizably cloud-like formation. The liquids still evolved and mixed, so the effect offered a limited photographic opportunity rather than a permanently stable miniature sky.

[Squires explains the original method, with drawings and a production photograph, in his own account.](https://effectscorner.blogspot.com/2009/02/cloud-tank-effect.html)

### Example: *Close Encounters of the Third Kind* (1977)—how can clouds behave as though something is directing them?

The clouds surrounding the visitors help make their arrival feel extraordinary. They gather around Devils Tower and near Jillian Guiler’s house, suggesting a presence before the spacecraft becomes fully visible.

Squires developed the tank process while working with Douglas Trumbull. Small aquarium experiments led to a much larger production tank.

The team also introduced light into the formations. Squires describes fiber-optic illumination associated with the liquid-delivery equipment, producing the impression of luminous objects within the clouds.

The photographed elements were then combined with the relevant scenes through rotoscoping and optical compositing.

This is a particularly effective use of the pattern: **the cloud becomes a moving curtain that conceals, announces, and reveals something.**

[VFX Voice’s retrospective includes Squires’s recollections, original cloud-tank Polaroids, and photographs of the individual elements.](https://vfxvoice.com/close-encounters-with-an-effects-milestone/)

### Example: *Raiders of the Lost Ark* (1981)—how can the sky become part of a supernatural event?

The opening of the Ark transforms the surrounding space into an expression of the power being released.

Cloud-tank work contributed to that effects sequence. Lucasfilm’s retrospective includes a production photograph of Richard Edlund and another ILM crew member working at the tank specifically for the opening of the Ark.

The sequence brings together several kinds of imagery and effects. The tank provides atmospheric material with an organic, changing structure that can participate in that larger construction.

The reusable idea is to make the environment respond dramatically. A cloud formation can supply gathering force, expansion, or an ominous transformation around the central action.

[Lucasfilm’s interview with Joe Johnston includes the tank photograph, concept paintings, and other production material from the climax.](https://www.lucasfilm.com/news/joe-johnston-indiana-jones-interview/)

### Example: *Star Trek II: The Wrath of Khan* (1982)—how can two spacecraft disappear inside a colorful nebula?

The Mutara Nebula provides a shifting environment for the ships’ final confrontation. Its clouds need depth, movement, and internal illumination.

ILM’s original production account describes creating changing formations in a tank containing fresh and salt water. The cloud material itself was pale; colored lighting supplied the nebula’s appearance.

As useful shapes developed, the crew adjusted lights, filters, and exposure to capture them. The spacecraft miniatures were photographed with lighting designed to match their particular backgrounds, and the elements were combined optically.

The same account describes the difficulty of maintaining continuity between formations photographed months apart. Capturing attractive motion was only part of the task; the resulting shots had to belong to one environment.

[American Cinematographer’s original account includes photographs of the tank photography and explains the lighting and compositing.](https://theasc.com/article/star-trek-50-part-iii-effecting-wrath-of-khan/)

## What makes the formation feel large?
Size comes from several agreements within the finished image.

**Recognizable surroundings establish scale.** A mountain, distant building, or spacecraft gives the cloud a relationship to something the viewer understands.

**Movement establishes weight and energy.** A rapidly dispersing plume can feel small. More deliberate movement may suggest a massive formation, although a supernatural event may call for sudden expansion.

**Framing removes contradictory evidence.** The water surface, injection equipment, glass edges, reflections, or stray bubbles can reveal the actual setup.

**Lighting establishes volume.** Dense regions, translucent edges, and changing illumination help the formation occupy space rather than resemble a flat pattern.

The liquid footage must support the particular image being constructed. Enlarging it alone does not settle those relationships.

## Camera orientation and speed are creative controls

The camera need not present the tank in its everyday orientation. Turning or reorienting the photographed element can make descending material appear to rise or spread in an unexpected direction.

Recording speed offers another control. Squires notes that the original work used both undercranking and overcranking: recording fewer or more frames per second than would be shown during playback, to speed up or slow down the formations.

These choices change how the movement reads. They do not make every liquid formation physically equivalent to atmospheric clouds. The finished shot still needs compatible lighting, direction, and surrounding action.

## Why the lighting matters so much
The pale cloud material can take on very different identities under different illumination.

Light from behind emphasizes translucent edges and can suggest something glowing within the formation. Side lighting reveals folds and overlapping masses. Colored illumination can turn related shapes into threatening weather, supernatural energy, or an imagined astronomical environment.

Compositing must preserve those relationships. A bright cloud edge needs a plausible relationship to the light in the rest of the scene. An object entering the cloud may need to disappear progressively, with its own lighting changing as it goes.

The integration supplies much of the effect’s credibility.

## The limitation: fluid behavior is only partly directable
Cloud tanks offer complex motion, but limited precision.

A promising formation may develop in the wrong place or disperse before the photography is ready. Once the water becomes too mixed or cloudy, the tank needs resetting. Repeating an exact shape is difficult.

That makes the technique especially useful when filmmakers can explore variations and select material afterward. It becomes more restrictive when a formation must follow an exact path, maintain a specific opening, or repeat identically across many shots.

A cloud tank also produces a visual analogy. Its usefulness comes from selected resemblances to atmospheric motion; it is not a literal working model of every phenomenon it represents.
