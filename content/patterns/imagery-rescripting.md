A person remembers being publicly humiliated and still experiences the scene as proof that nobody will intervene. In an imagined revisiting, a protective figure stops the humiliation and attends to what the person needs. The historical event has not changed. The work explores whether the scene's present emotional meaning can change when protection and agency become part of the imagined encounter.

## Core idea

Imagery Rescripting is used in Schema Therapy and related approaches to work with distressing memories and aversive imagined situations. It introduces a different response into the scene, with attention to the experiencing person's needs and the effect of the intervention.

The aim is not to persuade someone that an event happened differently. The imagined intervention is deliberately introduced and recognized as such. This distinction is central to responsible use of the method.

The defining move is a deliberate change within an imagined scene that addresses what was needed there. The new event is acknowledged as imagined. It may involve protection, choice, comfort, escape, or another fitting response, rather than a universally happy ending. The question is how the change is experienced from the position of the person whose needs matter in the scene.

## What changes in the experience

An intervention can also miss. Protection may feel controlling, comfort may arrive too quickly, or an imagined adult may demand forgiveness. These reactions provide information about what is needed and what does not fit.

It is possible to design a convincing alternative scene from an observer's perspective without experiencing any change from inside it. Rescripting therefore attends to what the intervention is like for the person represented in the scene. Feeling protected is different from merely describing protection.

The description here explains the organizing idea rather than specifying a protocol. Current clinical approaches include decisions about who introduces the intervention, when to begin, and how to manage emotional activation that require professional judgment.

The person may find an intervention helpful in theory but unavailable emotionally. They can also discover a different need from the one initially proposed. Feedback therefore concerns the lived effect of the imagined intervention: does protection reach them, does it introduce pressure, or does another part of the scene remain unresolved? The script is adjusted rather than defended.

## A worked example

In the humiliation example, changing the audience into universally admiring people may feel false or irrelevant. A more fitting intervention might be someone stopping the mistreatment, allowing the person to leave, or making clear that the behavior was unacceptable. The person's response, not the therapist's preferred happy ending, guides the work.

For example, someone imagines their adult self defending a child but remains aware mainly of the adult's anger. Further exploration may reveal that the child's need is to be taken somewhere quiet and told they did not cause the event. The revised response becomes more specific.

Suppose an imagined adult forcefully confronts the humiliating teacher. The person understands the intention but feels more exposed as everyone looks toward them. They want to leave the room with someone who takes them seriously. The imagined response changes to include privacy and an explanation that they are not responsible for the adult's conduct.

From the affected position, that change brings relief and sadness. The person can recognize the wish for protection without having to enjoy a scene of retaliation. Their original memory is not replaced with a claim that rescue occurred. Instead, the scene becomes available with a different relationship to need, responsibility, and possible support.

## Why the move may help

A memory's meaning can include expectations about helplessness, isolation, or personal defect. An imagined response may allow unmet needs to be encountered differently while the original event remains acknowledged. Several mechanisms have been proposed for imagery-based change; a meaningful session does not decide among them.

The clinical research literature concerns defined interventions and populations, not every improvised positive image. Differences in procedure, assessment, and context matter. The explanation here describes the intended experiential move and its uncertainties without claiming that a vivid alternative scene erases the original memory.

## When it helps—and what can get in the way

A second person repeatedly imagines an upcoming presentation ending in total humiliation. An imagery-based intervention may explore their capacity to respond, obtain support, or retain dignity even if a difficulty occurs. The relevant change is not a guarantee that the presentation will go well.

This distinction prevents rescripting from becoming wishful rehearsal. A useful imagined response needs to remain connected to plausible needs and available forms of agency, while clinical variants may use symbolic or imaginative elements for a specific purpose.

This may fit a distressing scene whose emotional meaning remains active despite intellectual understanding. It may fit poorly when the person does not want evocative imagery or when present circumstances require immediate external protection. A future scene can include realistic coping and support without manufacturing certainty that the feared event will not occur.

## Boundaries and common misunderstandings

Evocative memory work can be intense and is not an appropriate setting for guessing what happened or searching for hidden abuse. Uncertain details should remain uncertain. The introduction of an imagined intervention must not blur the distinction between memory, inference, and new imagery.

Assessment, consent, pacing, and closure matter, particularly with dissociation or overwhelming distress. Empty-Chair Dialogue addresses an imagined person; Receiving the Missing Experience concerns a new encounter with support; Discriminating Then from Now clarifies present context. Rescripting specifically alters the imagined course of a scene.

A practitioner should keep the status of every scene clear: remembered, uncertain, anticipated, or deliberately invented. New imagined details cannot establish historical facts. The person's response to the rescript also deserves respect; finding a protective figure intrusive is information about fit, not a reason to insist on receiving protection more fully.
