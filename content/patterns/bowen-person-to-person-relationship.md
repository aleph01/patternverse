## We talk every week. Why do we know so little about each other?

Every Sunday, Daniel phones his father, Peter. They discuss Daniel’s sister Nina, the neighbors, and whatever needs repairing.

The calls are usually pleasant. Yet Peter does not know that Daniel is considering leaving a management role he dislikes. Daniel knows little about how Peter experienced his own working life.

When Daniel considers mentioning his uncertainty, he imagines criticism. Asking about Nina feels easier.

In Bowen family systems theory, a **person-to-person relationship** is a documented relationship ideal and focus of clinical work. It concerns the capacity to communicate personally with each other, rather than depending on discussion of other people or impersonal subjects. It is not an additional core concept. [Papero, “Assisting the Two-person System,” 2014](https://www.thefsi.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2023/04/Assiting-the-Two-person-System.pdf)

## Contact without much personal exchange

The recurring problem is not a shortage of conversation. People may speak frequently while leaving their own experiences, thinking, differences, and relationship largely unmentioned.

A parent and adult child discuss another sibling. Partners discuss only their children’s difficulties. Friends maintain a connection through complaints about someone else. Each conversation can sustain contact while providing little opportunity to know the people participating.

Bowenian therapist Kathleen Smith distinguishes ordinary conversation about shared interests from relying on those subjects to manage discomfort. Sports, family news, and practical matters are not inherently obstacles. The question is whether personal exchange also has room to occur. [Smith, “Why We Stay Stuck in Superficial Friendships,” 2020](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/everything-isnt-terrible/202008/why-we-stay-stuck-in-superficial-friendships)

Bowen described person-to-person relating as an ideal involving freedom to discuss personal issues, according to his definition in *Family Therapy in Clinical Practice*, quoted by Bowenian researcher Mary Jane Wilt. An ideal provides a direction; it does not require every conversation to cover everything. [Wilt, 2023, citing Bowen, 1978, p. 499](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/anzf.1532)

## How a comfortable conversation can preserve distance

The Bowenian account emphasizes emotional reactivity: another person’s response can become so consequential that speaking personally becomes difficult.

Papero describes how an adult child may experience a parent as an emotionally charged, larger-than-life figure rather than another person with a life and history. Managing those reactions is part of developing a more direct adult relationship. [Papero](https://www.thefsi.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2023/04/Assiting-the-Two-person-System.pdf)

For Daniel, anticipated criticism may prompt concealment. Peter receives fewer facts about Daniel’s experience and has less opportunity to respond to it. Their familiar subjects keep the conversation moving. Neither has to encounter the uncertainty of disagreement about Daniel’s life.

Discussion of Nina can also provide a shared focus around which father and son feel connected. Bowen’s triangle concept proposes that tension can be distributed through three-person relationships without resolving the underlying difficulty. This is a theoretical formulation, not evidence that every mention of an absent person is triangling. [Bowen Center: Triangles](https://www.thebowencenter.org/triangles)

Daniel’s fear of criticism and Peter’s possible discomfort remain hypotheses. Their observable pattern—what gets discussed, what is omitted, and what happens when a personal subject appears—is the better starting point.

## Making more room for each person

Developing this relationship involves changing one’s participation while allowing the other person a response of their own.

In a November 1968 letter, Bowen described reviewing family contacts, examining the individual’s contribution to the family system, and working on person-to-person relationships across the extended family. His account placed the effort in ongoing relationships rather than in a single revealing conversation. [Bowen Archives: “Who Is in Your Family?”](https://murraybowenarchives.org/boyd-book/chapter-7/)

For Daniel, that could mean offering something accurate about his own life, listening to Peter’s thinking, and remaining interested without requiring approval. It could also mean asking about Peter’s experience without turning the conversation into an interview or demanding disclosure.

The Bowen Center’s account of differentiated functioning describes people expressing their views, considering another’s interests, and remaining available without feeling compelled to fix every discomfort. Personal exchange can therefore include help and reassurance; neither person must take over the other’s thinking or feelings. [Bowen Center: Differentiation of Self](https://www.thebowencenter.org/differentiation-of-self)

Daniel controls what he shares, how he listens, and how he responds to disagreement. He cannot produce Peter’s curiosity, openness, or agreement.

## Worked example: a different Sunday call

*The people, dialogue, and continuations below are invented teaching examples.*

### The familiar conversation

“How’s Nina getting on?” Peter asks.

“She’s changed jobs again.”

“She never sticks at anything.”

“Exactly.”

They discuss Nina’s choices, then finish the call pleasantly. Daniel’s work decision remains unmentioned. He has learned little about Peter’s experience, either.

Their conversation has maintained contact through a familiar shared subject.

### Different participation

“How’s Nina getting on?”

“I spoke to her Friday; she’s fine. I’ve got an update of my own: I’m considering leaving management.”

“Throw away a promotion? That’s irresponsible.”

Daniel notices the urge to defend himself at length or abandon the subject.

“I know you disagree. I’m still weighing it. I’m not asking you to decide.”

He explains that he misses technical work and dislikes managing staff. He does not announce that Peter is controlling or insist that his father understand.

After listening to Peter’s objections, Daniel asks, “What was changing jobs like for you?”

“I was afraid of losing a steady income,” Peter says. “I stayed longer than I wanted.”

Daniel learns something about his father’s experience. Peter still disapproves. Daniel has not decided what to do.

The following week, Daniel gives a brief update rather than pretending the conversation never happened. He also remains interested in Peter’s current life.

This is one possible continuation. Peter might offer little, change the subject, or become more critical. Daniel’s contribution can change without immediately producing a reciprocal exchange.

## Limits and common mistakes

**Personal does not mean unrestricted disclosure.** The aim is greater capacity for personal exchange, not surrendering privacy. Asking someone about their life does not create an entitlement to an answer.

**Directness can become pressure.** “We need to have a real relationship” can become a demand that another person disclose, approve, or behave differently. An I-position concerns one’s own thinking and conduct; it is not an instruction for the other person. [Papero](https://www.thefsi.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2023/04/Assiting-the-Two-person-System.pdf)

**A quiet relationship is not automatically emotional cutoff.** Bowen’s cutoff concept concerns managing unresolved emotional issues through reduced contact or avoidance. Reserve, practical conversation, cultural conventions, or a single awkward call cannot establish that process. [Bowen Center: Emotional Cutoff](https://www.thebowencenter.org/emotional-cutoff)

**Power and safety still matter.** A financially dependent person may face real consequences for disagreement. Children do not bear adult responsibility for developing their parents’ openness. Coercion, mistreatment, communication needs, and caregiving obligations must remain visible. Greater disclosure or renewed contact should not be treated as mandatory.

The sources establish terminology and describe theoretical and clinical approaches. They do not demonstrate that Daniel’s invented dialogue causes better outcomes. Nor can limited personal exchange diagnose someone’s differentiation or adequately explain an illness.

## Related Bowen concepts

**Differentiation of self** provides the broader frame: clearer self-definition while remaining meaningfully connected. **I-position** concerns expressing and acting on one’s own thinking.

**Triangles** and **detriangling** help explain relationships organized around third parties. **Emotional cutoff** addresses distance and avoidance used to manage emotional intensity. Person-to-person work belongs within this wider family system; it does not create a relationship isolated from everyone else. [Bowen Center: Differentiation](https://www.thebowencenter.org/differentiation-of-self), [Bowen Archives](https://murraybowenarchives.org/bowen-theory/)
