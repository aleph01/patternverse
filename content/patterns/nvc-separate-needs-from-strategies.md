*An editorial pattern drawn from Nonviolent Communication. The examples and dialogue are fictional teaching illustrations.*

One partner wants to spend Saturday together. The other wants the day alone. The first says, “I need you to make time for me.” The second says, “I need you to stop demanding things.” Each person’s need seems to require the other person’s surrender.

There may be a real scheduling conflict. There is also a distinction worth making: the thing someone wants to do is not necessarily the underlying reason it matters.

## Hold the purpose steady and loosen the method

NVC distinguishes needs or values from particular actions intended to serve them. Connection, rest, autonomy, and support name broad concerns. Spending Saturday together, staying home alone, or asking a particular person for help are strategies. Rosenberg’s process overview explicitly separates needs and values from a preference or specific action. [Rosenberg, “How You Can Use the NVC Process”](https://www.nonviolentcommunication.com/pdf_files/4part_nvc_process.pdf)

The practical question is: “What would this arrangement give me that matters?” It may reveal why a proposed solution feels so urgent. It may also reveal other arrangements worth considering.

Flexibility belongs at the level of strategy. Acknowledging a need should not require pretending that its current absence is comfortable.

## A weekend with competing meanings

For the partner asking for time together, Saturday represents reassurance that the relationship still has a place in an overcrowded week. For the partner asking for solitude, it represents recovery after six days of social obligations.

Once those meanings are visible, a different conversation becomes possible:

> “I miss having unhurried time with you. I don’t particularly need us to do the shopping together.”

> “I want to spend time with you too. I’m exhausted, and being around anyone all day feels difficult. Could I have Saturday on my own and plan Sunday breakfast with you?”

Sunday breakfast might work. It might not: perhaps one partner is leaving town, or repeated postponements have damaged trust. The pattern creates options; it does not certify that the first alternative is adequate.

A more complete agreement might include the immediate plan and a recurring arrangement: solitude this Saturday, breakfast on Sunday, and one protected evening together each week. Different strategies can serve different parts of the situation.

## A workplace dispute about visibility

A manager asks everyone to stay online until six. An employee objects because their working day begins early and they need to collect their child from school.

At the level of positions, the options appear to be compliance or defiance. Exploring the manager’s concern reveals that important client questions have gone unanswered late in the day. The manager wants reliable coverage and confidence that someone owns the response.

The employee wants to fulfill caregiving commitments and have a predictable schedule. They are willing to contribute to coverage but cannot personally remain available every afternoon.

Possible strategies include a rotating late shift, a shared response queue, or assigning a named backup before the employee leaves. Each proposal can be evaluated against the actual operational requirement and the employee’s constraints.

This example also exposes a limit: if there are too few staff to provide coverage, a vocabulary change will not create staffing capacity. Clarifying needs may reveal a resource problem that needs an organizational decision.

## Preferences can carry real knowledge

“Separate needs from strategies” does not mean all strategies are interchangeable. A person may prefer written instructions because spoken instructions are difficult to retain. A worker may need a particular tool to access a document. A relationship may include an explicit agreement that makes a specific action especially important.

The useful question is whether an alternative actually serves the concern, with acceptable costs. It is not whether someone can imagine a theoretically similar option.

A friend seeking companionship might be happy to call someone else tonight. A person seeking repair after you broke their confidence may reasonably need a response from you. The second situation includes responsibility that cannot simply be outsourced.

## Common mistakes

**Hiding a strategy inside the word “need.”** “I need you to agree with me” specifies another person’s response. The underlying concern might involve reassurance, coordination, or being understood. Agreement is only one possible route, and sometimes an unavailable one.

**Using flexibility to pressure the less powerful person.** “Your need is security; surely you can meet it another way” can become an excuse to avoid a promised payment or agreed protection. Clarification should illuminate obligations, not dissolve them.

**Treating needs as automatically compatible.** Naming rest and connection does not create more hours in a day. Some situations require prioritization, compromise, additional resources, or accepting disappointment.

**Jumping to alternatives before acknowledging the loss.** If someone wanted you at an important event, immediately offering a replacement activity can sound dismissive. Understanding what your absence meant may need to come first.

## Where this pattern earns its place

This distinction is especially useful when a disagreement has narrowed to one demanded solution, when several people want the same scarce resource, or when you feel trapped by your own first idea.

It can also guide solitary decisions. Someone convinced they must quit a project may discover that they mainly need a manageable workload or clearer authority. They may still choose to leave, but can assess the choice against the concern they are trying to address.
