*Describe a substantial event in disproportionately mild language.*

Something extreme receives language too mild for it. Listeners recognize the difference between the event’s scale and the speaker’s description, and supply the missing intensity themselves.

## Example 1 · Jerry Seinfeld

Describing the world’s heaviest man

> Have you ever seen that guy who has the record for fattest man in the world? Bob Hughes, the fattest man in the world… 1400 pounds. Ladies and gentlemen, the man has let himself go  

— Jerry Seinfeld

**The turn:** A small social judgment describes an extreme condition.

[Source](<https://www.dead-frog.com/comedians/jokes/jerry-seinfeld>)

## Example 2 · George Carlin

Death euphemisms — Doin’ It Again

> “Older” sounds a little better than “old” doesn’t it? Sounds like it might last a little longer…I’m getting old. And it’s OK because thanks to our fear of death in this country, I won’t have to die—I’ll “pass away.” Or I’ll “expire,” like a magazine subscription. If it happens in the hospital, they’ll call it a “terminal episode.” The insurance company will refer to it as a “negative outcome.” And if it is the result of malpractice, they’ll say it was a therapeutic misadventure.  

— George Carlin

## Example 3 · Bill Bailey

British happiness — Limboland

Bailey contrasts exuberant Australian enthusiasm with a British greeting that merely reports things are not too bad. He treats this tiny concession as the upper end of the national happiness scale.

— Bill Bailey

## What makes the structure work

The audience must already understand the scale being minimized. Otherwise the line can sound merely vague. Bureaucratic euphemism is one route; an ordinary social judgment applied to an extraordinary condition is another.
