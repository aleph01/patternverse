A person becomes angry with themselves for crying. By the time they describe the encounter, anger seems to be the whole problem. On closer attention, they recognize that sadness came first, followed by embarrassment, then self-directed anger about having been visibly affected.

## Core idea

Finding the feeling beneath the reaction means distinguishing an initial emotional response from a response to that response. Emotion-Focused Therapy calls these primary and secondary emotions. The distinction concerns their function and sequence in a particular situation, not a permanent hierarchy of authentic and inauthentic feelings.

The initial emotion is not always the quietest or gentlest. Anger may be a direct response to a boundary violation. Fear may be an immediate response to danger. Sadness may respond to loss. Each can also appear in a secondary role, depending on what happened.

The defining move is a distinction between what was felt and what happened in response to feeling it. The question concerns sequence and function, not a hierarchy of authentic and inauthentic emotions. A secondary response can itself be painful and important, while the initial response may be either fitting to the situation or part of an entrenched emotional pattern.

## What changes in the experience

A useful account includes what each emotion does. Embarrassment might make the person hide, anger might restore a sense of control, and sadness might register a disappointment. Several functions can coexist without one cancelling the others.

EFT also distinguishes primary responses that fit a current situation from entrenched emotional responses that may require transformation. The primary-secondary distinction alone is therefore insufficient. Earlier in a sequence does not mean truer, wiser, or healthier.

Attention may reveal a transition previously compressed into one word: sadness, awareness of being seen, embarrassment, then anger directed at the tears. Each component has a different object and action tendency. Describing the distinctions can allow the person to recognize which response needs company, which carries a rule, and which concerns the present situation.

## A worked example

In the example, the person's anger partly functions to stop crying. If the therapist treats anger as the only material, the sadness and the rule against showing it remain unexplored. If the therapist declares that all anger hides sadness, they impose a different simplification. The sequence needs to be discovered with this person.

The therapist initially asks what makes the person so angry with themselves. The answer is a familiar account of being too sensitive. When they revisit the moment, the client recognizes that the first tears concerned a friend's departure. Embarrassment appeared when another person noticed. Anger then helped them stop crying and return to an apparently capable manner.

Calling the sadness 'the real feeling' would miss the client's dilemma. Being visibly affected has consequences they care about. The work includes both the sadness and the expectation of humiliation. The client eventually says that they want to choose who sees their tears, rather than eliminate them. The distinction creates options without requiring unrestricted expression or declaring the anger a mistake.

## Why the move may help

Distinguishing layers can prevent a conversation from repeatedly addressing the reaction while leaving its object untouched. Soothing anxiety about tears differs from acknowledging grief. Challenging self-criticism differs from recognizing a legitimate need for protection.

Different responses may need different forms of engagement. Reassurance about performance may not reach shame; suppressing anger may leave grief unaccompanied; encouraging sadness may be irrelevant when direct anger concerns an actual violation. Differentiation can prevent a well-intended intervention from repeatedly addressing the wrong part of the experience.

EFT's primary/secondary distinction is a clinical formulation, not a laboratory test that identifies a uniquely correct emotion. The formulation earns its usefulness when the person recognizes a sequence and the work becomes more accurate. Some episodes remain mixed, and later understanding may revise what first seemed primary.

## When it helps—and what can get in the way

The pattern is useful when someone says they should not feel a certain way, when a response abruptly replaces another, or when regulation leaves an important emotion unrecognized. It can also help explain why a well-intended intervention misses: reassurance about embarrassment may not address the loss that elicited tears.

Imagine someone who feels shame immediately after a minor correction, then becomes anxious about being ashamed. Separating the anxiety from the shame can clarify the experience. It does not establish that the shame is an accurate judgment of the self or an adaptive guide to action.

Another person feels anger after being mistreated and then guilt about being angry. Here, exploring the guilt may reveal a learned rule against disagreement. Contacting protective anger could support a clearer boundary. The therapist still needs to consider the actual relationship and consequences of expression.

## Boundaries and common misunderstandings

There need not be a hidden feeling under every response. Someone can be angry because they are angry about what happened. A therapist who insists on vulnerability can make assertiveness unacceptable, especially where cultural or gender expectations already discourage it.

The person may also be unable to identify a clean sequence. Emotions overlap, descriptions develop over time, and physical discomfort can contribute. Uncertainty is preferable to inventing a tidy emotional stack.

The language of 'beneath' can encourage endless excavation. A therapist may privilege tears over anger or assume that a quieter emotion is deeper. The person may then learn to produce the preferred emotional display. An ordinary direct account—'I was angry because that was unfair'—can be sufficient and should not require a hidden softer explanation.
