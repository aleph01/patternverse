### Two interpretations of the same meeting

A person leaves a meeting convinced that a quiet colleague disliked the proposal. The colleague spoke rarely, asked for written details, and did not join the enthusiastic discussion at the end. From one perspective, the behavior suggests disapproval. From another, it looks like a preference for reviewing information before reaching a conclusion. A third interpretation concerns the colleague's role: perhaps they were responsible for checking feasibility and considered enthusiasm premature. The observations are fairly stable; the assumptions connecting them to a meaning vary.

In Sleight of Mouth, Robert Dilts describes Model of the World as reconsidering a belief through another framework for interpreting experience. People approach events with different expectations, distinctions, priorities, and histories. A behavior that signifies commitment in one setting may signify recklessness in another. Trying another model makes these differences available for thought rather than treating the first interpretation as the only one the event could support.

The pattern goes beyond imagining another person's feelings. It asks how that person organizes the situation. What counts as relevant evidence? What would a responsible response look like? Which risks are easy to notice from their position? These questions help explain why two people can attend to the same facts and still reach different judgments without either being unintelligent or deliberately obstructive.

### Understanding the logic of another position

An engineer and a support specialist discuss a feature that requires several additional setup steps. The engineer sees the steps as a small technical cost that enables a more powerful system. The support specialist sees a probable increase in confused customers and repeated help requests. Both understand the interface, but they meet it through different streams of experience. The engineer encounters its flexibility during development; the support specialist encounters its confusing parts when someone is already frustrated.

Trying the support specialist's model changes the questions the engineer asks. The setup is no longer evaluated only by whether a capable user can complete it. It is also evaluated by whether a new user understands why each step exists and can recover from a mistake. Trying the engineer's model may likewise reveal why removing every step would lose an important capability. The shared design can then respond to both kinds of experience rather than treating one as resistance to progress.

A personal example might involve friendship. One person regards frequent messages as evidence of care, while another sees giving space as a way of respecting autonomy. Each can interpret the other's behavior as a lack of consideration. Understanding the models does not determine how often they should communicate, but it changes what requires negotiation. They can discuss a rhythm that feels workable instead of defending their own custom as the universal meaning of care.

### Perspective taking still needs verification

Imagining another model carries the risk of inventing a person who conveniently explains the situation. A listener might assume that a colleague's caution comes from their profession, age, or culture without asking about the actual concern. Group membership can suggest possible influences, but it does not establish an individual's interpretation. The most informative version of the pattern combines tentative reconstruction with opportunities for correction by the person whose perspective is being considered.

There is also a temptation to conclude that every model is equally accurate. Different perspectives can reveal different information, but claims about what happened can still be checked. A customer's interpretation of a confusing interface deserves attention even if their explanation of the underlying system is technically mistaken. An engineer's explanation may be accurate while failing to account for the experience of using it. Respect for perspective and evaluation of evidence can coexist.

Power changes the conversation as well. Understanding a manager's pressures may help an employee navigate a disagreement, but it should not require accepting unreasonable demands. A person can understand how a decision makes sense within another model and still reject its consequences or assumptions. The pattern expands interpretation; it does not grant every interpretation authority over other people's boundaries.

### More information at the point of disagreement

Try Another Model of the World helps when people mistake their own conventions for obvious facts or a behavior seems to have only one possible meaning. Inspect How You Know examines the evidence behind the initial interpretation. Find the Positive Intention explores the purpose another person may be pursuing. Find an Analogy can introduce another way of understanding a relationship without claiming to represent a particular person's worldview.

Returning to the quiet colleague, the appropriate result is greater curiosity rather than a new certainty that they secretly approved. They may have disliked the proposal, needed time, or been occupied with another issue. The added models identify what is still unknown and how it might be clarified. They also reduce the chance that an unverified interpretation will shape the next interaction as though the colleague had already stated it.

## Pattern details

### Use when

- A behavior seems to have only one possible interpretation.
- Different roles or conventions produce recurring misunderstanding.

### Goals

- Understand the logic behind another position.
- Identify interpretations that require clarification.

### Mechanisms

- perspective taking
- interpretive model comparison

### Not ideal when

- Imagined perspectives become stereotypes or replace asking the actual person.

### Difficulty

easy

```patternverse-language
:language 1
:title "Try Another Model of the World"
!show {
    Choose a manageable, everyday situation. This is self-reflection, not treatment or emergency care. You can stop at any time. Do not use reframing to excuse harm, deny facts, or override a boundary. Your answers are not saved when you leave.
}
!ask facts {
    Separate the event and interpretation

    Describe an everyday disagreement or ambiguous behavior. What did you observe, and how are you interpreting it?
}
!ask model {
    Notice your own model

    What expectations, priorities, or conventions make your interpretation seem natural to you?
}
!ask alternative {
    Imagine another possible model

    What different priorities or experience could make the same event look different? Treat this as a possibility, not a diagnosis or stereotype.
}
!ask difference {
    Compare the questions

    What would seem important within that model? Which facts stay the same, and what remains unknown?
}
!ask limits {
    Keep evidence and boundaries

    Which claims can be checked? What boundary or unacceptable consequence remains even if you understand the other position?
}
!ask next {
    Invite correction

    What respectful question could you ask, or what bounded observation could help clarify the person's actual view? Do not assume you now know their motives.
}
```
