A person expects that disagreeing will end a valued relationship. They can list occasions when friends accepted disagreement, yet the expectation remains untouched. In therapy, one particular experience becomes vivid while the fear is also present: a friend listened to a clear refusal, remained warm, and continued the relationship. The two knowings become available together rather than living in separate compartments.

## Core idea

Coherence Therapy calls this a juxtaposition experience. A specific emotional expectation is held alongside personally convincing knowledge that contradicts it. The contrast must concern what the expectation actually predicts, not simply provide a generally positive experience.

Someone may fear that becoming visible invites attack. Praise does not necessarily contradict that expectation; visibility could still be experienced as dangerous despite approval. Another person may believe they will be abandoned if they refuse a request. A warm exchange that contains no refusal does not directly address that belief.

This specificity distinguishes juxtaposition from reassurance. A preferred idea may be comforting without conflicting with the particular knowledge organizing the difficulty.

The defining move is concurrent awareness of an identified expectation and experience that conflicts with it in the relevant respect. The target is specific emotional knowledge, not an unpopular general belief. A contradiction must be personally credible to the client; a therapist's persuasive argument is not enough.

## What changes in the experience

This is why the person's own recognition matters. An argument that looks logically decisive from outside may leave the emotional position unchanged.

Contradictory knowing may come from a remembered event, a current relationship, or an experience newly encountered in therapy or daily life. Its source is less important than its fit with the target expectation and the person's ability to hold both in awareness.

The person may recognize a mismatch, discover an exception that preserves the old rule, or realize that the proposed experience does not address it. Each possibility changes the formulation. The work is therefore not a search for positive evidence until the client agrees. It depends on accurate contact with both what is expected and what has actually been experienced.

## A worked example

In the opening example, the relevant experience includes disagreement and continued connection. The person can recognize why those features matter. The therapist does not need to convince them that every relationship is safe or that rejection is impossible.

Suppose the person recalls telling a friend they could not attend an event, followed by an ordinary affectionate exchange the next day. At first this seems to contradict the expectation that disagreement ends connection. The client then notices that they had offered an elaborate explanation and a compensating favor. The event may not contradict the more exact rule: refusal is acceptable only if it is fully repaid.

They identify another interaction in which a limit was accepted without repayment, but it still feels like a lucky exception. The therapist does not declare the rule disproved. They explore what made the second relationship different and whether that difference is relevant elsewhere. The outcome is sharper knowledge of the expectation and of the conditions under which contradictory experience becomes believable.

## Why the move may help

CT's reconsolidation account is a theoretical interpretation of the clinical process, rather than a biological event that can be directly certified from a client's reaction. Experiential mismatch, relief, changed expectations, and lasting symptom change are distinguishable observations. A session can provide one without establishing all the others.

The practical rationale is that general reassurance often leaves the exact emotional prediction untouched. A precise comparison may make a discrepancy available that was previously compartmentalized or dismissed. That is a useful therapeutic question without a guarantee that the discrepancy will produce permanent change.

## When it helps—and what can get in the way

A person believes that needing assistance makes them contemptible, while readily respecting friends who ask for help. That difference can remain conceptual: they may believe their own needs are uniquely unacceptable. A therapist cannot assume that pointing out inconsistency creates a meaningful mismatch.

The exploration needs to clarify the actual exception. Perhaps what feels dangerous is asking without repaying immediately, and their admired friends always reciprocate. The initial contradiction was incomplete. A more specific expectation requires different knowledge, or may remain uncontradicted.

This fits a clearly identified expectation and a potentially relevant experience that the person already recognizes or newly encounters. It may fit poorly when the target remains vague, the supposed contradiction is weak, or current circumstances confirm the concern. Discovery and practical assessment may need to precede any attempt at juxtaposition.

## Boundaries and common misunderstandings

A therapist should not pressure a client to admit that the old expectation is false. Contradictory knowledge must be personally credible, and the person can find that an apparent contradiction does not fit. More accurate understanding is preferable to a dramatic declaration of transformation.

For example, a person expresses disappointment toward a therapist and discovers that the therapist acknowledges a mistake without withdrawing. Repairing the Rupture may supply a relevant experience. Whether it becomes a juxtaposition depends on whether the old expectation and this different response are actually encountered together.

The therapist's wish to demonstrate successful change can distort both sides of the comparison. Continued uncertainty should not become evidence that the client is resisting a fact. An exception may be narrow, and real relationships may remain inconsistent. The work should increase discrimination rather than replace a rigid negative rule with an equally rigid positive one.
