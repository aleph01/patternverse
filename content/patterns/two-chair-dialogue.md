A person describes an exhausting internal argument. One position demands that they work harder; another wants to stop trying altogether. Discussing the conflict produces more reasons for both sides. When each position is given a separate chair and a direct voice, the person begins to notice what the argument actually does to them.

## Core idea

Two-Chair Dialogue uses spatial separation and enactment to make an internal interaction accessible. Gestalt and Emotion-Focused Therapy use chair work in different ways; in EFT, specific tasks address recognizable markers such as self-criticism or self-interruption. The furniture is a support for the task, not its mechanism by itself.

In a self-critical dialogue, one position voices a particular demand or judgment and the experiencing position responds. The aim is not to let the critic deliver unlimited abuse. The therapist helps keep the interaction specific, tracks its impact, and supports the emergence of feelings and needs that are otherwise difficult to express.

The defining move is an enacted relationship between distinguishable positions. Rather than narrating what each says, the person experiences speaking and receiving within the exchange. Spatial separation helps mark whose perspective is active, but the chairs themselves are a format. The clinical task depends on the particular conflict being explored.

## What changes in the experience

The critic may then reveal a fear of exclusion if the person disappoints others. This does not guarantee that both positions will reconcile. It does make their interaction more concrete and can reveal what each needs to understand.

Separating positions can make shifts in voice, feeling, posture, and intention easier to notice. Each position can address the other rather than being summarized by an observer. This provides material that discussion alone may not reveal.

### Enacting the interruption

Sometimes the problem is not a spoken criticism but a process that stops emotion as it emerges. Someone tightens, jokes, changes the subject, or tells themselves not to feel. EFT's self-interruption task explores both the interrupting action and the experience of being interrupted.

Imagine a person who laughs whenever they begin to express hurt. In an enactment, the interrupting position may become explicit: "Keep it light so nobody has to deal with you." From the experiencing position, they recognize the loneliness created by that instruction. The therapist can then help explore its protective purpose and its cost.

Not every laugh is an interruption. It may express pleasure, embarrassment, cultural style, or connection. The task requires a collaboratively recognized pattern, not the therapist's dislike of humor.

In self-critical work, the exchange makes a demand and its impact accessible. In interruption work, an action that cuts off experience becomes the subject. Both require a collaborative formulation: the practitioner cannot decide from laughter or a change of posture alone that an interruption has occurred. The person's understanding determines whether the task fits.

## A worked example

For example, "You are lazy" becomes a more specific demand about answering messages immediately. From the receiving position, the person notices both fear and exhaustion. Their response may become, "I need time to think, and I cannot be available to everyone all day."

The therapist asks the receiving position for a strong response to the critic. The client produces a defiant sentence but says it feels false. What actually arises is exhaustion and a request for enough time to think before answering. With this response given a hearing, the demand to reply immediately becomes more concrete: the critical position expects delay to cost the relationship.

The person can now recognize an interaction rather than a contest between laziness and discipline. One position is frightened of disappointing others; the other cannot function under constant acceleration. They do not finish with a permanent agreement. They identify a first workable limit around one kind of message, and also acknowledge the fear that the limit evokes. The dialogue has clarified needs and consequences without appointing a winner.

## Why the move may help

Enactment can make the impact of a familiar internal message harder to overlook. A demand that sounds sensible in summary may feel humiliating when addressed directly; a vague wish to give up may reveal a precise need for rest or autonomy. The shift in position can expose distinctions that a global debate has concealed.

Gestalt and EFT give chair work different formulations and task emphases. A changed tone, clearer need, or softened criticism can be meaningful in a session without establishing lasting resolution. Emotional intensity, dramatic performance, and an apparently conciliatory ending are not adequate measures of benefit on their own.

## When it helps—and what can get in the way

The pattern is useful when a repeated internal exchange is identifiable and the person can participate without losing chosen contact with the session. Abstract ambivalence may need clarification first. A person may prefer imaginal positions, objects, or ordinary dialogue; adaptations should preserve choice and acknowledge their differences.

The pattern fits a recognizable internal interaction with sufficiently distinct positions. It can be less helpful when the person cannot locate separate voices or feels pushed into theatrical expression. A conversation using their own ordinary language may preserve more contact. The format can also be adapted rather than treating physical chair switching as essential.

## Boundaries and common misunderstandings

Chair work can become emotionally intense. More intensity is not automatically more productive. The practitioner needs to recognize when to deepen, regulate, change the task, or stop. Unresolved work still needs an adequate ending and a return to the person's present situation.

A common mistake is choosing a winner in advance. Another is treating every position as a harmful critic. A demand may contain an important concern even when its manner is damaging. Equally, understanding the concern should not require accepting abusive self-talk.

A facilitator should not script a stronger, kinder, or more emotional reply as the correct endpoint. Forced compassion can suppress the objection the dialogue needs to reveal. The ending also matters: the person may need to step back from the enacted positions, identify what remains unresolved, and reconnect with the present encounter.
