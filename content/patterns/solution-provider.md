## What it is for

Use this pattern when customers need a working result but lack the expertise, time, or capacity to assemble and manage the necessary components themselves.

A business opening a new office can buy computers, networking equipment, software, and support from separate suppliers. It can still end up with a system that fails in everyday use.

Each component may work individually. The difficulty lies in selecting them, connecting them, configuring them, and keeping them working together.

A Solution Provider takes responsibility for those connections within an agreed scope.

The customer purchases an integrated answer to a defined need, supported by the products, expertise, and services required to make it work.

## The core idea

Start with the customer’s problem, then organize the necessary capabilities around it.

The provider translates requirements into a design, selects or supplies components, manages implementation, and verifies that the resulting solution meets agreed criteria.

The relationship may continue through maintenance, operation, training, or improvement.

This goes deeper than offering a broad assortment. A One-Stop Shop gives customers a convenient destination for related purchases. A Solution Provider takes on more of the work and responsibility required to make those purchases function together.

The provider can use its own products, outside suppliers, or both. Its distinctive contribution is the ability to integrate them into a dependable solution.

The agreed scope defines the promise. Responsibility for a production line, tool fleet, or maintenance service does not mean responsibility for every aspect of the customer’s business.

## How it creates value

**It connects expertise across boundaries.** The provider understands how equipment, software, processes, and people affect one another.

**It reduces coordination work.** Customers spend less time translating requirements between suppliers, managing handoffs, and investigating who should resolve a problem.

**It makes requirements testable.** A broad ambition becomes a specification that can guide design and acceptance: capacity, compatibility, response times, or other relevant measures.

**It supports continuity.** When ongoing service is included, knowledge gained during implementation can inform maintenance and improvement.

The value comes from the combined system. A small improvement in one component may matter less than eliminating a recurring failure between two components.

That wider perspective is part of what the customer pays for.

## Famous example: Tetra Pak

Tetra Pak combines food-processing and packaging equipment with automation, engineering, implementation, and services.

Its integrated lines connect equipment and controls across production stages. The company describes solutions extending from raw-material reception to packaged products on pallets, supported by services and performance commitments. [Tetra Pak: Integrated Lines](https://www.tetrapak.com/en-us/solutions/integrated-solutions-equipment/integrated-lines)

Its plant-building approach covers requirements, design, implementation, commissioning, and potential continuing maintenance and optimization. [Tetra Pak: The Plant-Building Process](https://www.tetrapak.com/en-us/insights/cases-articles/plant-building-process)

The customer’s need is a functioning production capability. Individual machines are components of that capability.

This illustrates the importance of integration: capacities, controls, material flows, and operating requirements need to fit together.

It also shows why scope must be established collaboratively. A provider needs to understand the customer’s products, site, workflows, and constraints before it can define a credible solution.

## Famous example: Hilti Fleet Management

Hilti extends its tool offering into managing a customer’s tool fleet.

Its US Fleet Management service combines tools with monthly fees covering repairs, maintenance, theft coverage, and other services. It also offers advice on the tool mix, digital labels, and loan tools during eligible repairs. Terms and exclusions apply. [Hilti: Tool Fleet Management](https://www.hilti.com/content/hilti/W1/US/en/business/business/equipment/fleet.html)

The proposition addresses the work around owning tools: selecting them, budgeting, arranging repairs, replacing equipment, and handling administration.

The tool remains essential, while the service takes on part of the surrounding management burden.

The boundary is equally clear. Managing a tool fleet can support construction work, but it does not make Hilti responsible for completing the customer’s building project. The solution serves a defined operational need within that larger activity.

## Famous example: Rolls-Royce TotalCare

Rolls-Royce TotalCare integrates engine-maintenance planning, management, repair, and overhaul.

The company describes it as a risk-transfer service covering specified maintenance-cost and time-on-wing risks. Payment is based on engine flying hours, and Rolls-Royce coordinates services across suppliers while the customer retains operational control. [Rolls-Royce: TotalCare](https://www.rolls-royce.com/products-and-services/civil-aerospace/services/totalcare.aspx)

Here, the solution includes technical expertise, planning, supplier coordination, and responsibility for defined risks.

The payment arrangement creates an incentive to manage maintenance and reliability effectively over time. That incentive depends on the actual agreement and the costs the provider bears.

This example combines Solution Provider with usage-based charging and risk transfer. Those features strengthen this particular model; other solution providers can charge differently.

## The business model

A solution provider can earn through project fees, equipment margins, implementation charges, recurring service contracts, usage charges, or performance-related payments.

The defining feature is the responsibility it accepts, rather than one particular payment method.

A customer might own the equipment while purchasing an integrated implementation. Alternatively, the provider might retain assets and supply an ongoing service.

Either arrangement requires clarity about deliverables, dependencies, acceptance, maintenance, and changes.

A broader customer relationship can also reveal opportunities for improvement. Those insights become commercially valuable when the provider can turn them into better performance or lower operating costs.

## The economics

Customers may pay more to reduce coordination effort, implementation risk, downtime, or the need for specialist staff.

The provider can earn from that expertise and from repeatable ways of delivering the solution. Standard designs, reusable software, established supplier relationships, and accumulated operating knowledge can improve its economics.

Responsibility also creates costs.

A fixed-price project can lose money through misunderstood requirements, integration problems, or repeated changes. An ongoing service can become expensive when failures occur more frequently than expected.

The business therefore needs to price the whole delivery obligation, including project management, support, subcontractors, rework, and the risks it accepts.

Selling more components does not automatically produce a better solution or a better margin.

## Useful when

This pattern is especially useful when several components must work together and failures between them are costly.

It fits complex installations, operational systems, and services where customers value expertise and dependable implementation more than choosing every component themselves.

It is strongest when the provider understands the problem well enough to define a realistic scope and has sufficient control over the factors it promises to manage.

It fits less well when requirements remain highly exploratory or success depends mainly on decisions outside the provider’s influence.

## Common mistakes and limits

**Calling a collection of products a solution.** The components need to work together in the customer’s setting, with responsibility for integration made explicit.

**Accepting an undefined outcome.** Ambitions such as “improve productivity” need agreed measures, assumptions, and boundaries.

**Taking responsibility without control.** A provider cannot reliably manage a promise when it lacks access, authority, information, or dependable partners.

**Customizing everything.** Excessive variation can make each sale a new engineering project. Repeatable elements help preserve delivery quality and margins.

**Leaving handoffs unresolved.** Subcontracting does not remove the need to establish who coordinates work and handles failures.

**Making future changes unnecessarily difficult.** Documentation, usable data, training, and clear exit arrangements help customers retain confidence in a deeper supplier relationship.
