**Pattern 20 of 49 · Part 4: Melody and Hooks**

**Use when:** A short idea is memorable but its next phrase feels unrelated.

A songwriter can improvise for several minutes and still leave without a phrase they want to remember. The problem may be too much available material. A small motif makes development inspectable: the writer can hear what stays connected and what changes.

Start with a Motif means treating a short rhythmic or melodic identity as material for a larger phrase. A motif can contain only a few notes. Its identity may depend as much on timing, accents, or direction as on the exact pitches.

## Working choice

Preserve a motif’s identifying rhythm or interval relationship while building a connected reply. A motif is material for development, not a requirement that every bar visibly derive from one cell.

## Hear the seed as a combination of features

Porch Light's seed is E4–G4–A4 with durations one, one, and two quarter-note beats. It rises by a minor third and then a major second. Its longest note comes last. Those features together give it a compact identity.

A writer does not need to preserve every feature in every appearance. Keeping the rhythm while changing the pitches creates one family of variations. Keeping the pitch contour while changing the rhythm creates another. The original is useful as a reference against which these changes can be heard.

A motif can remain useful even when its later appearances are separated by several phrases. Recognizable timing can connect those distant returns.

The seed is not yet a full melody. It needs placement, continuation, and a relationship to harmony. Repeating it for the duration of an eight-bar verse could be effective in a particular setting, but it does not automatically supply the verse's phrasing or lyrical movement.

## Build a four-bar phrase

An original sketch places the seed over C in bar one. Bar two holds G4 for two beats and leaves two beats of rest. Bar three repeats E4–G4–A4 over F, where A is a chord tone. Bar four answers with G4–E4–D4 over G, using the same one–one–two rhythm.

| Bar | Harmony | Melody and duration in beats |
| --- | ------- | ---------------------------- |
| 1   | C       | E4(1), G4(1), A4(2)          |
| 2   | Am      | G4(2), rest(2)               |
| 3   | F       | E4(1), G4(1), A4(2)          |
| 4   | G       | G4(1), E4(1), D4(2)          |

This is a four-bar development sketch, not the full song's two-bars-per-chord verse. Naming the distinction keeps the demonstration honest. The sketch makes the motif recur in a changed harmonic context and then supplies a related answer.

## Choose a different continuation

Suppose the answer feels too final for an opening. A version ending on B4 over G can point toward a later C5. The rhythm remains familiar, but the melodic destination changes. Another version can keep D4 and shorten it, leaving a rest that invites a new line.

These alternatives answer specific questions: should the phrase invite continuation, settle briefly, or create a sharper reach toward the next idea? That is more useful than generating random note permutations and hoping one feels inspired.

An answer need not reverse every interval. It can preserve the seed exactly while the harmony changes beneath it. The listener may hear a new relationship because the final A becomes stable over F after extending the opening C chord. Harmony can develop an idea without replacing it.

## Find motifs outside pitched melody

Platform Three's bass pattern, D on beat one and A on the “and” of three, is also a compact identity. A higher instrument can borrow its timing using E and F. The relationship is rhythmic even though the pitch intervals differ.

A lyric can supply a recurring stress pattern. An instrumental sound can supply a repeated attack and decay. These are useful motifs when they generate recognizable relationships across a song. Calling every small event a motif, however, can make the term too broad to explain anything.

## Keep development selective

A motif becomes restrictive if the writer believes every later note must be derived from it. A chorus can introduce a different focal idea. A bridge can contrast with both. The motif supplies connection where connection is useful; it does not prohibit fresh material.

The opposite failure is abandoning the seed before it has had a chance to establish itself. A new tune in every bar may create variety while leaving little for the listener to recognize. A brief recurring feature can connect a much wider span of material.

This pattern helps when improvisation produces many notes but few clear phrases, when a fragment lacks continuation, or when sections seem unrelated. It is less helpful if the song's character depends on long, unfolding melodic lines that resist small-unit repetition. Even there, recurring rhythm or contour may provide a subtler connection.

## Seven Nation Army: a motif does more than introduce

The White Stripes' [Seven Nation Army, official video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0J2QdDbelmY), begins with a compact low riff that remains an organizing feature after other parts enter. Its particular rhythm and contour make it recognizable; a generic instruction to “repeat something low” would not preserve its identity. When the arrangement gains weight, the listener can still recognize the figure rather than having to learn a completely new one.

The distinction from a finished melody is useful. A short riff can supply enough identity to start writing while leaving vocal phrasing, harmony, and larger contrast undecided. Porch Light's E–G–A seed does that on a smaller scale. An answering descent shares its rhythm without copying its pitch direction, giving the seed a next event rather than merely another loop. Seven Nation Army demonstrates the reach of a compact figure, not a requirement that every part of a song derive from one motif. The sound of the low riff also should not be used by itself to infer which instrument or studio process produced it.
