Elena knows that a teacher should not have read from her private notebook in front of the class. She can explain why it was wrong. Yet remembering the scene still brings the feeling that her only choices were to endure it silently or make herself more conspicuous by objecting.

A therapist offers an imagined possibility: somebody interrupts the reading, closes the notebook, and gives it back. Elena can picture the action, but the first version feels like another performance in front of an audience. What she wants is more particular. She wants the reading to stop without having to defend the value of what she wrote.

The difference begins there. Understanding the absence of protection has made a need clearer. Now the work concerns whether an experience of protection can become available in a form Elena can actually encounter, rather than merely agree would have been desirable.

## The difficulty this pattern addresses

Insight can reveal the organization of a problem while leaving a familiar emotional position intact. A person knows that they deserve respect but still feels without a voice when corrected. They recognize that a request is reasonable but experience asking as an act of imposition. They understand why receiving care is difficult and continue to find care inaccessible when offered.

An explanation can make the difficulty less bewildering. It does not necessarily supply a different experience. Knowing that silence once preserved safety is distinct from experiencing a refusal being heard. Naming an absent caring response is distinct from feeling care meet a particular hurt.

Repeated reassurance can miss this gap. The therapist says that the person is allowed to take time, make mistakes, or have needs. The person may agree while encountering the same urgency, shame, or loneliness. The statement has conveyed a useful idea, but its intended quality has not become available.

Sometimes the offer itself recreates the problem. Praise can feel like a new standard. Comfort can arrive before an injury has been acknowledged. A proposed empowering action can become an instruction to perform confidence. The therapist's generosity does not determine what the person receives.

There is also a danger in reproducing a painful situation with greater vividness but little new possibility. An imagined scene becomes more detailed, a critical voice more forceful, or an old account more convincing. The person experiences the familiar position again without access to protection, choice, company, or another response. More experiential work is not automatically different experiential work.

Make a New Experience Possible addresses the gap between recognizing what is missing and encountering something relevantly different. It includes receiving a response from another person and developing a response of one's own. It can involve an ordinary adjustment in the room, a new emotional position, an imagined intervention, or a symbolic enactment.

The scale can be small. Having a preference alter the distance between two chairs may be more significant than a powerful declaration of support. A person may briefly feel entitled to finish a sentence, or discover that an error can meet help rather than contempt. The experience earns its relevance through its fit with the actual difficulty.

## The core move

**Make room for a specific, fitting difference to be experienced, and follow what happens when the person encounters it.**

The difference might concern what is offered: acknowledgment, practical support, a boundary, protection, time, or a reliable response. It might concern what the person can do: object, ask, receive, rest, approach, or withdraw. It might concern what meets pain internally: tenderness, a protective response, or a less condemning view that becomes emotionally accessible.

New means newly available in this situation, not necessarily something the person has never experienced before. Someone may know how to be firm at work but lose access to that capacity with a sibling. A person who readily cares for others may encounter some of that care in relation to their own distress. The work can bring an existing possibility into a place where it has been difficult to access.

Possible matters as much as new. The therapist creates conditions and offers invitations; they cannot decide that the person has experienced protection, trust, or comfort. A fitting offer may remain too much, too unfamiliar, or simply unwelcome today. That response can clarify what needs to change in the encounter.

The experience can arise through different routes, whose status remains important.

| Route | What can become available | What remains distinct |
| --- | --- | --- |
| An actual relationship | A real response to a request, objection, mistake, or feeling. | This person's response does not predict everybody else's. |
| Bodily support or action | A different experience of distance, support, movement, or agency. | A posture does not prove a belief or rewrite a history. |
| An emotional response toward oneself | Care, protection, or another way of meeting particular pain. | Self-response does not replace every need for help from others. |
| Imagery or symbolic enactment | An experience of an intervention or possibility absent from a represented scene. | The representation does not change what historically happened. |

These routes can combine. An imagined intervention may help someone find protective words, while the actual therapist's response makes it possible to revise those words without embarrassment. A bodily adjustment may reveal that a generous offer of closeness is less useful than room to move.

A meaningful difference also need not be pleasant at first. Receiving acknowledgment can bring grief about how long it was absent. Discovering a right to object can bring anger. An honest limit may be more useful than reassurance because the person can finally understand the terms of the relationship. The aim is a fitting new possibility, rather than a required positive mood.

## How the experience unfolds

The movements below describe recurring features of the work. They are not a procedure for producing a corrective experience, and an encounter can stop at any point.

### Discover what would be different enough to matter

A general wish such as feeling supported leaves many possibilities open. One person needs practical assistance. Another wants their difficulty recognized before advice. Another needs a helper to remain interested while accepting a refusal. The same warm response can fit one concern and miss another.

The desired difference can emerge through dissatisfaction with an initial offer. “I don't need you to tell me it was fine. I need you to understand why it hurt.” That correction identifies something more particular than reassurance. The therapist can follow it without assuming that they already know the remedy.

Sometimes what is missing is outside the therapy relationship's capacity. Dependable housing, an accessible workplace, or a fair division of care cannot be supplied by an imagined supportive figure. The inquiry needs to acknowledge the practical absence, rather than redescribe it entirely as a difficulty receiving support.

### Introduce a concrete possibility

Something has to differ in the encounter, not only in the explanation. A question is answered more directly. A request changes what happens. An imagined figure intervenes. A person tries a supported position or gives an accessible caring response a voice. There is something available to respond to.

The proposal can be modest and clearly bounded. A therapist can agree to pause before interpreting without promising never to misunderstand. A person can explore one refusal without being required to become assertive in every relationship. A useful difference is often credible because its scope is clear.

The introduction should also make the kind of activity understandable. An imagined intervention is identified as imagination. An enactment has roles and an ending. A physical adjustment is collaborative and optional. Clarity about what is being offered helps preserve the person's ability to choose.

### Follow the experience of encountering it

An offer and its reception are separate events. A caring sentence can bring warmth, doubt, irritation, grief, or no noticeable response. A different distance may feel freeing or lonely. The imagined adult may seem helpful, intrusive, implausible, or beside the point.

The useful question concerns what actually changes, including whether the original difficulty remains. A person may admire the words while continuing to feel unheard. They may experience some protection but dislike the accompanying instruction to forgive. These differences help identify which part of the offer fits.

No particular bodily response is required. Tears, relaxation, a firmer voice, or a deep breath can accompany meaningful contact, but none independently establishes it. Some people describe an ordinary, quiet recognition: “I can see that I have a choice here.”

### Revise the conditions rather than demand a better reception

When the response does not fit the intention, the work can change. The wording may need to become less reassuring and more accurate. A protective figure may need to stop speaking on the person's behalf. A movement may need to become smaller, adapted, or imagined. The person may want no further offer.

Difficulty receiving something can be important material, but it should not automatically be assigned to the client's defenses. The offer may genuinely be intrusive, insufficient, or poorly timed. The therapist's contribution belongs in the inquiry.

A response of no can itself make a relevant difference when it is respected. That possibility is not a reason to design a deliberately poor offer. It is a reason to let feedback alter an ordinary clinical proposal.

### Allow the new response and its aftermath to become recognizable

If something useful becomes available, time can be given to its particular quality. The person may notice that protection does not require shouting, that a mistake can meet help, or that supported rest feels different from collapse. They can also notice what is still difficult.

The first feeling may be followed by grief, suspicion, or a wish to withdraw. These responses do not cancel the difference, and the difference does not cancel them. The work can accommodate mixed experience without rushing to a story of successful transformation.

Later, the person may consider whether and how this possibility belongs outside the session. An imagined boundary is different from stating it to an employer. An experience of being heard by this therapist is different from expecting every relative to listen. Carrying a possibility into life requires attention to actual relationships, opportunities, risks, and resources.

### How different approaches implement the move

The approaches below make different kinds of experience available through different therapeutic structures. Their grouping is an editorial synthesis. All accompanying encounters are fictional illustrations, not reports from the cited sources or instructions for conducting the methods.

#### Hakomi: explore a missing experience and the conditions of receiving

Hakomi gives this family a direct name: the missing experience. Kurtz describes making previously unavailable possibilities such as safety, support, or freedom experientially accessible. The Hakomi curriculum pairs missing experience with attention to the nourishment barrier, the difficulty of receiving something potentially helpful. The offered experience and the response to it are both relevant. [Kurtz's account](https://hakomi.com/a-little-history/), [Hakomi curriculum](https://hakomiinstitute.com/programs/curriculum/).

Imagine someone who becomes tense whenever they are invited to rest. They describe the tension as a fear of being lazy. An agreed statement about having done enough brings immediate irritation. The person hears a verdict on their productivity, even when the verdict is favorable.

A different possibility becomes clearer: rest without having to submit an account of work completed. The therapist stops trying to establish that the person has earned the break. They acknowledge tiredness and leave the decision about what to do with it to the person. For a moment, the person experiences being accompanied without being evaluated.

The meaningful difference is not a more convincing reassurance. It is a change in the terms of contact. Whether that can be received remains open; the person may still expect evaluation to return.

A contemporary Hakomi description emphasizes that missing experience concerns something unavailable in present experience, even where past learning is relevant. That distinction prevents the phrase from becoming a claim that a therapist can supply a literal replacement childhood or infer its facts from a current reaction. [Present-time account of missing experience](https://hakomi.ca/about/what-hakomi).

#### AEDP: make a different relational encounter available, including repair

AEDP emphasizes emotional work within an engaged therapeutic relationship. Jennifer Imming's account of repair describes attending to a rupture, recognizing the patient's experience, and acknowledging the therapist's contribution. The relevant new possibility may arise through how a difficulty is addressed in the actual relationship. [AEDP account of repair](https://aedpinstitute.org/journal/the-work-of-aedp/).

In a fictional encounter, a person describes an important disappointment. The therapist responds with an optimistic interpretation, and the person goes quiet. When the therapist checks, the person says that the optimism left them feeling responsible for demonstrating progress.

The therapist acknowledges moving too quickly and stops defending the interpretation. They invite the person to clarify what was missed and remain with the disappointment. The person is uncertain at first, then notices that disagreement has not led to an argument about whose understanding is correct.

The experience is actual and limited. This therapist has responded to this correction in this way. It does not establish that every misunderstanding will be repaired well, or that the person's other relationships will become more responsive. It provides one encounter whose difference can be explored.

Repair should not require creating a rupture on purpose or making the therapist's regret the center of attention. The contribution lies in responsiveness when a difficulty occurs. A person can remain angry or unconvinced while the therapist takes appropriate responsibility; reconciliation is not a performance owed in return.

#### Individual Emotion-Focused Therapy: develop a caring response that reaches pain

In individual Emotion-Focused Therapy, self-soothing can involve bringing care, tenderness, and comfort into contact with underlying anguish. The Greenberg Institute distinguishes this experiential task from coping strategies for immediate regulation. It describes several routes to a caring response, including an adult self, an imagined caring other, or compassion first accessible toward someone else. [EFT account of self-soothing](https://www.greenbergiseft.ca/self-soothing).

Imagine someone distressed after disappointing a friend. They can say that people make mistakes, but the phrase does little to reach their shame. When they imagine a younger cousin in the same situation, they readily feel concern and a wish to help with the repair.

They explore whether any of that quality is available toward themselves. The first sentence is too general: “You are a good person.” What reaches the painful position more closely is, “You can put this right without spending the night attacking yourself.” The caring response includes responsibility for what happened.

The person does not suddenly feel warm toward themselves. They notice that the wish to repair and the belief that they must suffer have become a little more distinguishable. The new experience is an accessible response to pain, rather than repetition of a compassionate sentence until it sounds convincing.

The source also recognizes that self-soothing can provoke objection when it feels like being left to provide everything oneself. That concern may require actual relational attention. This particular task should not replace legitimate needs for help, and its form need not involve a child image or parental voice.

#### Imagery rescripting: introduce a fitting intervention into a represented scene

Imagery rescripting deliberately changes an imagined scenario so that an unmet need can be addressed and its present emotional meaning explored. Arntz's updated protocol discusses both the imagined intervention and the person's experience of receiving it. The work is identified as active imagination; the historical event is not replaced with a claim that the new scene actually occurred. [Arntz's protocol account](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/302328587/Imagery_Rescripting.pdf).

In a fictional example, someone remembers being blamed for a sibling's behavior. They know the blame was unfair. In an imagined version, a responsible adult stops the accusation and speaks directly to the people making it.

Initially the intervention takes the child away from the room. The person says that leaving does not quite meet the need: they still imagine everyone believing the accusation. What matters is having responsibility placed accurately before they leave. The imagined adult addresses the unfair blame and then gives the child a choice about remaining nearby or going elsewhere.

That revision changes the quality of the scene. The person can encounter protection with acknowledgment of what was wrong. The imagined figure has not simply supplied a happier ending; the intervention has become more responsive to the experienced injury.

This illustration selects one organizing principle rather than reproducing a trained clinical protocol. The represented event's uncertainties remain intact; the new imagery is identified as constructed.

#### Sensorimotor Psychotherapy: explore a different bodily action or condition

Sensorimotor Psychotherapy uses collaborative experiments with bodily experience, including comfort, posture, relational distance, and resources. Ogden's teaching material emphasizes keeping clients in charge of physical interventions and finding manageable ways to make contact with the body. The relevant new experience can be a small variation in support or action, without an elaborate imagined scene. [Ogden's teaching material](https://sensorimotorpsychotherapy.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/Trauma-Dissociation-Body-Handout.pdf).

Imagine someone who habitually leans forward while listening, ready to respond before the other person has finished. They notice that this becomes tiring. An experiment with leaning back does not initially feel restful; it feels like abandoning the conversation.

They explore a more supported position that still permits engagement. The person discovers that their back can meet the chair while their attention remains with the therapist. A brief exchange confirms that they can ask for repetition and do not have to anticipate every word.

The useful difference is specific: support and participation have become possible together for a moment. Upright or relaxed posture is not inherently the goal. The person's body, comfort, access needs, and preferred way of engaging determine which variations are relevant.

The source also discusses how positive or neutral bodily experiences can themselves become difficult. A favorable intention therefore does not justify prolonging an experiment that is unwanted or disorganizing. The resulting observation might be a new resource, a clearer concern, or a decision that this route is not helpful today.

#### Psychodrama: create a represented encounter with a different response

Psychodrama can use role players, objects, and staged scenes to explore experiences and possibilities beyond an ordinary conversation. Giacomucci describes corrective scenes, strengths-based roles, subsequent role training, and closure. New experience can arise through what happens in an enacted relationship, while the scene remains a constructed representation. [Essentials of Psychodrama Practice](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-981-33-6342-7_13).

In a fictional group encounter, someone explores repeatedly losing their place when others speak over them. An auxiliary participant takes a supportive role in an agreed scene. The first intervention speaks on the person's behalf so forcefully that they feel displaced again.

The protagonist specifies a different support: someone asks the group to pause, then gives the floor back. With that response enacted, they can finish a short statement in their own voice. The support makes room for participation instead of taking over the whole exchange.

The scene offers an experience of being backed while retaining authorship. It does not predict that an actual family or workplace will behave similarly. Later role training may explore what the person can do in less cooperative circumstances, but that is a different task from receiving the supportive response in this scene.

Giacomucci also emphasizes closure and de-roling. Participants leave the roles they have represented and return to the actual group relationship. A supportive role does not create an ongoing obligation to serve as that figure outside the enactment. Maintaining that distinction helps keep the represented experience and the real relationships clear.

#### Comparing the implementations

| Approach | How a different experience is made available | What the person encounters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hakomi | A fitting offer, with attention to conditions that affect receiving. | A possibility such as support, freedom, or recognition in the present. |
| AEDP | Responsive emotional contact and attention to relational repair. | A real interaction in which their experience affects the therapist's response. |
| Individual Emotion-Focused Therapy | Access to a caring emotional position that meets particular pain. | A different emotional response toward the vulnerable experience. |
| Imagery rescripting | A deliberately changed intervention in an imagined scene. | Protection, agency, or care within a clearly identified representation. |
| Sensorimotor Psychotherapy | A collaborative variation in bodily action, support, or distance. | An available bodily and relational possibility that can be assessed directly. |
| Psychodrama | A staged encounter with defined roles and responses. | An enacted possibility followed by a return to the actual group. |

These approaches do more than tell the person that something different is possible. They arrange a context in which a difference can be encountered. What the encounter means, whether it fits, and what follows remain questions for the person and therapist to examine together.

## A worked example

The following fictional encounter returns to Elena. It illustrates the broader pattern through an imagined intervention, without reproducing a formal imagery-rescripting protocol.

Elena remembers a teacher reading from her notebook in front of classmates. She has discussed the event before and regards the action as a breach of privacy. Yet when she prepares to share unfinished writing now, a familiar feeling appears: once someone has access, she has no say in what happens next.

She would like to explore that difficulty, but she does not want to read the private passage aloud or reconstruct everything said in the classroom. The therapist agrees. The relevant material is already available through her description of the intrusion and the position she experienced. Further disclosure is not a condition of proceeding.

They discuss whether an imagined intervention might be useful. The distinction is explicit: they will be making a scene, not recovering a more accurate memory or pretending the past happened differently. Elena is interested in whether protection can become more than an idea, and she knows she can stop or alter the imagined version.

The therapist's first proposal is an adult who enters, stops the reading, and tells the class that Elena is a thoughtful writer. Elena dislikes it immediately. Praise leaves the audience in place and shifts the performance from humiliating her to admiring her. What she wanted was not a better judgment of the writing.

That objection identifies the missing condition. “Nobody needs to decide whether it was good enough to be private,” she says. The therapist acknowledges the distinction and drops the praise. The imagined adult closes the notebook and says that it belongs to Elena. The contents are not discussed.

Elena can picture the intervention, but the adult's arrival still feels too dramatic. She imagines everybody looking at her. They pause to clarify what matters: she wants the action stopped, and she also wants room to decide what happens afterward. Rescue that makes her more visible is only partly helpful.

The imagined scene changes again. The adult stands beside the desk, stops the reading directly, and places the closed notebook within Elena's reach. The adult then asks whether she wants to remain in the room, go outside with company, or have a moment without further attention.

Elena notices an unfamiliar possibility. She does not have to make the whole situation acceptable before choosing. She wants to take the notebook and sit somewhere quiet with someone nearby. She does not want to explain what she wrote.

The scene can accommodate that wish. The imagined companion remains available without asking to see the notebook. Elena says that this last part matters more than she expected. Protection includes not turning the recovery from the intrusion into another disclosure.

There is sadness as she imagines it. The sadness does not mean that the intervention failed to soothe her; she describes being more aware of how alone she had felt. At the same time, the new scene has a specific quality she can name: someone takes the boundary seriously without requiring a public case for it.

The therapist asks how much she wants to stay with that. Elena wants a short pause, then ordinary conversation. The imagined scene ends, and they return explicitly to the current room and relationship. The therapist does not interpret the image as proof that an adult was available at the time or could have intervened in exactly this way.

In discussing what happened, Elena distinguishes two experiences. She has often said that the teacher was wrong. Today she could briefly imagine the response she needed and experience having some say in it. That does not make the memory pleasant, and the grief about what was absent remains.

They also consider the actual session. Her objection to the praise had changed the therapist's proposal. The therapist had not required her to produce the private passage. Those are real features of this encounter, distinct from the imagined classroom intervention. They may carry meaning in their own right.

Near the end, Elena mentions a present dilemma: a friend has asked to read a draft. She does not decide that she should now agree. She recognizes a possible condition: she could share a section and ask that it not be passed on. Whether that friend will respect the request is an actual relational question, not something resolved by imagery.

For now, she wants to keep writing without treating the invitation as an urgent test. The new possibility is not unlimited openness. It is a more available sense that access can have terms and that privacy does not have to be earned through exceptional work.

The immediate outcome remains bounded. An imagined experience of protection became more fitting through correction; a real therapist respected those corrections; Elena identified a distinction she could use. A lasting change in the old expectation has not yet been established, and the exercise has not changed the historical event.

## Why this movement may help

One contribution is adding something that explanation alone cannot supply. A person can understand what a respectful response would look like while remaining unable to encounter it in relation to their own difficulty. A concrete relational action, a caring position, or a fitting imagined intervention may make that difference accessible in another way.

A second contribution is specificity about need. Elena's example shows why generic kindness can miss the point. Praise addresses unfavorable judgment; she needed protection of privacy regardless of judgment. Developing a fitting experience can refine the understanding of what was absent and what matters now.

A third possibility is expanded response capacity. Someone may discover a small available action: finishing a sentence, asking for distance, accepting help on explicit terms, or responding to a mistake without humiliation. The development can be useful before it becomes easy or general. The person has encountered an option, rather than only learned a rule about what they ought to do.

A fourth contribution is a different relationship to participation. In a collaborative encounter, the person can adjust the offer and influence what happens. The changes they request are part of the experience. When correction matters, receiving support need not require giving up authorship.

A fifth possibility is the availability of new information about relationships. A therapist responds to an objection without retaliation, or a represented scene helps distinguish care from control. Such an event can become material for further reflection and learning. Its relevance depends on the person's experience of the difference, not only the therapist's intention.

These are clinical rationales for the editorial pattern. They do not establish one mechanism across the approaches. A helpful moment can provide relief, a resource, or a new possibility without proving durable change in the response that brought someone to therapy.

Coherence Therapy makes a useful distinction here. Its account of juxtaposition holds that a corrective or welcome experience is not automatically an encounter with the particular emotional learning it would need to disconfirm. This article preserves that distinction without treating the approach's neural explanations as established by a successful moment. Making a new experience possible and bringing contrasting experiences into relationship are separable clinical moves. [CT discussion of corrective experience and juxtaposition](https://www.coherencetherapy.org/discover/reconsolidation-FAQ.htm).

There can also be value without a direct contradiction. A supported posture may make the session more comfortable. Company can make grief less lonely. A caring response can help someone undertake a needed repair. These developments do not have to be promoted into claims of complete transformation to deserve attention.

## Conditions, pitfalls, and limits

### Fit the experience to the actual difficulty

Warmth, praise, closeness, encouragement, and permission are not interchangeable. Someone may need acknowledgment of harm before comfort, accurate responsibility before reassurance, or room to choose before support can feel useful. The person can help distinguish these needs, including by finding an initial proposal unhelpful.

A supposedly corrective experience can reproduce the familiar difficulty when its terms are wrong. Speaking for someone may remove their voice. Praising independence may make help harder to request. Inviting relaxation may dismiss a practical obligation. The content and conditions of the offer need attention together.

### Keep the desired outcome open to correction

A person should not have to report comfort because a therapist has been kind. Gratitude, tears, a softened face, or a reassuring statement can coexist with discomfort or compliance. The work needs room for an ordinary account of what happened, including no change.

The therapist may also become invested in supplying what was missing. That investment can make feedback difficult. A proposal should remain small enough to revise or abandon, and the person's objection should not become a failure to receive healing.

### Preserve the distinction between representations and facts

Imagery and enactment can be emotionally meaningful while remaining constructed. A protective adult in a scene does not establish that someone was secretly protective in the past. An imagined apology does not show what an actual person believes. A role player's response is not a prediction of how the represented person would behave.

Arntz explicitly distinguishes rescripting as active imagination from installing a revised factual memory. This article keeps the same boundary. Original memory details should not be supplied by the therapist as if known, and newly imagined events should remain identified as such. [Discussion of memory and rescripting](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/302328587/Imagery_Rescripting.pdf).

The distinction also allows symbolic work to be useful without demanding literal belief. A person can know that an intervention did not occur and still find value in encountering its meaning in the present.

### Make support credible within real limits

An actual therapeutic relationship has time, role, and availability boundaries. Those limits can be acknowledged while care remains real. Exceptional promises or unclear arrangements may make support feel less dependable, particularly where earlier help carried hidden obligations.

A therapist cannot become an unrestricted parent, partner, rescuer, or substitute community. A represented figure can express an imagined possibility without assigning the therapist or a group member the corresponding role outside the task. The source and scope of the experience need to remain understandable.

### Include grief, anger, and difficulty receiving

A welcome difference can highlight an absence. Someone encounters protection and feels angry that it was not available earlier. They receive interest and become sad about years of loneliness. They find an inward caring response and object to having needed to develop it alone.

These reactions belong to the process. They should not be bypassed by returning immediately to the positive part or interpreting mixed feelings as resistance. The new possibility and the cost of its earlier absence can both matter.

Nor does the person have to welcome a particular offer. Sometimes the difficulty is the offer itself; sometimes it reveals a concern that needs support; sometimes there is no useful explanation. There is no obligation to keep trying until receiving becomes possible.

### Respect access, pacing, and preferred forms

Not everyone visualizes clearly, relates to parts, welcomes bodily attention, or finds enactment helpful. A person may prefer ordinary conversation, a concrete environmental adjustment, words without imagery, or no experiment. An approach should not define depth by the channels it happens to privilege.

Physical experiments need to fit pain, disability, sensory needs, and the person's preferences. An educational article is not sufficient training for touch, physical pressure, restraint, or intensive trauma work. The task can change or stop when the proposed route is uncomfortable or unhelpful.

### Keep inner experience connected to outer conditions

A new sense of agency cannot guarantee an employer's fairness. An experience of care cannot supply absent childcare. A self-supportive response does not make ongoing mistreatment acceptable. The work can inform practical choices while remaining clear about the conditions it cannot change by itself.

Sometimes the fitting new experience is a concrete difference in the environment: a reliable accommodation, a more equitable arrangement, or access to needed help. Therapy should be able to recognize that requirement rather than continually searching for an inward version of what is materially missing.

### Distinguish a possible response from a lasting change

Something new can be available once, available with support, or available in a specific context. These are meaningful but different observations. Whether it becomes accessible elsewhere needs to be discovered over time.

The person may also decide not to act immediately. Elena can recognize that access can have terms without sharing a draft that day. A demand to demonstrate change can make the new possibility into another performance requirement. Its value includes the person's freedom to decide when and where it belongs.

## Its place in the wider process

Make a New Experience Possible introduces or develops a fitting difference. The two inform each other: an initially unhelpful offer can clarify the need, while a clearer need can guide a more relevant offer.

[[support-what-protects|Support What Protects]] attends to what keeps an encounter manageable or preserves something important. Support may itself become the new experience, as when a boundary is taken seriously. It may also be necessary before another possibility can be approached. Neither move requires getting protection out of the way as quickly as possible.

[[give-experience-a-form|Give Experience a Form]] makes expression or representation available. This pattern concerns what becomes different within or through that form. Enacting a familiar criticism does not yet provide a caring response; an imagined scene can reproduce a painful position or introduce a fitting alternative. Conversely, an actual relational difference can occur without enactment.

[[follow-the-whole-wave|Follow the Whole Wave]] attends to the development of the encounter, including mixed feelings, pauses, and an ending. A new possibility may need time to be received, followed by sadness, uncertainty, or rest. More positive intensity is not necessarily the fitting continuation.

[[let-contrasting-experiences-meet|Let Contrasting Experiences Meet]] concerns bringing different experiences or forms of knowing into contact. This article concerns access to the new side of such a possible encounter. A caring moment can be a resource without yet changing the expectation that care will be withdrawn.

[[let-the-change-register|Let the Change Register]] attends to a difference once it is present. [[find-the-next-living-expression|Find the Next Living Expression]] concerns its possible continuation into action, relationship, language, or rest. Both depend on identifying what actually happened rather than assuming the intended experience was received.

## Expressions and sources

Make a New Experience Possible names an editorial family of moves rather than a treatment formally shared by all the approaches discussed. Their aims, task structures, and evidence bases differ. The examples are fictional, and their outcomes illustrate possibilities rather than establish effectiveness.

Ron Kurtz's [A Little History](https://hakomi.com/a-little-history/) and the Hakomi Institute's [curriculum](https://hakomiinstitute.com/programs/curriculum/) ground the account of missing experience and barriers to receiving. The [Vancouver Hakomi description](https://hakomi.ca/about/what-hakomi) emphasizes their present-time character. Their contribution is the particularity of what can be received, rather than reassurance alone.

Jennifer Imming's [The Work of AEDP](https://aedpinstitute.org/journal/the-work-of-aedp/) grounds the relational repair comparison. The fictional encounter here is not a reproduction of its published session. The [Greenberg Institute's account of self-soothing](https://www.greenbergiseft.ca/self-soothing) grounds the individual Emotion-Focused Therapy comparison, including different pathways to care and possible objections to self-soothing.

Arnoud Arntz's [2025 updated imagery-rescripting protocol](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/302328587/Imagery_Rescripting.pdf) grounds the account of imagined interventions, experienced needs, and the distinction from factual memory. Its organizing principle is selected here without reproducing the protocol.

Pat Ogden's [Trauma, Dissociation and the Body teaching material](https://sensorimotorpsychotherapy.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/Trauma-Dissociation-Body-Handout.pdf) grounds the Sensorimotor comparison involving manageable bodily experiments and the client's participation. Scott Giacomucci's [Essentials of Psychodrama Practice](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-981-33-6342-7_13) grounds the account of corrective scenes, role training, closure, and de-roling.

The Coherence Psychology Institute's [discussion of corrective experience and juxtaposition](https://www.coherencetherapy.org/discover/reconsolidation-FAQ.htm) supplies the distinction between a different experience and its relationship to an existing emotional expectation. The article does not infer reconsolidation, erasure of a learning, or permanent change from a welcome moment. Further clinical assessment and later experience are needed to understand what has changed and what remains.
