*Reintroduce earlier material in a later context that gives it renewed comic meaning.*

A detail or joke returns after intervening material has moved the audience elsewhere. Its reappearance rewards memory, but the stronger callback also gives the remembered material a fresh application or consequence.

## Example 1 · John Mulaney

A babysitting analogy returns at a teenage house party

Early in New in Town, Mulaney compares leaving children with a barely older babysitter to putting a horse in charge of a dog. Much later, when describing an unsupervised teenage party, he brings back the animal comparison: the teenagers are now the dogs, and their absent supervisors are the horses.

— John Mulaney

## Example 2 · Iliza Shlesinger

A first-date slight becomes the final breakup question

Shlesinger remembers a date who answered a question about his middle name without asking hers. She moves into an elaborate fantasy about the perfect breakup, with digressions about rain, appearances and staging. At its climax, the boyfriend insists he knows her; she challenges him to supply the middle name he never asked about.

— Iliza Shlesinger

## Example 3 · Tig Notaro

A familiar compliment returns as an imagined fan’s mechanical greeting

Notaro recounts repeatedly encountering Taylor Dayne and delivering the same carefully paced compliment about her voice. After several encounters and the story of a friend trying to film one, Notaro imagines Dayne reporting her renewed fan support to her manager. The familiar greeting returns, now as an imitation of Dayne’s strangely robotic fan.

— Tig Notaro

**The turn:** The repeated greeting first becomes a running gag. Its later return in an imagined report changes who is presenting it and how Notaro herself appears, turning the refrain into a callback with a new point of view.

## What makes the structure work

The return needs something to gain from the distance. Immediate follow-ups are usually tags; repeated wording alone may be a refrain. In these examples, notice what changes: the situation, the purpose of a question, or the speaker’s point of view. Keep both the original idea and its later application clear enough for the audience to make the connection.
