*Make separately filmed moments feel like one continuous experience.*

A character runs through a building, emerges onto a crowded street, and climbs into a moving vehicle. The camera stays with them. There is no apparent opportunity to stop, reset, change equipment, or replace an actor with a stunt performer.

That uninterrupted experience can make a scene unusually tense. We share the character’s route and wait through events alongside them. But filming the entire journey in one take may be impractical, dangerous, or unnecessarily restrictive.

**Hidden cuts let filmmakers preserve that experience while constructing it from separate recordings.** Performances can be selected, locations connected, and difficult action filmed in manageable sections. The audience experiences a continuous shot.

## The puzzle: a passing coat conceals a missing afternoon

Imagine a corridor sequence. The camera follows a woman. Someone crosses close to the lens, their dark coat briefly filling the picture. As they pass, we continue following her.

How could the first part have been filmed in the morning and the second after lunch?

The coat provides a moment when the audience cannot inspect the corridor or the woman’s position. The editor joins two recordings during that obstruction. If the movement before and after it agrees, the brief loss of visibility feels like an ordinary interruption of our view.

This is an **occlusion cut**: conceal the join behind something that blocks the camera. Darkness and rapid, blurred camera movements provide other opportunities. [Adobe’s introduction to invisible cuts](https://www.adobe.com/creativecloud/video/hub/ideas/what-is-an-invisible-cut-in-film.html) describes both obstruction and whip-pan methods.

The important ingredient is what survives across the interruption: a convincing sense that the same action is still unfolding.

## A hidden cut can be simple; a stitch can be elaborate
The terms overlap, but a useful distinction concerns how the transition is made.

A **hidden cut** might be a straightforward edit between two compatible frames. No digital effects are necessarily required.

An **invisible stitch** may construct the connection over several frames. Artists align the images, adjust timing, reshape small discrepancies, or replace parts of the scene. Different areas of the picture can change from one take to another at different moments.

For *1917*, visual effects supervisor Guillaume Rocheron described transitions ranging from simple blends to reconstructed three-dimensional scenes. Some joins transferred individual body parts at different times, rather than switching the entire image at once. [His production interview explains the range of approaches.](https://beforesandafters.com/2020/01/17/the-big-effects-moments-in-the-one-shot-1917/)

Consequently, there may be no single frame that contains “the cut.”

- **Block the view.** A coat, pillar, doorframe, or other foreground object covers the relevant part of the picture. The next recording takes over behind it.
- **Use rapid movement.** A whip pan turns recognizable scenery into streaks. Compatible direction, speed, brightness, and blur help two recordings connect.
- **Match a view closely.** Both recordings include an overlapping stretch of camera movement and action. Their images are aligned and blended.
- **Build a connecting passage.** Digital scenery, a digital character, or a reconstructed camera move bridges recordings that cannot meet directly.

These methods can work together. A foreground obstruction might conceal most of a join while digital work repairs the actor’s still-visible hand.

### Example: *Rope* (1948)—how does the camera outlast its film supply?

Alfred Hitchcock’s apartment thriller sustains unusually long passages of action. Yet the Technicolor camera could record only a limited length of film before reloading.

One solution was to move close to an actor’s back until the jacket dominated the image. A subsequent recording could begin against the same jacket and move away, continuing the scene.

The apparent continuity depended on choreography: the actor’s position, camera placement, and resumption of movement all had to cooperate. The obstruction supplied a forgiving place to connect them.

*Rope* also contains visible cuts; it should not be described as a film with every edit concealed. Its value as an example is the clarity of the underlying trick: **turn a necessary interruption in recording into a plausible interruption of sight.**

[American Cinematographer’s illustrated account](https://theasc.com/article/alfred-hitchcock-rope/) explains the recording constraint and the production’s elaborate coordination of actors, camera, furniture, and removable walls.

### Example: *Birdman* (2014)—how can a continuous camera move contain a night passing?

In one transition, the camera looks toward the skyline at night. Time accelerates, daylight arrives, and the movement eventually carries us into Riggan’s dressing room.

The camera seems to keep traveling, although the passage connects different times and photographic elements.

Rodeo FX constructed the transition using photographed scenery projected onto digital geometry. A virtual camera connected those elements, while changes in lighting and color carried the image through the passing hours. Elsewhere, the team adjusted timing and combined performance material to maintain the film’s rhythm.

The lesson extends beyond concealing a recording break: **an apparently continuous view can contain a substantial passage of story time.** Continuous camera movement does not guarantee continuous time.

[FXGuide’s interview with visual effects supervisor Ara Khanikian](https://www.fxguide.com/fxfeatured/rodeo-helps-birdman-soar-with-invisible-fx/) explains the skyline transition, performance adjustments, and other invisible work, with production imagery.

### Example: *1917* (2019)—how can a plane crash and its aftermath happen beside the actors without interruption?

Two soldiers watch an aircraft approach and crash nearby. They then move toward the wreck to rescue its pilot.

The sequence combines several kinds of material: a digital aircraft, a separately filmed practical crash, footage of the actors, and another recording with the wreck arranged on location. Rocheron explained that the aftermath was dressed overnight before filming continued.

The construction allows an apparent single event to include an effects shoot and a substantial reset. [Computer Graphics World’s interview details the crash and its transitions.](https://www.cgw.com/Press-Center/In-Focus/2020/Stunning-Visual-Effects-in-1917.aspx)

Matching remained essential. Roger Deakins described using playback of the selected preceding take to reproduce actor positions, distance from the lens, and camera speed when beginning the next section. [His cinematography interview explains that preparation.](https://britishcinematographer.co.uk/exclusive-interview-roger-deakins-cbe-bsc-asc-on-1917-bc97/)

The film also includes a deliberate cut to black and a time jump. Its concealed joins support extended continuous passages; the whole story is not an uninterrupted real-time recording. Editor Lee Smith discusses both that explicit break and the extensive editorial work behind the illusion. [Read his interview.](https://awardswatch.com/interview-film-editor-lee-smith-on-making-1917-look-seamless/)

## What has to agree across the seam?
Matching the last frame of one recording to the first frame of another is only part of the problem. The movement through those frames must also feel continuous.

A walking actor cannot abruptly change stride. A camera cannot appear to lose momentum without a reason. A nearby doorway cannot suddenly shift against the distant background because the second camera position is wrong.

Lighting, focus, motion blur, clothing, props, and background action all provide evidence of continuity. Sound provides another layer: footsteps, dialogue, ambience, and reverberation should carry naturally through the join.

An obstruction makes some differences temporarily unavailable for inspection. It does not excuse a conspicuous change immediately afterward.

## Why planning matters
A useful transition needs material on both sides. Filmmakers often record overlapping action and camera movement so the editor has a stretch in which to find the best connection.

That overlap also gives effects artists room to adjust timing and blend compatible moments. Rehearsal helps establish repeatable positions and movement without requiring every shot to use computerized motion control.

The cost is that decisions become interdependent. Selecting one performance may constrain the beginning of the next section. A change in camera speed can affect the whole transition.

## When the trick starts to show
A camera that repeatedly dives into dark jackets or swings toward empty walls can make viewers anticipate every join. The concealment becomes a recognizable gesture.

The strongest transition belongs naturally to the scene: someone passes through a crowded space, the camera follows a motivated turn, or the action continues through a carefully matched blend.

Ordinary editing can also serve a scene better. An apparent continuous shot needs rhythm, emphasis, and purposeful movement just as much as a conventionally edited sequence.

## Connections to other patterns

**[Match cuts](/pattern/film-match-cuts)** connect images through shared shape, position, or action; a hidden join uses matching to sustain the impression of one shot.

**[Creative geography](/pattern/film-creative-geography)** constructs a convincing route from separate places. Invisible stitching can make that route appear uninterrupted.

**[Substitution splices](/pattern/film-substitution-splices)** use a recording break to change what is in front of the camera. Hidden cuts use a related opportunity to keep a larger action flowing.

Across all these variations, the reusable principle is precise: **design a transition that preserves the viewer’s expectations about movement, space, and action—even while the underlying recording changes.**
