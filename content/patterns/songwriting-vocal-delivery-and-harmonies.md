**Pattern 47 of 49 · Part 8: Arrange, Revise, and Finish**

**Use when:** The written melody and words work, but their meaning disappears in performance or under continuous vocal layers.

Two singers perform the same notes and words. One clips the last word and leaves a pause. The other carries its vowel through the bar line. The written melody is recognizable in both, yet the phrase communicates differently. Vocal delivery is part of how a song's meaning becomes audible.

## The move

Choose delivery and backing voices around the lead sentence's meaning, adding layers selectively.

Record Porch Light's title once with a sustained final vowel and once with an earlier release. Listen to the request each delivery suggests before adding backing parts. Compare a unison double with a separate harmony on only the last two chorus lines. Keep consonants and entrances clear enough for the sentence to remain intelligible. If the unlayered line still fails in several straightforward performances, return to its words or rhythm; additional takes and harmonies should not have to conceal the same writing problem.

A harmony is a new pitch relationship that must fit the chords and performer; it is not simply a double made more elaborate.

## Locate the center of a sung event

Delivery includes timing, articulation, emphasis, dynamics, tone, and phrase endings. Backing vocals add relationships through repeated performances, different pitches, or separate responses. These decisions deserve the same specificity as chord and melody choices.

A consonant may begin before the vowel that carries a pitch. In “light,” the opening consonant and final consonant frame a sustained vowel. A recording's earliest sound is therefore not always the perceptual center of the note. Aligning every sound mechanically can make language less clear.

Porch Light's title spans two bars. A restrained version lets the vowel in “on” continue almost to the boundary. A more direct version releases it earlier and leaves a gap. The former connects onward; the latter marks the request more firmly. The context determines which reading is convincing.

A singer can also emphasize different words without changing the written notes. Leaning into “I” in “I can make it home” can underline agency. Emphasizing “home” can foreground the destination. Those are interpretations to compare, not fixed meanings encoded by one volume change.

## Distinguish a double from a harmony

A vocal double is another performance of essentially the same line, often at the same pitch or an octave. Its small differences can create thickness or width. A harmony uses different simultaneous pitches. A response is a separate phrase that answers or comments on the lead.

These layers serve different jobs. A double can strengthen a title without adding a new melodic line. A harmony can change the combined chord color. A response can divide the lyric into conversational roles. Adding all three continuously may obscure the focal voice.

The exact timing of consonants matters when words are doubled. Slight vowel differences can be attractive, while two prominent final consonants may sound like an unintended extra attack. The useful decision depends on the intended degree of unity.

## Build a harmony against the chords

An original two-note example places lead E4 over C and a backing G4 above it. Both belong to the C triad. When the harmony changes, continuing a fixed interval without checking the resulting notes can create a different sonority than intended.

Suppose a lead A4 appears over F. A backing C5 fits the triad, but a higher part may also compete with the lead's prominence. A lower harmony or a selective entrance can preserve the lead's place. “Always sing a third above” is too simple for a whole arrangement.

Porch Light's final chorus can add one backing voice only on the last two lines. The new entrance marks the return without changing every earlier presentation. Another version uses a quiet unison response to the title. These are original arrangement alternatives preserved as choices rather than layered together by default.

## Write for the intended performer

A comfortable one-off high note may become tiring when repeated through a long chorus. A consonant-heavy phrase can demand more clarity at a brisk tempo. The melody's tessitura, breath spaces, and vowel choices should be heard in a real performance.

A demo can communicate the intended phrase without imitating a famous singer's timbre. An ordinary clear voice is enough to reveal stress, duration, and melody. A technically impressive performance that changes the phrase beyond recognition may be less useful for evaluating the writing.

## Cellophane: delivery is already an arrangement decision

FKA twigs' [cellophane, official video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YkLjqFpBh84), places a highly exposed vocal against a restrained piano setting before the surrounding texture develops. Soft attacks, sustained vowels, shifts of intensity, and the ends of phrases carry information that a lyric sheet cannot preserve. A repeated thought can sound newly vulnerable or insistent because its delivery has changed.

The writing lesson does not require reproducing the singer's range or technique. Porch Light's title can become a different request when its final vowel is held, released early, or approached with more space. That comparison should be heard before a double or harmony is added. Cellophane also helps separate an expressive lead from the mere quantity of vocal layers: adding more voices is not automatically what makes a sentence communicate. This is an interpretation of a particular performed setting, not a claim about the artist's private intention or an instruction for difficult vocal production. The intended performer needs a version whose words, register, and phrasing they can deliver clearly and comfortably.

## Let delivery reveal the draft

If every performance struggles with the same line, the words or melody may need revision. If the line works when sung simply but disappears under doubles and harmony, the arrangement may need simplification. Distinguishing those cases prevents endless retakes from covering a compositional problem.

The Porch Light delivery and harmony comparisons are original. Berklee's [Natural Music in Language](https://www.berklee.edu/berklee-now/news/berklee-riffs-natural-music-in-language) supports the underlying attention to speech rhythm and deliberate emphasis.
