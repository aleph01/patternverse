## 1. A recognizable situation

Your nine-year-old asks to stay home. Eventually, they explain: several classmates imitate their voice, hide their belongings, and laugh when they get upset.

“Have you told the teacher?”

“Yes. They said to ignore it. Please don’t tell anyone—you’ll make it worse.”

You want to protect your child, but they are frightened of what adult involvement might bring.

**The task is to make the child safer while preserving their trust and involvement.** Coping skills can help, but stopping bullying cannot depend on the targeted child finding the perfect response.

## 2. What might be happening

Start with the actions and their effects. The word “teasing” can cover very different experiences.

**Mutually enjoyable joking.** Both children participate freely, and either can stop without punishment or humiliation. Laughter alone is insufficient evidence of enjoyment: ask privately how the child experiences it.

**An insensitive remark or misunderstanding.** A child may say something hurtful without anticipating its effect. What happens afterward matters: do they listen, stop, and make a change?

**An ordinary disagreement.** Children with reasonably equal power may argue over rules, possessions, or plans. They may need help resolving the issue without mistreating each other.

**Bullying.** This involves unwanted aggression, unequal power, and repetition or the potential for repetition. Power can come from physical strength, popularity, numbers, access to embarrassing information, or the ability to exclude someone. Bullying includes relational and online behavior as well as physical attacks. [StopBullying.gov’s definition](https://www.stopbullying.gov/bullying/what-is-bullying)

**Discriminatory harassment or another serious incident.** Slurs, sexual humiliation, threats, or an assault require attention even before anyone establishes a repeated pattern.

These distinctions guide the response; they should not become hurdles a child must clear to receive protection.

Ask what happens, where, who participates or watches, whether the child can leave, and what happened when they sought help. Investigate whether adults see only the final reaction rather than the preceding behavior.

Avoid assuming a motive such as jealousy, insecurity, or a troubled home. Understanding the other child may eventually inform intervention, but protection does not depend on explaining their psychology.

## 3. How development changes the picture

### Preschool and early childhood: adults need to observe and intervene directly

Young children may struggle to describe a pattern or distinguish an accident from deliberate targeting. They may show distress through avoiding an activity, clinging, aggression, or changes in play.

Ask educators to observe specific situations. Repeated grabbing, exclusion, or frightening behavior needs adult interruption and teaching, even when children’s understanding of intent is still developing. Bullying-like patterns can begin in preschool; young age does not remove the need for protection. [ADL’s early-childhood guidance](https://nynj.adl.org/sites/default/files/What-Can-I-Do-About-Bullying-Among-Young-Children.pdf)

Do not expect a preschooler to deliver an assertive speech or independently manage a group.

### Primary school: make the route to help concrete

Children can increasingly explain incidents and practice responses. They still need adults to arrange accessible help.

“Tell a teacher” is incomplete. Identify whom to approach, where that person will be, and what happens if the first adult is unavailable or dismisses the report. A child who freezes may need a gesture, written note, or another way to signal distress.

### Adolescence: involve the young person in a discreet plan

Reputation, privacy, friendship groups, and online circulation can make disclosure feel risky. Ask what they fear will happen if adults intervene.

Discuss who needs information and how it will be shared. Offer choices within the protection you must provide. Do not promise secrecy when there is serious danger; explain your next step and involve them wherever possible.

Most intervention research concerns school-age children, with substantial evidence from primary and middle schools. Programs should not be assumed equally effective across preschool, early adolescence, and later adolescence. A 2024 review found that evidence varied across programs and age groups. [George and colleagues, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ps.20230541)

## 4. What needs balancing

**The child’s autonomy and adults’ responsibility for safety.** Their concerns about intervention deserve careful planning. They should not have to manage serious harm alone.

**Immediate protection and continued participation.** A safer seating arrangement may help. Requiring the targeted child to abandon every activity can leave them paying the cost of other people’s behavior.

**Coping and accountability.** Learning how to seek help or leave a situation can expand the child’s options. Responsibility for bullying remains with those doing it and the adults responsible for responding.

**Careful investigation and timely action.** Adults may need several accounts. They can increase supervision and provide a safe contact while investigating.

**Practical constraints.** Parents cannot supervise the school day, and schools have limited staff. A useful plan names realistic responsibilities rather than relying on constant parental availability or vague promises to “keep an eye on things.”

## 5. Possible responses

### A. Make disclosure useful and safe

**Fits:** the child tells you something happened, or you notice concerning changes.

Listen before asking a long sequence of questions. Begin with clear statements:

> “I’m glad you told me. You don’t deserve to be treated that way. We’ll work out what help is needed.”

Ask about immediate safety: injuries, threats, being prevented from leaving, or fear about the next encounter. Then collect enough detail to guide action. Record the child’s words, dates, locations, and previous reports without repeatedly interrogating them.

If they say, “Don’t tell,” explore the concern: public embarrassment, retaliation, punishment for their own reaction, or a previous adult failure. Explain what you propose to do and when you will update them.

Listening and nonjudgmental inquiry are professional recommendations. They support assessment and trust; they are not independently proven techniques for stopping bullying. [American Academy of Pediatrics guidance](https://www.healthychildren.org/english/safety-prevention/at-play/pages/bullying-its-not-ok.aspx)

If the child has retaliated, address that action separately. It does not cancel their need for protection.

### B. Obtain a specific adult protection plan

**Fits:** repeated targeting, unequal power, serious harassment, or unsuccessful previous reports.

Contact the responsible teacher, school leader, safeguarding contact, coach, or activity organizer. Describe actions and their impact rather than relying only on the label “bullying.”

Ask for a plan that answers:

- What protection begins now, including at the locations where incidents happen?

- Who can the child approach, and what will that adult do?

- How will staff address those participating and reduce opportunities for retaliation?

- When will you review whether the plan is working?

Summarize the agreement in writing. If the response is ineffective, escalate through the organization’s reporting process with the existing record.

Official guidance recommends coordinated protection, minimizing unnecessary disruption to the targeted child, and persistent follow-up. It cautions against parents directly confronting the other family, which can worsen matters. [StopBullying.gov: Supporting Children Involved](https://www.stopbullying.gov/prevention/support-the-children-involved)

There is causal evidence for coordinated school intervention. A meta-analysis of 69 randomized trials, involving approximately 112,000 students aged four to seventeen, found small average reductions in bullying and improvements in mental health. Benefits persisted over the available follow-up, averaging roughly seven months; that does not establish permanent effects. [Fraguas and colleagues, 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33136156/)

These findings concern complete programs. They do not establish that an assembly, punishment, or single supervision change works alone. The 2024 review found the strongest evidence for approaches operating across school, classroom, and individual levels. [George and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ps.20230541)

### C. Expand the child’s options and support recovery

**Fits:** alongside protective action, when the child wants help handling encounters or their aftermath.

Practice a small number of options appropriate to the situation:

- A brief “Stop” or “Don’t call me that,” when speaking feels safe.

- Leaving toward an identified adult or supervised place.

- Reporting what happened, including when an earlier report did not help.

- Staying near supportive peers where that is comfortable and feasible.

They may need to skip the verbal response and get help immediately. Freezing, crying, or failing to sound confident does not make the bullying their fault.

Outside those encounters, protect access to enjoyable activities and relationships. Ask whether they want company, distraction, or conversation. Avoid making every evening a bullying debrief.

One randomized trial of **Resilience Triple P**, involving 111 families with children aged six to twelve experiencing chronic bullying, found improvements in several victimization, distress, and relationship measures compared with an assessment-control group. Both groups improved over time. The program combined parenting support and child social-emotional training; its developers conducted the study. This is promising package-level evidence, not proof that assertiveness practice alone stops victimization, or that the results apply equally to teenagers. [Healy and Sanders, 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25311286/)

Additional therapeutic support can address distress while adults continue working to stop exposure.

### D. Address online harm while preserving supportive contact

**Fits:** bullying through messages, gaming, social media, or shared content.

Help record abusive text, dates, account names, and links. Report through the platform and involve the school when classmates or school relationships are involved. Use blocking, muting, and privacy controls as appropriate.

For sexual images involving children, seek specialist reporting guidance rather than downloading or forwarding them.

An adult can handle documentation so the child does not repeatedly reread harmful material. Public replies and retaliatory posts can extend the audience; discuss safer alternatives.

Australia’s eSafety Commissioner recommends these steps and cautions against automatically confiscating devices, which may also remove supportive relationships. This is professional safety guidance, rather than experimental evidence for each action. Reporting routes vary by country. [eSafety’s parent guide](https://www.esafety.gov.au/parents/issues-and-advice/cyberbullying)

Blocking one account may not address screenshots, replacement accounts, or in-person behavior. Check what continues after the technical changes.

## 6. Examples in practice

**A four-year-old is repeatedly frightened by a “game.”**

Several children chase your child, surround them, and laugh when they cry. Staff describe it as ordinary chasing.

You ask whether your child chooses to participate, can stop it, and can leave. The immediate response is closer supervision and interruption when participation is unwanted. Teaching everyone how to invite, refuse, and stop play is an adult task. The young child need not establish someone else’s malicious intent.

**A nine-year-old tries the advised response, and it fails.**

Your child says “Stop,” walks away, and tells a supervisor. The group follows and calls them a tattletale.

You acknowledge that they used the agreed plan. The next step is revising adult protection: staff know the location, respond immediately to the follow-up targeting, and check privately with your child.

More rehearsal would leave the failed adult response unchanged. Your child may remain afraid even after supervision improves.

**A fifteen-year-old fears losing their social world.**

A group circulates humiliating edits of their photograph. They beg you not to remove their phone.

Together, you identify supportive contacts, document the harmful posts, report them, and agree who at school needs to know. You arrange check-ins that do not publicly identify your child as the complainant.

This cannot guarantee that nobody guesses who reported. Being honest about that limit allows you to plan for retaliation rather than promising confidentiality you cannot ensure.

These examples are practical synthesis, not separately tested interventions.

## 7. Common mistakes and limitations

**Debating the label while harm continues.** A school may need time to classify an incident. Protection can begin meanwhile.

**“Just ignore it.”** Disengaging from a particular interaction may help a child leave safely. Adults ignoring repeated targeting abandons their responsibility.

**Requiring confidence as the solution.** Eye contact, a firm voice, or a witty answer cannot reliably overcome unequal power. They may also be inaccessible or unsafe for a particular child.

**Treating bullying as an equal-blame dispute.** Forced mediation or an immediate reconciliation meeting can expose the child to further pressure. Official guidance distinguishes bullying from conflict between people with equal power. [StopBullying.gov guidance](https://www.stopbullying.gov/prevention/support-the-children-involved)

**Measuring success by punishment or an apology.** Neither establishes that incidents, retaliation, or fear have stopped. Consequences need accompanying supervision and follow-up.

**Changing the child to remove the target.** A haircut, concealed interest, or different manner of speaking might become the family’s desperate strategy. Discuss any choices the child wants, while making clear that their characteristics do not justify mistreatment.

**Assuming “evidence-based” means effective for everyone.** Programs vary in implementation and results. Average reductions across schools leave some children needing additional intervention. Evidence for preventing bullying is also stronger than evidence identifying the best treatment for every child’s lasting distress.

## 8. What to observe over time

Check both exposure and recovery:

- Are incidents, threats, and retaliation decreasing?

- Can the child access help when needed?

- Has targeting moved to another location or platform?

- Can they participate safely without constant avoidance?

- Are sleep, appetite, concentration, and enjoyment recovering?

- Do they trust that reporting leads to useful action?

Ask privately and concretely. “Anything else happen?” may miss exclusion or fear; “What happened at lunch this week?” provides a clearer starting point. Fewer reports can mean improvement, discouragement, or fear of telling.

Bullying is associated with emotional difficulties, but association alone cannot establish causation. A meta-analysis of 16 quasi-experimental studies—using methods such as twin comparisons and matching—found smaller remaining effects consistent with bullying contributing to distress, especially anxiety and depression. These designs cannot remove every source of bias, and effects tended to weaken over time. Long-term harm is not inevitable. [Schoeler and colleagues, 2018](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10056348/)

Consult a healthcare or mental health professional when distress persists, school attendance deteriorates, physical complaints recur, or the child withdraws from usual activities. Such signs can have several causes and warrant assessment. [AAP guidance](https://www.healthychildren.org/english/safety-prevention/at-play/pages/bullying-its-not-ok.aspx)

Serious injury, credible immediate threats, or concerns about self-harm require prompt professional help; immediate danger requires emergency assistance.
