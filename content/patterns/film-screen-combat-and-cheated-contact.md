*Make the audience feel an impact that the performers do not have to deliver.*

A story needs a punch to feel devastating. One character must seem powerful, another suddenly vulnerable. The audience needs to understand who struck whom, how hard, and what changed.

Simply photographing two people moving their arms is not enough. The movement has to become an apparent collision.

Screen combat distributes that job across choreography, camera position, performance, editing, and sound. **Cheated contact** is the part of the illusion in which an attack appears to connect although the striking limb or prop misses its apparent target.

The aim is to create convincing evidence of force while controlling the physical event being filmed.

Imagine a fist passing in front of someone’s face, separated from it in depth.

From one position, the camera sees an obvious gap. From another, the fist crosses the face’s outline. For a moment, the two shapes overlap in the picture even though they do not meet in space.

At that moment, the other performer reacts. A synchronized impact sound supplies another piece of evidence.

The audience reads the combination as cause and effect: the fist arrived; the head moved; something hit.

The camera has not recorded a collision. It has recorded the visual relationships from which we infer one.

## Three contributions make the blow readable

| Contribution | What it supplies |
|---|---|
| The attack | Direction, intention, and apparent arrival |
| The reaction | A visible consequence: recoil, loss of balance, interruption |
| The soundtrack | The apparent instant and character of impact |

Camera position determines how those contributions line up. Editing determines which moments survive and how they connect.

Fight coordinator Kevin Inouye describes non-contact hits in these terms: an approaching attack, apparent overlap, and an appropriate response. He stresses that angle and timing must agree with the chosen camera position. [His explanation for camera and editing crews](https://studentfilmmakers.com/deputizing-the-fight-coordinator-posse/).

### Example: Creed III—put the camera where the contact can disappear

In *Creed III* (2023), cinematographer Kramer Morgenthau specifically identifies over-the-shoulder angles as a way to conceal the point of contact without the performers actually hitting one another.

The camera team developed the fights alongside the stunt department through filmed rehearsals. The viewpoint was part of the choreography, rather than something selected after the fighting had been designed. [American Cinematographer’s production account](https://theasc.com/article/creed-iii-jordan-morgenthau/).

This makes screen fighting a performance for a particular view. A convincing miss from one position can become an obvious miss from another.

Moving the camera therefore changes more than the composition. It changes the apparent relationship between the attack and its target.

## The person being hit helps create the hit
The attacking performer supplies only half the event. The receiving performer makes the force seem to continue through another body.

A reaction that begins too early can look like anticipation. One that arrives too late can feel disconnected from the swing. A huge recoil after a seemingly slight movement can suggest a different kind of impact from the one the scene intended.

The direction matters too. The audience reads movement as a consequence of what came before it. Compatible trajectories help two separately controlled actions become one apparent collision.

This is why a screen fight can demand expressive movement that differs from ordinary fighting. The action must communicate intention, effort, and consequence clearly enough to be read through the camera.

### Example: John Wick—why doesn’t a convincing fight require constant cutting?


![The camera crew works inside the glass-office set, viewed from outside the scenery.](https://theasc.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/John-Wick-12-JW3_D36_12347.jpg)

*John Wick: Chapter 3 – Parabellum (2019) — Behind-the-scenes photograph.* A camera crew works inside the glass-office set. This view from outside the scenery reveals the working space around the choreographed action, rather than a finished film frame. [Image source](https://theasc.com/article/john-wick-chapter-3-slayin-in-the-rain/). Credit: Niko Tavernise / Lionsgate; published by American Cinematographer.


*John Wick: Chapter 3 – Parabellum* (2019) frequently lets us see sustained exchanges. Its cinematographers and director describe coordinating performers, camera movement, and lighting like a dance, with wide views that expose the choreography to inspection. [Production interview](https://theasc.com/article/john-wick-chapter-3-slayin-in-the-rain/).

That approach gives the illusion a different test. Instead of constructing every impact from fragments, the filmmakers must maintain readable relationships across an unfolding passage.

Editor Evan Schiff explains that Chad Stahelski generally dislikes cutting halfway through a punch. Schiff looks for brief natural pauses in the choreography when changing angles. He also describes building substantial effects tracks—including punches and kicks—during the edit. [Schiff’s interview](https://www.provideocoalition.com/aotc-jw3/).

The reusable lesson is that **editing supports a chosen style of combat**. A cut can connect preparation to consequence, adjust timing, or change emphasis. It need not conceal every apparent impact.

A long take, meanwhile, still contains choreography, selected viewpoints, and constructed sound. Continuous photography does not make the fight an uncontrolled physical contest.

### Example: Raiders of the Lost Ark—where does the weight of a punch come from?


![Harrison Ford and Pat Roach face one another during the airfield fight.](https://m.media-amazon.com/images/M/MV5BODM0ZDlhZGUtODE3Yi00MDgwLWEwYjgtYzg1M2VkNWE4MWM1XkEyXkFqcGc%40._V1_.jpg)

*Raiders of the Lost Ark (1981) — Film frame.* Harrison Ford and Pat Roach in the airfield fight. The frame establishes the physical encounter; the apparent weight of its blows also depends on movement, reactions, editing, and the separately constructed soundtrack. [Image source](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt0082971/mediaviewer/rm195672833). [Technical account](https://www.comingsoon.net/extras/news/94884-ben-burtt-and-dennis-muren-on-indiana-jones-the-complete-adventures). Credit: Lucasfilm / Paramount film frame, reproduced by IMDb.


Indiana Jones’s fights in *Raiders of the Lost Ark* (1981) have a distinctive, emphatic sound. The blows seem heavier because the soundtrack gives each one a strong audible arrival.

Sound designer Ben Burtt explains that the team built a library of impacts using materials including leather jackets and sports equipment. Those recordings became the apparent sounds of bodies being struck. [Burtt’s firsthand account](https://www.comingsoon.net/extras/news/94884-ben-burtt-and-dennis-muren-on-indiana-jones-the-complete-adventures).

The sound does more than increase volume. Its onset establishes an instant of contact; its texture suggests the kind of impact; its relationship to breath, clothing, and subsequent movement helps the event occupy the scene.

The apparent blow can therefore combine movement photographed in one place with sound recorded from something entirely different.

Sound reinforces the visual construction, although an exposed gap or badly mismatched reaction can still break it.

## Cheated contact is one tool within screen combat

Some screen action includes controlled contact with padded areas, specialized props, or protected costumes. Other moments rely on separation, camera alignment, doubles, wires, or digital additions.

For *John Wick: Chapter 4* (2023), stunt coordinators describe combining padded tactical costumes with softened nunchuck props for the Osaka action. [Their account for The Credits](https://www.motionpictures.org/2023/03/john-wick-chapter-4-stunt-coordinators-on-how-they-crafted-the-craziest-wick-yet/).

It would therefore be misleading to claim that every movie blow misses, or that everything visible in a fight uses the same method. The construction can change from moment to moment.

The relevant distinction is between **the force the story asks us to believe in and the physical action arranged to represent it**.

## What exposes the illusion?
An unsuitable angle reveals empty space between attack and target. An incompatible reaction makes the movement feel voluntary rather than caused. A sound that arrives before or after the apparent impact separates the event into unrelated parts.

Too much concealment can create a different problem. If framing and cutting prevent us from understanding the exchange, the audience may lose track of position, intention, and advantage.

Clarity also serves the drama. A fight becomes more involving when we can recognize a failed attack, a successful defense, growing exhaustion, or a change in tactics.

A succession of spectacular impacts is not automatically a readable struggle.

## When the pattern is useful
Cheated contact is useful whenever a scene needs an apparent strike without that strike being physically delivered to its visible target. The broader screen-combat pattern organizes those moments into character, rhythm, and changing power.

Its neighboring techniques solve additional parts of the event. **[Wire-assisted stunts](/pattern/film-wire-assisted-stunts)** extend a reaction beyond ordinary movement. **[Breakaway props](/pattern/film-breakaway-props)** provide prepared destruction. **[Digital doubles and face replacement](/pattern/film-digital-doubles-and-face-replacement)** connect stunt performance to character identity. **[Hidden cuts](/pattern/film-hidden-cuts-and-invisible-stitching)** can join separate passages into an apparently continuous exchange.

The central illusion remains a coordinated relationship: an action appears to arrive, another body appears to receive it, and the film makes us experience the connection.
