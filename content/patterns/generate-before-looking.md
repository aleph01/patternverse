An answer often looks obvious after it has been revealed. Once you know the ending of a story, the result of an experiment, or the meaning of an unfamiliar word, it becomes difficult to recover the uncertainty that existed beforehand. The solution seems more predictable than it really was.

Generate Before Looking preserves that uncertainty for a moment. Before seeing the answer, make a prediction, propose an explanation, attempt the problem, or guess what will happen next.

A person learning wine tasting might describe what they smell before reading the tasting notes. Someone watching a documentary might predict why an archaeological settlement was abandoned before hearing the researchers' interpretation. A reader encountering an unfamiliar word might infer its meaning from context before reaching for a dictionary.

The attempt may be wrong, and that is not a defect. Once you have generated an expectation, the actual answer has something to collide with. The difference becomes meaningful: Why did I expect this? Which clue did I miss? What assumption led me in the wrong direction?

This changes the learner's relationship to the answer. Instead of information simply arriving, it becomes feedback on a model you had already begun to construct.

The attempt does not need to be long. One of the easiest ways to misuse this pattern is to turn every lesson into a heroic struggle. Spending thirty frustrating minutes trying to reinvent something that could have been explained clearly in five is not automatically better learning. Often a brief, genuine attempt is enough.

The pattern is especially useful in domains where hindsight is powerful. Historical events look inevitable once we know what happened. Scientific findings seem obvious once we hear the result. Artistic choices can look natural after seeing the finished work. Making a prediction first restores some of the original uncertainty.

A simple habit captures the idea well: before revealing the answer, decide what you think the answer might be.
