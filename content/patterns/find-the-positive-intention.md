### What the objection is protecting

Imagine that you have spent weeks developing a proposal and, when you finally present it, someone at the table says it is far too risky. It is easy to hear the objection as an obstacle that now has to be overcome. You can defend the proposal, produce more evidence, or argue that the danger is exaggerated. Yet the exchange may become more useful if you first understand what the objection is protecting. The person might be worried about wasting six months, losing a customer's trust, or committing colleagues to something they cannot reverse.

Robert Dilts calls this movement toward the purpose behind a statement the Intention pattern in Sleight of Mouth. The shift matters because a concern and the strategy used to express it are different things. Reliability may be a shared aim even when one person pursues it by delaying a release and another by testing with a small group of users. Recognizing the aim gives the conversation a point of contact. It also makes the disagreement more specific: what would actually protect reliability in this situation?

The word positive requires some care. A purpose can be understandable from the person's perspective without being admirable or beneficial to everyone. Avoiding embarrassment, retaining control, and protecting status can all help explain a reaction. It would be misleading to rename them generosity simply because the pattern asks us to look for an intention. The useful task is to discover what the response is accomplishing for the person, while keeping its effects visible.

### Separating a concern from its strategy

A product manager refuses to launch a feature until every known defect has been fixed. The team initially responds with arguments about deadlines. Each argument makes the manager more insistent, because the willingness to launch sounds like indifference to quality. Eventually a colleague asks which failure would most damage customer trust. The manager identifies incorrect billing. That answer changes the discussion. Billing defects become a release condition, while cosmetic defects can be documented and repaired later. The wish to protect customers survives, but it no longer requires every issue to have the same priority.

This is how the pattern creates alternatives. If the objection and the purpose remain fused, removing the objection appears to mean abandoning what matters. Once they are separated, several strategies can be compared against the same concern. A cautious pilot, an explicit rollback plan, or an independent review might serve the purpose better than a blanket refusal. The agreement does not have to be emotional or complete. It only needs to be specific enough that both people can recognize what the proposed alternatives are supposed to preserve.

The distinction also helps explain apparently puzzling personal habits. Someone who postpones sharing a draft may describe the problem as laziness. A closer look could reveal a fear of being judged before the work represents what they intended. Showing a small excerpt to a trusted reader might provide useful feedback while preserving some protection from premature exposure. This explanation remains a possibility to investigate; the habit could also involve exhaustion, competing demands, or an unclear task. A plausible intention should open inquiry rather than end it.

### Understanding without excusing

There is a conversational difference between asking what matters to someone and announcing why they behave as they do. A flattering explanation can still feel intrusive. Telling a colleague that their criticism comes from a deep commitment to excellence may sound like an attempt to manage them, especially if their actual concern is workload or unfairness. Tentative language and the person's own account are more reliable. A question about what they want to prevent allows an answer that the listener did not anticipate.

Understanding an intention also leaves responsibility intact. A wish to feel secure does not justify monitoring a partner's messages. A desire for respect does not justify intimidation. In these situations the pattern can help identify another way to address the concern, but it cannot substitute for a boundary or a change in behavior. It can become particularly unhelpful when the person affected by harm is expected to keep translating it into benevolent motives. The impact deserves direct recognition alongside any account of purpose.

Dilts presents intention as an organizing assumption within his framework. In a general communication setting, it is better treated as a search direction than as a claim that every act must conceal a good motive. Sometimes people are uncertain about what they wanted; sometimes several aims conflict; sometimes an action is largely habitual. The pattern remains useful if the inquiry reveals only a modest concern that can be addressed more directly.

### A shared criterion for moving forward

Find the Positive Intention is especially helpful when discussion keeps returning to the same objection and each response increases defensiveness. It offers a way to ask what the objection is doing before attempting to remove it. Redefine the Terms can then make the concern more precise, while Shift the Outcome can examine whether the conversation is pursuing the right goal. Put a Frame Around the Belief asks a further question about the circumstances in which this protective strategy became persuasive.

What changes is the shape of the disagreement. Instead of treating another person's position as something to defeat, the participants can compare ways of addressing a concern they now understand more clearly. They may still disagree about acceptable risk, responsibility, or cost. But the disagreement has become easier to examine, because a particular strategy no longer has exclusive ownership of the value it claims to protect.

## Pattern details

### Use when

- An objection repeatedly triggers defensiveness.
- A shared concern is hidden behind opposing strategies.

### Goals

- Understand the concern behind a response.
- Compare alternative ways to address the concern.

### Mechanisms

- intention inquiry
- concern and strategy distinction

### Not ideal when

- Understanding a motive is being used to excuse harm or override a boundary.

### Difficulty

easy

    Describe a response or habit you would like to understand. Stick to what happened, without guessing anyone's hidden motives.

    Separate the strategy from its effect

}
!ask concern {
    Try a tentative intention

    What concern, need, or purpose might this strategy be trying to serve? Write 'possibly' rather than treating it as a fact. 'I don't know' is a valid answer.

    Find another way

    What safer or more workable strategy might serve that concern without repeating the unwanted impact?
}

    Keep responsibility in view

    What facts, consequences, or boundaries must the alternative still respect? A possible intention does not excuse harmful behavior.
}
