An argument can sound decisive because it answers a position nobody actually holds. A proposal becomes more sweeping in the summary, a qualification disappears, or the writer assigns motives the source never expressed. The response may be persuasive on its own terms while leaving the actual disagreement untouched.

This pattern helps when a reader cannot tell what a piece answers, a summary turns another position into an easy target, or a writer's contribution blends into the view it disputes. **Establish the relevant position faithfully, then make the relationship of your contribution to it clear.**

Fair representation does not require agreement, an exhaustive review, or equal space for every view. It gives the reader a reliable starting point for assessing the response that follows.

## Identify the proposal before interpreting its implications

A response needs something more definite than _people say_ or a loose category such as _traditional thinking_. The relevant starting point may be a particular proposal, a published argument, an established practice, or a documented pattern of claims.

Imagine a fictional committee arranging monthly talks in a reading room with forty seats. Its draft proposal says:

> Keep advance booking for all forty places. People travelling from outside town need to know there will be space. Offer booking by phone as well as online.

A response initially summarizes it this way:

> The committee wants every place booked online, putting administrative convenience ahead of open access.

That sentence does more than shorten the proposal. It removes the phone option, substitutes a motive, and drops the reason involving people travelling to the event. Readers are being asked to assess a different arrangement.

A response can make a substantial objection without those changes:

> The committee proposes booking all forty places, online or by phone, so people travelling from outside town can secure a seat. That addresses the risk of a wasted journey. It leaves a different question unresolved: whether any places should remain available to people who learn about the talk late.

The response now has a readable point of departure. It can propose holding back some places or investigate how late arrivals are currently handled. Accurately presenting the committee's reason has not prevented disagreement; it has specified it.

The revision also does not establish that a mixed arrangement will work. Its capacity, administration, and effect on travellers would still need consideration. Fair entry into the conversation makes that further argument possible rather than supplying its result.

## Preserve the qualification that changes the position

Some details can be omitted without altering what a source argues. Others determine the view the writer needs to answer.

In the reading-room proposal, _by phone as well as online_ matters if the response concerns people who cannot book online. The number of seats matters if the response discusses setting aside a portion. The colour of the room's curtains probably does not.

The writer can select according to the response's purpose while preserving the original relationship among the important parts. A summary about the allocation principle might be shorter than one about the booking procedure. Neither should make a limited proposal sound like a general opposition to access.

The same care applies to words such as _some_, _usually_, and _under these conditions_. Removing one may turn a qualified claim into a universal one. A response to the universal claim would then miss what the source actually said.

Selection needs enough context to remain accurate, not enough detail to recreate the whole source. A summary can be brief and faithful at the same time.

## Separate stated reasons from an assessment of effects

The committee's proposal may have consequences its authors have not acknowledged. A writer can examine those consequences without attributing them as intentions.

For example, the response might argue that reserving every seat would leave no guaranteed allocation for walk-ins when bookings are full. That concerns how the proposed arrangement operates. It is different from claiming that the committee wants to discourage unplanned attendance.

Likewise, the writer might find that booking reduces administrative effort, but that would not make convenience the committee's stated priority. The distinction keeps the source's account and the response's interpretation available separately.

A source can explicitly express a troubling aim or make an unsupported assertion. Fair representation need not soften it into something more reasonable. The aim is accuracy, including features that make a position difficult to defend.

Nor should a writer silently improve the proposal and attribute the improved version to its authors. A further argument in its favour can be introduced as a possible defence. It should remain distinguishable from what the committee actually wrote.

## Make the new contribution identifiable

A summary can be accurate yet occupy so much space that readers cannot locate the writer's response. Once the necessary starting point is available, the piece needs to show what it adds.

The contribution may disagree, accept part of the proposal, supply missing information, narrow its application, or shift the question. In the reading-room case, it might agree that travellers need certainty while arguing that reserving every seat is not the only possible allocation.

That relationship can be stated plainly. A formulation such as _I agree with the aim, but would reserve a small walk-in allocation_ identifies both continuity and difference. It does not require a ceremonial compliment before criticism.

Some pieces will need a fuller account because the disagreement depends on a chain of reasoning rather than a single proposal. Others can orient the reader in a sentence. The necessary length follows from what readers need to understand the contribution.

## Quote where wording matters, summarize where the position matters

A short quotation can preserve a consequential phrase. It still needs enough context for readers to know how that phrase functions in the source.

Quoting only _all forty places_ would not establish that the committee favours online-only booking. A perfectly accurate fragment can support a misleading account when a relevant condition is omitted.

Where exact language is not the issue, a clear paraphrase may present the proposal more efficiently. The writer remains responsible for attribution and fidelity even when no quotation marks appear. A string of selected quotations is not automatically a fairer account than a careful summary.

The reader also needs to know whether the position is a draft suggestion, an adopted rule, or one member's preference. Changing that status can make a response appear to challenge an established policy when it is really taking part in a discussion still underway.

## Choose the relevant conversation without inventing consensus

An argument does not need to catalogue every possible opinion. It should establish the position or practice that makes its contribution intelligible and consequential.

A single committee note cannot stand for what all event organizers believe. Conversely, a widespread practice may be relevant even if no one has written its rationale in a convenient sentence. A writer can describe the practice and identify uncertainty about its reasons rather than invent a representative speaker.

This pattern establishes the starting conversation. Anticipating a serious challenge to the writer's own proposal is another job. Both matter, but a thorough rebuttal cannot repair a starting position that has already been misrepresented.

## Source note

The Harvard Guide to Using Sources' [Summarizing, Paraphrasing, and Quoting](https://usingsources.fas.harvard.edu/summarizing-paraphrasing-and-quoting), especially its summary and paraphrase guidance, calls for accurate representation, clear attribution, and selection relevant to the writer's response. The committee, proposal, and responses here are invented. No source example, exercise, empirical claim, or requirement for equal attention to every position is adopted. Motive–effect distinctions, proposal status, and the boundary with answering objections are editorial development; no full book-passage review is claimed.
