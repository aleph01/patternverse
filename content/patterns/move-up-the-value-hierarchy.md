### When two worthwhile aims conflict

A team has promised to release a product on Friday. On Thursday, testing reveals that some customers could receive incorrect invoices. The deadline still matters: colleagues have prepared announcements, customers expect the feature, and delays carry costs. Yet the problem introduces another criterion, accurate billing, that may deserve priority over punctuality. The decision becomes clearer once the team recognizes that two legitimate aims are competing and that keeping the date is no longer the only measure of responsible performance.

Robert Dilts calls this movement Hierarchy of Criteria in Sleight of Mouth. A judgment organized around one criterion can be reconsidered through another that matters more in the relevant situation. A person may accept discomfort to preserve honesty, spend more to obtain reliability, or relinquish convenience to protect privacy. The lower criterion does not become worthless. Its priority changes because the available choice cannot satisfy all values equally.

In this adaptation, moving up the hierarchy means examining precedence rather than assuming a fixed ladder of noble values. Comfort, independence, accuracy, loyalty, and care can each have different weight in different circumstances. The pattern asks which consideration should govern a particular trade-off and why. Naming that consideration makes the choice easier to understand and its costs harder to conceal.

### What a higher criterion changes

A person avoids telling a friend that a recurring arrangement no longer works for them. Their immediate criterion is keeping the conversation comfortable. Silence meets that criterion today, but it creates unwanted commitments and growing resentment. When they consider the honesty needed for a sustainable friendship, some discomfort becomes an acceptable cost. They can then seek a considerate way to speak, preserving comfort where possible while allowing honesty to determine whether the subject is raised at all.

The shift should change behavior in a way that corresponds to the value. Honesty does not require delivering every grievance at once or ignoring the friend's circumstances. It supports a clear account of what has become difficult and what would work better. The lower criterion still informs how the conversation happens. A hierarchy can establish direction while leaving several values active in the implementation.

A purchasing decision provides a less emotionally charged example. The least expensive tool may be sufficient for occasional use, while dependable performance may deserve priority for work that must be completed every day. Comparing the tools only by price obscures the reason to spend more. Comparing them only by reliability may encourage unnecessary expense. The appropriate precedence depends on the task and consequences of failure. The pattern makes that dependence explicit rather than treating either frugality or quality as universally superior.

### Values should not become a pressure tactic

Appealing to a higher value can make coercion sound principled. A manager can invoke loyalty to justify excessive work, or a friend can invoke generosity to discourage a boundary. The language suggests that a refusal would reveal a moral defect rather than a reasonable disagreement about priorities. A fair use of the pattern allows the person affected to participate in identifying the values and deciding how much a sacrifice is justified.

The hierarchy also should not be treated as permanent without examination. A person may choose independence in one situation and mutual support in another because the conditions differ. Some values function as limits rather than quantities to optimize. A commitment to privacy, for example, may establish boundaries within which convenience is pursued. The decision may require specifying acceptable conditions, not merely ranking two abstract nouns and announcing a winner.

Sometimes the apparent conflict can be reduced through design. A team might preserve much of the deadline by releasing unaffected components while delaying billing changes. A difficult conversation might become clearer and less distressing through better timing. Looking for such options does not eliminate the need to decide what takes precedence if the conflict remains. It prevents the hierarchy from becoming a premature excuse to neglect a value that could still be served.

### A reason for accepting a cost

Move Up the Value Hierarchy is useful when a choice is stalled because one criterion dominates or a necessary cost feels like proof that the decision is wrong. Shift the Outcome can introduce another legitimate goal without necessarily claiming that it is more important. Find the Positive Intention identifies what the current criterion protects. Follow the Consequences helps compare what different priorities will actually produce over time.

The result is a more accountable trade-off. The team can explain why accurate billing takes precedence over the date, the friend can understand why an uncomfortable conversation is being initiated, and the buyer can judge whether greater reliability justifies the expense. None of these decisions makes the cost disappear. A defensible priority explains why the cost is being accepted and leaves room to reduce it where the situation allows.

## Pattern details

### Use when

- Two legitimate values conflict.
- One dominant criterion obscures a justified cost.

### Goals

- Make priorities explicit and accountable.
- Preserve lower-priority values where possible.

### Mechanisms

- value comparison
- criterion precedence

### Not ideal when

- An appeal to loyalty or generosity is being used to override another person’s boundaries.

### Difficulty

medium
