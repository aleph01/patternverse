*Bring the location to the performers.*

Two characters argue while driving through a city. Buildings pass behind them, streetlights change, and the conversation continues without interruption.

Filming that scene on a real road introduces competing demands: traffic, weather, sound, camera access, and the need to repeat a performance. A science-fiction scene presents a larger version of the problem: the view outside the windows may not exist anywhere.

**Background projection brings that surrounding world onto the stage.** Actors perform in front of a screen displaying scenery photographed or created separately. The camera records the foreground performance and the displayed background together.

The technique allows filmmakers to control a performance while giving it the appearance of happening somewhere else.

## The puzzle: how can the car travel while its wheels stay still?


In a traditional studio driving setup, the car remains on the floor. Behind it, a screen shows footage recorded from a moving vehicle.

Through the film camera, the screen’s edges disappear. The passing scenery becomes the view through the car’s windows. Small movements, changing light, reflections, and the performer’s reactions help connect the stationary foreground to that moving world.

The separately recorded scenery is called a **background plate**. It might contain a moving street, a landscape, clouds, or activity happening beyond a window. A still photograph can also supply an apparently distant environment.

The underlying pattern is simple: **photograph the surrounding world separately, then display it where the camera expects that world to be.**

## Rear projection: put the projector behind the screen

In rear projection, the projector shines onto the back of a translucent screen. The actors and camera occupy the other side.

The camera photographs the image transmitted through the screen, with the performers in front of it. Keeping the projector behind the screen prevents its beam from having to cross the foreground performance.

This arrangement became closely associated with studio driving scenes, but its possibilities extend beyond transportation. Projected scenery can supply danger, enormous scale, or a subjective transformation.

### Example: *Vertigo* (1958)—how can a room become a remembered place during a kiss?

Scottie embraces Judy after reshaping her appearance into that of Madeleine. As the viewpoint moves around them, the room appears to become the stable where he previously embraced Madeleine.

The change makes memory occupy the present.

American Cinematographer describes a circular background set incorporating elements of the room and stable. Footage of that environment was projected behind James Stewart and Kim Novak, who stood on a rotating platform. Their movement was coordinated with the background.

Projection therefore provides more than a substitute location. It lets the filmmakers change the psychological meaning of the space while keeping the performers together in the foreground.

The movement also demonstrates how a carefully prepared plate can support a more elaborate shot than a stationary view through a car window. [Read the illustrated production account.](https://theasc.com/article/flashback-vertigo/)

## Front projection: send the image from the camera side

<!-- film-illustration:front-projection-layout -->
![An overhead plan shows a beam splitter aligning projection with the filming axis. A reflective screen returns the background toward the camera; practical systems also manage shadows and spill.](../assets/filmmaking/front-projection-layout.svg "A beam splitter aligns the projected image with the filming direction. The reflective screen returns the background toward the lens. Illustrative scenario, not a documented film setup.")
<!-- /film-illustration:front-projection-layout -->


Front projection places the projector on the same side of the screen as the performers.

One important version uses a highly reflective screen and a partially reflective mirror to align the projector’s light with the camera’s viewing direction. That alignment helps place the performers’ shadows behind them from the camera’s perspective.

Other front-projection arrangements use different screen materials and projector positions. The shared feature is the direction from which the image reaches the screen; there is no single universal front-projection rig.

### Example: *2001: A Space Odyssey* (1968)—how can a studio contain a prehistoric landscape?

The “Dawn of Man” sequence places its performers against expansive terrain, giving the action a scale far beyond the foreground set.

Effects artist Douglas Trumbull explained that the sequence used large photographic transparencies projected onto a giant background screen. The production combined those landscapes with the physical foreground where the performers worked.

The apparent depth comes from joining two kinds of space: nearby objects that bodies can occupy and touch, and distant scenery that only needs to look convincing from the camera.

This is a particularly effective division when the background is far away. Distant mountains need much less visible perspective change during a small camera movement than a nearby doorway would.

[Trumbull’s original American Cinematographer account describes the projection equipment and screen construction.](https://visual-memory.co.uk/sk/2001a/page3.html)

### Example: *Oblivion* (2013)—how can a glass house stand above the clouds?

![A glass-house set is surrounded by a panoramic screen showing clouds.](https://www.fdtimes.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/03/2417_D034_00150.jpg)

*Oblivion (2013) — Set photograph.* The glass-house set surrounded by projected clouds. Twenty-one projectors created a panoramic background that appeared through the windows, reflected in the glass, and contributed illumination. [Image source](https://www.fdtimes.com/2013/03/29/claudio-miranda-asc-on-oblivion/). Credit: © Universal Pictures; published by Film and Digital Times.

The Sky Tower has large windows, reflective surfaces, and an expansive sky outside. Its surroundings need to appear in the windows, in reflections, and in the illumination falling across the actors.

Cinematographer Claudio Miranda described surrounding the set with an enormous screen showing cloud footage photographed from a Hawaiian volcano. Twenty-one projectors combined their images into a panoramic background.

The displayed sky also supplied much of the scene’s lighting. Choosing a different sky changed the surrounding view and its effect on the glass and performers.

This is an important extension of the pattern: **the background can participate in the foreground photograph.** Its colors and bright areas become light sources and reflections.

Miranda’s interview includes production photographs and explains the projection surface, photographic choices, and lighting. [See the Film and Digital Times interview.](https://www.fdtimes.com/2013/03/29/claudio-miranda-asc-on-oblivion/)

## The modern relative: LED backgrounds

In *First Man* (2018), a displayed sky surrounds the X-15 cockpit. The following setup and finished shot show how the stage becomes a flight environment. LEDs emit their own light; they are the display-based relative of projection.
LED walls replace the projector-and-screen arrangement with panels that display images directly.

They continue the broader idea of photographing performers against a displayed environment. Some play recorded footage; others show a three-dimensional scene rendered for the camera’s current position.

That second approach addresses an important limitation of conventional plates: perspective.

When a camera moves sideways through a real environment, nearby objects shift against distant ones. This relative movement is **parallax**. A prerecorded image contains the perspective of its original camera. Moving the studio camera cannot reveal a new view around objects inside that recording.

A tracked, rendered environment can update its perspective as the camera moves.

### Example: *The Mandalorian* (2019–)—how can a camera move through an alien landscape inside a studio?

The production combined physical foreground scenery with environments displayed on surrounding LED walls. Camera tracking allowed the rendered background perspective to respond to the camera’s movement.

American Cinematographer describes an early shot in which the camera rises as the Mandalorian approaches distant structures: part of the walkway was physical, while the displayed environment extended the world beyond it. [Read the illustrated technical account.](https://theasc.com/article/the-mandalorian/)

The screens also provided surrounding light and reflections, helping place the performers within the environment. [Lucasfilm’s StageCraft feature explains the production approach.](https://www.starwars.com/news/the-mandalorian-stagecraft-feature)

## What makes the foreground and background belong together?
A convincing projected scene requires several agreements.

**Perspective must agree.** The plate’s camera height, viewing direction, and apparent scale should suit the foreground shot. A view recorded from high above a road will look strange behind someone sitting in an ordinary car.

**Movement must agree.** Passing scenery establishes speed and direction. A sudden turn in the plate needs a compatible response from the performers, camera, or vehicle setup.

**Light must agree.** A bright exterior behind a face with incompatible studio lighting can make the two layers feel separate. Moving shadows and reflections help suggest that the foreground occupies the displayed world.

**Image quality must agree.** Differences in contrast, color, sharpness, and blur can expose the screen. Camera exposure and display timing also need to work together without visible flicker or banding.

These are photographic relationships, rather than problems solved by making the screen larger.

## Where the method reaches its limits
A screen remains a physical boundary. Performers can interact with the practical scenery in front of it, but entering a displayed doorway requires another solution.

Camera movement also needs planning. A fixed plate provides limited freedom to change viewpoint; a tracked environment introduces its own rendering and display constraints.

LED imagery can provide useful surrounding illumination, but strong direct sunlight may still require additional lighting or location work. The Mandalorian’s cinematographers discuss that limitation alongside the system’s benefits in their production account.

Projection also moves decisions earlier. The background must be available for photography, and changes afterward may affect reflections and lighting already recorded on the foreground.

## Connections to other patterns

**[Creative geography](/pattern/film-creative-geography)** joins separately filmed places into a convincing world. Projection can put those places together within a single image.

**[Miniatures](/pattern/film-miniatures) and matte paintings** can supply the scenery photographed or displayed as a background.

**[Motion control](/pattern/film-motion-control-and-repeatable-camera-movements)** helps coordinate repeatable camera movements when a projection or composite requires them.
