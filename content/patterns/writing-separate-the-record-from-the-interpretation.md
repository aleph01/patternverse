A factual account can become less trustworthy as it becomes smoother. Information from a document, someone's later recollection, and the writer's explanation flow through the same assured voice. Readers can follow what happened without being able to tell which parts are recorded and which have been supplied to make sense of the record.

This pattern helps when a report conceals the origin of a claim, a remembered scene sounds like a transcript, or an interpretation acquires the authority of the document beside it. **Make the basis of consequential statements visible where readers need it.**

The aim is not to remove interpretation. It is to let an observation, a reported explanation, a memory, and an inference do their different work without silently becoming interchangeable.

## Notice when the account changes its basis

Imagine a writer preparing a short history of a neighbourhood cinema. The available material includes a programme listing the final screening on 18 November, a manager's letter announcing closure for repairs, and an interview recorded decades later in which a former usher remembers rainwater reaching the back row. All documents, dates, people, and wording in this example are invented.

The draft says:

> The cinema closed on 18 November because its owners could no longer afford to repair the leaking roof. Rain fell on the back row as the manager watched the final audience leave, knowing the building would never reopen.

The account reads as one continuous scene, but its claims have different foundations. The programme lists a screening. The letter gives a stated reason for closure. The usher supplies a later memory. The owners' finances, the manager's presence that evening, and the manager's private knowledge have not been established.

The problem survives even if the explanation seems plausible. A believable connection is still something the writer has added. A source can support part of a sentence without supporting everything its grammar joins together.

## Give each source the claim it can actually support

A revision can retain the material while distinguishing its basis:

> The surviving programme lists a final screening on 18 November. In a letter circulated that month, the manager announced a closure for repairs. Interviewed decades later, a former usher recalled rainwater reaching the back row, though she could not place that memory on the final night. These accounts suggest a building in need of work; they do not establish why the cinema never reopened.

The paragraph no longer supplies a complete explanation of the closure. That incompleteness belongs to the available account. The reader can now distinguish a dated record, an announced reason, a later recollection, and the writer's limited inference.

Even the programme requires care. A listed screening is evidence of what was scheduled; establishing that it took place may require another record. Calling it the final screening could mean the last one the writer has found rather than the last one that occurred. Which distinction matters depends on the claim the history makes.

This is more than adding a citation to the original paragraph. A reference to the manager's letter would not make the financial explanation or private thought part of that letter. The prose must give the source an accurate role.

## Let attribution verbs preserve the difference

Small choices can change what readers think a source establishes. A manager _announced_ repairs, _explained_ a closure, or _admitted_ that a building was failing: those formulations carry different implications.

If the invented letter merely says the cinema will close for repairs, _announced_ describes its action. _Admitted_ adds a suggestion of reluctant disclosure. _Confirmed_ can imply that the account settled a question independently. Neither implication should enter simply because the writer wants a more varied verb.

Likewise, an usher _recalled_ something in an interview; the interview did not necessarily _prove_ the event occurred exactly as remembered. Naming the basis does not require dismissing the recollection. It allows the reader to understand what kind of evidence it supplies.

Once a source is clearly established, every sentence need not repeat its full introduction. Attribution can carry across a passage while the ownership remains unambiguous. A new source or a move into the writer's interpretation may need a fresh signal.

## Preserve memory without manufacturing a transcript

The same issue appears in personal writing. A narrator may remember the force of a conversation more securely than its exact words.

Consider a separate invented memoir passage. The narrator remembers asking her mother about an empty room, being refused an answer, and later understanding the refusal differently. She has no recording or contemporary written account.

Writing a precise exchange inside quotation marks could imply a record the narrator does not have. She can instead distinguish the remembered event from the later interpretation:

> I remember asking why the room was locked and being told to leave it alone. I cannot recover her exact words. At the time I heard irritation; now I wonder whether she was trying to keep the conversation from reaching my brother.

The passage retains feeling and reflection. It does not turn a present hypothesis about the mother's purpose into knowledge the child possessed, or imply access to the mother's private thoughts.

The appropriate treatment depends on the work's conventions and the nature of the recollection. A general note about reconstructed dialogue may orient readers, but it cannot make an uncertain consequential detail certain. Some passages need a local distinction because the interpretation turns on it.

## Keep disagreement in the record readable

Two accounts may conflict. Suppose the invented usher remembers several evenings with water in the auditorium, while another employee remembers only a problem in the foyer. Combining them into a single vivid scene would remove the disagreement by creating an event neither described.

A useful account can identify what each person recalled and what remains unresolved. It can also examine differences in vantage point or date when there is evidence for them. It should not invent a reconciliation merely because disagreement interrupts the story.

Naming uncertainty need not stop the writing. The unresolved difference may itself matter to the history. But _the accounts differ_ is a finding about the available accounts, not a finding that one witness must be mistaken in a particular way.

## Make the distinction proportionate to the work

Readers do not need an explanation of how the writer knows every ordinary connective fact. Constant qualifications can overwhelm a passage and obscure the claims that most need attention.

The useful threshold concerns consequence: would knowing the basis change how a reader assesses this statement? A reconstructed conversation, an attributed motive, a contested date, or a cause inferred from sequence often needs more visibility than a routine descriptive transition.

Source notes can hold fuller references and methodological explanations. The body should still preserve distinctions necessary to understand the claim at the point of reading. Smooth prose and visible provenance can coexist when attribution is placed where the basis changes.

Nor does visible provenance guarantee a sound argument. A source can be named accurately and interpreted poorly. This pattern makes the account available for assessment; further reasoning determines what the material supports.
