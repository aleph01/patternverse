## What it is for

An office needs a different room arrangement, but moving one partition means disturbing a service route, a ceiling system and a facade junction. The structure is sound, yet a modest change produces extensive demolition. The building's parts have been tied together as if they would all become obsolete at the same moment.

## The core idea

**Shearing Layers**, associated with Stewart Brand and developing Frank Duffy's time-layer model, describes parts of a building changing at different rates. Room layouts, equipment and finishes may need renewal while the main structure remains useful. Design their connections so work on a faster-changing part causes less damage to the durable parts around it. This gives future alterations a practical starting point instead of tying every component to the same replacement cycle.

Original schematic.")

## How it works

Identify durable structure and the systems likely to change sooner. Test plausible changes to rooms and services, then design accessible interfaces that allow them without unnecessary damage to retained parts. Coordinate those interfaces with fire, acoustic and envelope requirements, preserving useful records for future adaptation.

## Why it works

The model distinguishes site; structure; skin, meaning the outer enclosure; services such as pipes, wiring and ventilation; the space plan, or internal layout; and movable contents. These names help reveal dependencies. Moving a partition may require new lighting while the frame remains unchanged. Actual lifetimes depend on exposure, materials, use and care, so use the layers to ask what is likely to change rather than assign guaranteed replacement dates.

Separation also has limits. Layers need to work together for structure, fire safety, acoustics, waterproofing and environmental control. An air barrier cannot simply detach from every adjacent component without a designed connection. The goal is useful independence within a coordinated assembly. “Loose fit” should not become a synonym for gaps, poor tolerances or unresolved technical responsibility.

## Architectural examples

### Lloyd’s Building — services made legible

At Lloyd's Building, external service towers and circulation can be distinguished from the principal occupied volume. Their separation makes different building functions visible. It offers a starting point for considering access and replacement: which parts need attention more often, and how could workers reach them? Actual adaptability also depends on the connections and maintenance routes, which require more detailed records than this exterior view supplies.

### An adaptable school — illustrative scenario

In a hypothetical school, a frame and facade module support several classroom sizes. Service routes remain accessible, and partition interfaces are designed for the expected alternatives. Combining two teaching rooms still requires an acoustic and ventilation review, but it does not automatically require cutting the primary frame. The building is adaptable for specified scenarios rather than for every imaginable future institution.

### An apartment conversion — illustrative scenario

In a hypothetical apartment conversion, a retained structural shell accommodates new services in accessible zones rather than embedding every pipe in irreversible finishes. A future bathroom replacement can then occur with less disturbance to neighboring rooms. The route still needs appropriate fire and acoustic treatment, access and leak management. Accessibility and maintenance are not separate from adaptability; they help determine whether the future work is feasible.

![Two sections compare services cast into permanent construction and services with planned access.](../assets/architecture/loose-fit-layers-2.svg "Make likely changes possible while respecting fire, acoustic and structural requirements. Original schematic.")

### Tate Modern: a durable framework reused

Tate Modern shows a durable industrial structure accommodating a new public use. The retained hall gives the conversion spatial capacity while new activities and systems are introduced. It is a useful comparison for asking what can stay through a change of program. The shearing-layers model then directs attention to the particular interfaces that allow rooms, equipment or services to change within that larger framework.

## Design considerations

Identify a few credible changes with the client and operators. A workplace may change room layout frequently; a laboratory may renew equipment while retaining a stable circulation pattern. Decide where access, spare capacity or modular connections earn their cost. Extra height or an oversized service zone has resource consequences. Flexibility is valuable when it addresses a plausible need, not simply because the word sounds responsible.

![Plans show two room layouts within the same retained structural frame.](../assets/architecture/loose-fit-layers-3.svg "Useful adaptability is demonstrated through plausible scenarios, not an empty floor plate. Original schematic.")

A floor plate is the usable arrangement of a building level. An open one may still be hard to divide if windows, toilets, services or fire-separated areas cannot support the new rooms. A building with many existing partitions may suit several uses if the rooms connect well. Draw the likely alternatives with access, light and sound separation included before judging adaptability from the amount of open floor alone.

Changes also involve people and schedules. A building may be physically adaptable but impossible to refurbish without unacceptable interruption to its service. Consider phasing, temporary routes and areas that can operate independently. These operational relationships belong in the adaptability discussion alongside construction details. A plan that can change one zone at a time may offer more useful resilience than one requiring a complete shutdown.

Obtain professional advice for actual changes to structure, fire strategy, services and envelope.

## Useful when

This pattern helps at briefing, structural planning, facade coordination and refurbishment. It can also guide a condition survey by distinguishing failures: an outdated service system does not prove the frame is obsolete. Conversely, retaining a frame should not force occupants to accept permanently inaccessible or unsuitable spaces. The model supports diagnosis and options, not retention at any cost.

- Changing the office layout requires rebuilding permanent construction.
- Can services be replaced without cutting into the structure?
- How do we design for different rates of building change?
- Our long-lived frame is tied to short-lived equipment.
- We need an adaptable plan without pretending every use is possible.
- Should partitions follow the structural grid exactly?
- Why does a small refurbishment trigger so much demolition?
- How can a building stay useful as its occupants change?

## Common mistakes and limits

Common mistakes include quoting fixed lifespans without evidence, providing inaccessible services, and allowing a proprietary facade or ceiling system to dictate every future partition. Records and replacement availability matter. A theoretically separable component can become a practical obstacle if its interface is undocumented or its manufacturer no longer supports it. Prefer understandable connections and preserve the information needed to work on them.
