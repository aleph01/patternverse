It is possible to become very good at exercises that resemble the skill you actually want. A language app, a writing course, or a series of cooking videos can produce real learning while still leaving an important gap between practice and performance.

Practice the Real Thing means making sure that the actual activity appears regularly in the learning process. If you want to speak Italian, have conversations. If you want to perform music, play for other people. If you want to become comfortable cooking, prepare complete meals rather than only studying recipes or isolated techniques.

Real situations contain complications that exercises conveniently remove. Conversations are unpredictable, audiences create pressure, ingredients vary, and real tasks rarely announce which technique should be used. These complications can feel inconvenient, but they are often part of what competence actually requires.

A person might perform perfectly in a language exercise that asks for the correct past tense while still struggling to use that tense in a fast conversation. The exercise teaches the construction; the conversation teaches when and how to access it under realistic conditions.

Direct practice should also vary enough that the skill does not become tied to one narrow context. A speaker who only rehearses alone can benefit from different rooms, audiences, time limits, and levels of preparation. A cook who always follows the same recipe can try the same underlying technique with different ingredients.

This does not mean abandoning drills, exercises, or simplified practice. Those are often useful precisely because they make parts of a complex skill easier to isolate. The problem arises when they quietly become substitutes for the activity they were meant to support.

A useful test is to ask: if I became excellent at my current practice, would that automatically make me good at the thing I ultimately care about?

If the answer is only partly, the learning probably needs more contact with the real thing.
