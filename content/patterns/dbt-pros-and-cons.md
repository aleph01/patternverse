## A familiar moment

You open a group chat and find a comment that feels unfair. A sharp reply would take seconds. You imagine the satisfaction of making your point publicly. The possibility of an argument tomorrow seems much less vivid than the relief of pressing Send now.

You may already know there could be consequences. In the moment, they are difficult to keep in view. DBT Pros and Cons gives you a place to examine both the appeal of the immediate action and what may follow it, alongside a specific alternative.

## Understand the skill

In Dialectical Behavior Therapy, or DBT, Pros and Cons is a distress tolerance skill. It compares acting on an urge with resisting that urge or using a more effective response. It has four parts: benefits and costs of acting, and benefits and costs of not acting or choosing the alternative.

Both immediate and later effects belong in the comparison. An urge may offer real short-term relief. Naming that appeal can make the comparison more honest. Similarly, a helpful alternative may involve discomfort, effort, or delayed satisfaction. [DBT Self Help's explanation](https://dbtselfhelp.com/pros-and-cons/) distinguishes this from a general list about an ordinary decision.

The purpose is to support a choice you can keep in view under pressure. It is not a contest where the option with the most bullet points wins. One serious consequence can matter more than several minor advantages. You can identify uncertainty rather than pretending you know exactly what will happen.

## When to use it

Try the skill when an action offers immediate relief but may create difficulties later: sending an angry message, abandoning a task in frustration, or cancelling a valued plan to escape manageable discomfort. It is especially useful to prepare a comparison before a familiar urge becomes intense.

Choose a manageable everyday urge for self-guided practice. If you are at immediate risk of serious harm, prioritize safety and appropriate support rather than debating the behavior in a worksheet. Physical needs such as rest, food, or prescribed care are not failures of distress tolerance. A pros-and-cons exercise should not be used to pressure someone into enduring abuse or giving up a necessary boundary.

## How to practice

Use the established four-part comparison and consider time in each part. The specific alternative and takeaway card below are interactive adaptations.

1. **Name the urge and action.** Be concrete: “Post the sharp reply in the group chat.” Identify the situation briefly.
2. **Name the alternative.** Choose something usable, such as waiting before replying and asking for clarification privately. “Do nothing forever” is rarely the only alternative.
3. **List the benefits of acting on the urge.** Include immediate relief or satisfaction if that is its appeal, and any possible later benefits.
4. **List the costs of acting on the urge.** Consider immediate and later effects on the practical issue, relationships, safety, and your goals.
5. **List the benefits of choosing the alternative.** Consider what it protects now and makes possible later.
6. **List the costs of choosing the alternative.** Include effort, waiting, uncertainty, or a feeling that may remain.
7. **Review what matters most.** Distinguish predictions from known patterns. Choose one next action and keep the most relevant reasons accessible.

[DBT.tools' Pros/Cons guide](https://dbt.tools/distress_tolerance/pro-con.php) recommends preparing and reviewing a comparison when an urge appears.

## See it in action

For the group chat, the urge is to post: “You clearly have no idea what happened.” The alternative is to wait, then ask privately what the person meant before deciding whether a public response is needed.

The benefit of posting now is immediate satisfaction and the possibility of correcting a misleading impression quickly. The costs include escalating a public exchange, losing focus on the original issue, and spending the evening checking replies. These are possibilities, not certain outcomes.

The alternative gives you time to understand the comment and prepare a more precise response. It may protect the relationship and your attention. Its costs are also real: you must sit with irritation, delay saying your piece, and risk not receiving an immediate explanation.

You decide that understanding the issue matters more than the first burst of satisfaction. Your next action is to save the draft elsewhere and close the chat for a short break. You keep a note: “Waiting is uncomfortable; it leaves me more ways to respond.”

Later, you may still choose to challenge the comment publicly. The comparison has not required silence or agreement. It has helped you choose the timing and form of a response with more of its consequences in view.

## Try it yourself

**See the Four Sides** takes about 7–10 minutes. Use a manageable urge, keep your details private, or work with the chat example. You will fill four short sections, mark immediate and later effects, and choose the considerations that matter most.

You will leave with a comparison and a concise reminder for the next time the urge appears. This is an original interactive adaptation. It does not count points, calculate a best option, or decide your values for you.

## When it feels difficult

**“I shouldn't admit that the urge has benefits.”** Understanding its attraction is part of making a realistic plan. Write the immediate payoff without endorsing the action. A smaller first step is finishing the sentence, “For a moment, this might give me…”

**“The immediate benefit still outweighs everything.”** Bring one later consequence closer with a concrete description: time lost, a repair needed, or a goal made harder. You can also make the alternative more supportive. Start with a brief delay or a request for help rather than demanding a large act of willpower.

**“I keep expanding the list and can't choose.”** Limit yourself to the few consequences that change the decision. Separate information you need from uncertainty you cannot resolve now. Your smaller step can be choosing what to do for the next few minutes rather than deciding the whole issue.

## Bring it into daily life

Keep a short comparison for one recurring, manageable urge somewhere easy to reach. Read it when you notice the cue, then update it after experience. Consequences may differ from your prediction, and that is useful information.

Which consequence becomes easier to overlook when the urge is strong, and how could you keep it in view?

“When I notice myself chasing immediate relief, I can try reviewing what acting and waiting may each cost and offer.”
