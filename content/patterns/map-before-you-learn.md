Beginning immediately feels productive, but it is easy to spend weeks moving efficiently in the wrong direction. Large subjects often contain many possible routes, prerequisites, and subskills, only some of which matter for a particular goal.

Map Before You Learn means spending a modest amount of time surveying the territory before committing deeply to one path.

Suppose you decide to learn watercolor painting. Before working systematically through the first course you find, you compare several curricula. The same themes keep appearing: water control, pigment behavior, washes, edges, value, color mixing, composition. You also notice that botanical painting, landscapes, and urban sketching emphasize these foundations differently.

The map begins to emerge.

A similar approach works in almost any field. Someone learning a language may discover that pronunciation deserves early attention. A person learning local history may realize that geography and a basic timeline make later reading easier to organize. Someone learning photography may discover that composition, light, exposure, and editing are distinct but interacting areas.

The initial map will be imperfect because beginners do not yet know enough to design the perfect curriculum. That is not a problem. The purpose is orientation, not omniscience.

A small amount of mapping can reveal missing prerequisites, irrelevant material, or a more useful order of study. It can also clarify what competent performance actually looks like, which is often less obvious than expected.

There is a danger of turning preparation into procrastination. Researching the perfect course, book list, or method can become an elaborate way of avoiding contact with the subject itself.

The map should therefore be temporary. Its job is to reduce unnecessary wandering, not eliminate uncertainty.

Look at the territory, choose a plausible route, and then start moving.
