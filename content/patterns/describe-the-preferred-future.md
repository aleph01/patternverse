## Purpose and problem

Ellie, sixteen, and her father Paul both want more trust. He imagines knowing where she is without having to chase an answer. She imagines an evening with friends that is not interrupted by repeated messages asking where she is.

Their agreement is sincere, but the word hides a disagreement about what would change. A therapist accepting it as the goal could be working toward two different destinations.

These fictional people introduce a common difficulty: “less anxiety,” “a better relationship,” and “more independence” need the meaning supplied by the people whose lives are involved.

## Core idea

**Describe the Preferred Future means developing a recognizable account of life when a wanted change is happening.**

Picture an ordinary scene: a Saturday evening, a difficult conversation, the first hour after waking. What would be different? What would the person feel or do? What would another person notice, and how might they respond? What would remain difficult even in this better situation?

The description gives the work a direction and exposes assumptions. People can describe what would matter before knowing how to achieve it. The account remains a possibility, not a prediction or promise.

## How it works

### Give a broad hope an everyday setting

Preferred-future conversations are central to solution-focused brief therapy, developed by Steve de Shazer, Insoo Kim Berg, and colleagues. The familiar **miracle question** invites someone to imagine that the difficulty bringing them to therapy has been resolved, then describe how they would recognize the difference. Its purpose is the unfolding conversation, rather than a special effect of the wording. The SFBTA manual emphasizes detailed descriptions of wanted change, including what clients and others would notice. [SFBTA treatment manual, third edition](https://www.sfbta.org/Solution%20Focused%20Therapy%20Treatment%20Manual%20for%20Working%20with%20Individuals%20-3rd%20Edition-%20final.pdf)

That invitation is one possible form. A conversation can instead explore a more manageable afternoon, a valued relationship, or a small sign that the work is useful. Someone need not imagine a problem disappearing, close their eyes, or visualize a complete scene.

### Describe what matters, not just what stops

“We would stop arguing” leaves several possible futures: people might listen, withdraw, agree under pressure, or cease contact. Asking what would happen instead helps distinguish them. The answer might include disagreement expressed without humiliation, time apart chosen freely, or a decision made together.

Recognizable does not mean externally measurable in every detail. Feeling less afraid or having room to think matters even if nobody else can see it. Concrete descriptions should clarify those experiences without replacing them with a performance for observers.

### Establish whose future is being described

The broader UCL systemic competence framework includes helping participants identify overlapping goals, working collaboratively on goals, and revising them as information emerges. This connects preferred-future work with systemic practice without implying that all traditions organize therapy around a miracle question. [UCL: Basic systemic competences](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/brain-sciences/sites/brain_sciences/files/migrated-files/Systemic_basic_systemic_competences.pdf)

Overlap does not require identical wishes. A child, parent, and school may each want something different from “a successful school day.” The therapist's preference can also enter the description, especially when a service has already defined success. An apparently shared goal needs checking with those expected to live it.

### Keep desired signs distinct from a route toward them

Leoš Zatloukal distinguishes small **signs** of progress from small **steps**: a sign describes how improvement would be recognized; a step concerns something undertaken to move toward it. His account also distinguishes broad collaborative purposes, detailed future descriptions, goals, skills, and values. These are flexible conversational forms, not a compulsory sequence. [Zatloukal: Varieties of Work with Preferred Futures](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10591-024-09725-9)

“I can disagree and still feel heard” describes a desired difference. “We will hold a weekly meeting” proposes a method. The meeting might help, but attending it would not by itself establish that anyone feels heard.

## Worked examples and case studies

### Two meanings of more trust

*Fictional teaching scenario. All people, dialogue, circumstances, and outcomes are invented.*

Paul worries when Ellie returns later than expected. Ellie says answering one message often produces four further questions. They initially locate the solution in the other person: she should become reliable; he should stop worrying.

Asked to describe a Saturday evening with enough trust, Paul imagines knowing the return arrangement and receiving an update if it changes. Ellie imagines concentrating on her friends while that arrangement stands. A sign for her would be a brief acknowledgment of an update, followed by space to continue the evening.

Asked about a delay, Ellie describes explaining that she missed a bus without being immediately accused of lying. Paul wants to be able to ask what happened without every question being treated as an attack. The therapist checks whether each recognizes the other's description, rather than declaring the two accounts compatible.

Ellie accepts that an update when plans change differs from continuous checking. Paul accepts that more information is not always more trust. They can now identify a limited overlap: clear plans, relevant updates, and discussion of delays without accusations. They have not agreed how often contact is needed or whether the return time should change.

In this fictional continuation, a later conversation starts more calmly, but Paul still wants more reassurance than Ellie considers reasonable. Their descriptions clarify the disagreement; they do not decide whose arrangement should be adopted or establish that trust has improved.

### A better future with assistance still present

*Fictional teaching scenario. All people, circumstances, dialogue, and outcomes are invented.*

Dalia lives with a progressive neurological condition. Her wife Elin helps with personal care, and a service supplies additional assistance. Dalia wants to feel like a host in her own home again. Elin wants visits to stop leaving her exhausted. With Dalia's agreement, a service worker joins the discussion. The service's written goal emphasizes greater independence.

The therapist initially asks Dalia to imagine waking tomorrow with the problem gone. She objects: her condition will still be there. The question sounds as though a worthwhile future requires becoming someone else.

The therapist acknowledges the mismatch and asks whether they could instead describe a visit that would feel worth having with her current support needs. Dalia imagines choosing the guests and food, directing the preparation, and talking at the table rather than having every conversation concern her care. Assistance would remain. What changes is her opportunity to shape the occasion.

Elin welcomes the picture but says it still appears to require her to cook, organize, and clean. Asked what she would notice in a sustainable version, she describes having enough help that she can sit with the guests too. Dalia had not intended to assign her that work and agrees that both being able to participate matters.

The service worker recognizes that “less assistance” would miss their stated aim. A revised description includes Dalia making choices and both women participating, with practical help available. Whether that help can be funded remains unresolved.

In this invented account, the future description changes before their circumstances do. It exposes a resource requirement and corrects an assumption about independence. It neither promises that symptoms will improve nor turns inadequate support into a problem the couple should solve through better communication.

### Getting through the exam, without passing it

*Published clinical vignette, adapted from Zatloukal (2025), case example 2. The paper identifies its cases as excerpts from the author's real therapeutic practice and says clients' consent was sought.*

The client was preparing for a notary examination after a previous attempt she experienced as humiliating. As the new date approached, she reported difficulties eating and sleeping, considerable stress, and outbursts affecting her relationship. She wanted to get through the exam day without repeating the embarrassment.

Zatloukal invited a miracle description focused on navigating that day without feeling embarrassed. Together they developed a sequence extending from her hotel morning through eating, travelling, waiting, taking the exam, and the period afterward. They paused to consider actions, thoughts, feelings, and interactions.

The therapist also introduced possible difficulties, including bad weather, a difficult question, and an unpleasant examiner. The client explored how her preferred way of managing would look under those conditions. The description therefore included coping with an imperfect day, rather than requiring every circumstance to cooperate.

The author reports that she felt calmer and more confident afterward, and reported better concentration and sleep at the next session. He describes her subsequently managing the examination with composure, including a question she found uncomfortable. However, she did not obtain enough points to pass and needed to retake it the following year. He reports that she nevertheless felt proud of managing the experience without the previous collapse or embarrassment. [Zatloukal: Case example 2, The Exam](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10591-024-09725-9)

This account illustrates a meaningful distinction between how someone wants to participate and the external result they also hope for. Passing remained an unmet requirement. The report does not establish that the conversation caused the reported changes, and it supplies no account of the later retake or the partner's experience. Those outcomes should not be filled in.

## Conditions for useful application

A future description needs the person's authorship, even when someone else initiates the conversation. Pictures, objects, writing, interpretation, communication aids, or additional time may help people express preferences. Fluency in an imagined scene is not a test of motivation.

Consent also concerns the goal itself. Reconciliation, shared living, forgiveness, and greater family involvement cannot be presumed desirable. Where there is coercion or abuse, one person's wish for togetherness must not overrule another's safety or freedom. NICE recommends private, one-to-one enquiry about domestic abuse in a safe setting. Joint future planning should not bypass that requirement or redistribute responsibility for harmful actions. [NICE, PH50, recommendation 6](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ph50/chapter/Recommendations#recommendation-6-ensure-trained-staff-ask-people-about-domestic-violence-and-abuse)

## When to use it

This pattern is useful when people know what they want to stop but cannot yet say what they want instead, when broad goals conceal different expectations, or when therapy risks continuing without a recognizable purpose.

Two brief fictional contrasts show the range. Bereaved siblings may want family meals where their mother's name can be spoken, rather than meals without sadness. A pupil referred for “better behavior” may describe being able to ask for a quiet break and return to class without public criticism. The preferred difference includes how others respond.

When someone needs immediate protection, medical care, or practical assistance, imagining a future should not delay that response. Some people first need their loss or present difficulty heard without being redirected toward a better tomorrow.

## Tradeoffs

Specificity can expose disagreement that broad language allowed everyone to avoid. This may improve the honesty of the work while making consensus harder. Not every desired future can be reconciled with another person's wishes.

Detailed descriptions can also become burdensome. Enough detail means enough to clarify a meaningful difference; it does not require scripting the day. Aspirations may depend on housing, care, income, access, or institutional decisions that participants cannot supply. A useful description can reveal those requirements while leaving uncertainty about whether they will be met.

## Common mistakes and limitations

**Installing the professional's ideal.** More independence, more contact, and fewer disagreements are not universal measures of a better life. Ask what each would make possible and whether it is wanted.

**Replacing a prohibition with forced positivity.** Wanting intimidation or pain to stop is already meaningful. Exploring what else would matter must not dilute that need.

**Mistaking description for commitment.** Imagining a possibility does not mean agreeing to pursue it, consenting to a particular intervention, or promising that another person will change.

**Turning signs into surveillance.** A recognizable difference should support understanding. Repeatedly counting, checking, or demanding demonstrations can undermine the very trust or freedom being sought.

**Claiming that clarity produces improvement.** This pattern offers a clinical rationale for clarifying direction. The cited framework describes competence; the vignettes illustrate practice. Neither proves that a detailed future description by itself improves outcomes.
