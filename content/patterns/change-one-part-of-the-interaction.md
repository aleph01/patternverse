## Purpose and problem

Maia describes a difficult shift at work. Owen suggests ways to handle her manager. She explains why each suggestion will not work; he offers more. Eventually she stops talking, and he feels that his effort to help has been rejected.

Both can explain this familiar exchange. They can even agree that advice sometimes arrives too soon. Yet another explanation does not necessarily give either of them a different way to participate when it happens again.

These fictional people introduce the pattern's practical question: is there one agreed part of the exchange that someone could approach differently? The change might concern a reply, who takes responsibility, how a request is made, or the arrangement surrounding the conversation.

## Core idea

**Change One Part of the Interaction means trying a specific, agreed alternative at a meaningful point in an exchange or arrangement.**

The unit of change is something people can identify: asking before advising, directing a question to the person concerned, clarifying who owns a task, or arranging protected time to answer. “Be more understanding” is a hope; a different response makes one possible expression of that hope available to experience.

Changing a contribution changes part of what others encounter. They may respond differently, continue as before, or object. The pattern offers a possibility for learning and change, not a way to control someone else's response.

## How it works

### Choose a point where an alternative matters

The chosen change should connect to something the participants want. Novelty alone is insufficient. Asking before offering advice might matter to Maia because she wants room to describe her day; using the same question mechanically could make her feel managed instead.

“One part” means an identifiable focus, not a requirement to isolate a single variable perfectly. Everyday relationships contain changing circumstances. A limited focus can clarify what was attempted without turning the encounter into a controlled experiment.

### Distinguish related therapeutic methods

Historical brief interactional work at the Mental Research Institute examined how attempted solutions could participate in maintaining difficulties, then developed tasks intended to alter those patterns. The SFBTA manual describes that history while distinguishing it from the later solution-focused emphasis on constructing wanted change and developing existing successes. [SFBTA treatment manual, third edition](https://www.sfbta.org/Solution%20Focused%20Therapy%20Treatment%20Manual%20for%20Working%20with%20Individuals%20-3rd%20Edition-%20final.pdf)

Structural family therapy gives an important place to **enactment**: bringing interaction into the session rather than relying entirely on accounts of it. Minuchin and Fishman's *Family Therapy Techniques* includes a dedicated chapter on this work. The UCL competence framework describes choosing manageable interactions, noticing difficulty carrying them out, and helping participants develop alternatives. An enactment can reveal a familiar response as well as offer an opportunity to try another. [Minuchin and Fishman: Enactment](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctvjnrtsx.9), [UCL: Specific systemic techniques](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/brain-sciences/sites/brain_sciences/files/migrated-files/Systemic_specific_systemic_techniques.pdf)

Solution-focused work may also move from a person's proposed skill or preferred difference into a concrete rehearsal, as the published case below illustrates. These approaches have different histories and explanations of change. This field guide groups their overlapping practical move without treating them as one method.

### Make the agreement usable

A workable proposal identifies who is choosing what, in which situation, and for what purpose. It also leaves room to decline, stop, or revise. A therapist might offer an idea, but its apparent elegance cannot substitute for the participants' understanding and willingness.

Agreement concerns more than the person performing the new action. Others may need to know what is changing, especially when a role or shared arrangement is affected. Someone can set a boundary on their own participation without securing another person's approval; that does not guarantee safety or entitle them to control the other person's choices.

### Include the conditions surrounding the action

A request to “wait for his answer” may require additional appointment time. A shared responsibility needs access to the relevant information. Reducing one person's contribution without arranging necessary assistance can leave a gap rather than create freedom.

The question is therefore both relational and practical: what would make this alternative possible, and what must continue to be provided?

## Worked examples and case studies

### Before the first piece of advice

*Fictional teaching scenario. All people, dialogue, circumstances, and outcomes are invented.*

Maia wants Owen to understand the pressure she is under. Owen wants to offer something useful instead of listening helplessly. They initially describe each other as dismissive and impossible to help. Their exchange also occurs when both are tired; her workplace understaffing remains a real source of distress.

The therapist asks where an alternative would make a difference. Maia identifies the first suggestion, before she has finished explaining. Owen says he cannot tell when she wants ideas. They agree that, during these conversations, he can first ask whether she wants company in the difficulty or help considering options.

They try a brief version in the session. Maia says she wants him to listen. Owen starts to suggest a phrase she could use with her manager, notices the familiar move, and stops. He asks what was hardest about the shift instead. Maia continues her account. They are trying a different reply, not requiring her to agree with his explanation of her work.

In the invented continuation, the question helps on one evening: Maia feels heard, and Owen feels less rejected. On another, she says she is too tired to choose what kind of response she needs. They agree that the question should not become another demand, and that she can say she does not know.

They have found a possible alternative, with limits. Maia's working conditions are unchanged, and Owen's own wish for attention still needs a conversation. One helpful exchange does not settle whether support in the relationship is reciprocal.

### When a new boundary has no support behind it

*Fictional teaching scenario. All people, circumstances, dialogue, and outcomes are invented.*

Jin is an adult with a motor disability who uses a communication device. His aunt Beth attends care reviews at his request and knows his daily routines. Jin wants more say in his support. Beth wants to assist without making his decisions, while the coordinator wants to finish a long agenda within the appointment.

Questions often go to Beth, who answers quickly. Jin sometimes abandons an answer he has begun composing. Initially, the difficulty is described as Beth speaking for him. The first agreed change is that she will stop answering questions addressed to Jin.

At the next review, the coordinator waits briefly, then moves to another topic. Beth keeps quiet, feeling she must obey the new rule. Jin leaves without expressing two priorities. Her withdrawal has not created usable space for him.

With Jin's permission, the therapist and coordinator examine what the change required. Directing questions to Jin was insufficient while the pace remained unchanged. Beth's knowledge could still be useful when he wanted it; treating all her contributions as interference had oversimplified the situation.

The coordinator obtains a longer review for the next occasion. Jin supplies his priorities beforehand and chooses which questions he wants Beth to help answer. During the meeting, the coordinator protects time for his responses rather than filling each pause. Beth adds details when invited.

In this invented continuation, Jin expresses a preference about weekend support and corrects a mistaken account of his routine. A transport request remains unresolved, and longer appointments have not been secured permanently. The revision changes an institutional arrangement as well as an interpersonal role. Disability-related assistance remains necessary; the service's pace is part of the work, not a failure of either Jin or Beth.

### Rehearsing a request during a computer game

*Published clinical vignette, adapted from Leoš Zatloukal (2025), case examples 1, 8, and 9. The paper describes examples from the author's real therapeutic practice and says clients' consent was sought.*

An eleven-year-old boy attends with his mother and wants less arguing and more peace at home. When the therapist explores what might be learned, the boy says that both he and his mother could learn something. The therapist starts with him and indicates that the mother will also be asked.

The mother suggests communication; the boy proposes listening. The therapist checks what listening means, distinguishing hearing someone from doing what they ask. The boy's answer includes both. This makes the proposed skill more specific, while showing why the word alone could conceal different expectations.

Drawing on the boy's interest in computer games, the therapist introduces the idea of a potion giving him a listening ability. The boy imagines playing while his mother asks him to do something. The therapist invites them to act out that exchange; the boy agrees and his mother nods.

Zatloukal reports that they developed a scene involving the game and a request to do some work, refining and replaying it several times with laughter. He interprets the rehearsal as making the skill concrete and initiating practice. He also reports that they could continue practising at home, through role-play and ordinary situations. [Zatloukal: Learning to Listen and The Superpower Potion](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10591-024-09725-9)

The account does not provide an independent measure of reduced arguing or a follow-up interval for this example. Nor does it detail the corresponding change in the mother's conduct. Those gaps matter: a child's willingness to rehearse cannot establish that responsibility for family conflict belongs to the child. The example illustrates how a proposed difference becomes an interaction people can try, not proof that rehearsal resolved the difficulty.

## Conditions for useful application

People need sufficient understanding, willingness, and practical capacity to attempt the alternative. Accessible communication and a credible way to stop matter more than completing a task as prescribed. A change agreed with someone absent from the session requires their involvement where their participation is needed; agreement should not be assumed.

Safety changes what is appropriate. Someone subjected to coercion should not be tasked with finding the response that stops another person's abuse. Refusing demands or changing contact can carry risk, so confrontation and joint experiments cannot be default recommendations. NICE recommends private, one-to-one enquiry in a safe setting. Responsibility for harmful actions remains with the person carrying them out. [NICE, PH50, recommendation 6](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ph50/chapter/Recommendations#recommendation-6-ensure-trained-staff-ask-people-about-domestic-violence-and-abuse)

## When to use it

This pattern fits when a familiar interaction is sufficiently understood, a wanted difference is clear enough, and someone is willing to explore an alternative. It is especially relevant when insight has not changed what people do next.

Two brief fictional contrasts show its range. Adult siblings replace messages relayed through their mother with a shared appointment update, with her agreement about information sharing. A couple agrees that either may pause an escalating discussion, with an understood way to arrange its continuation; the pause is not permission to disappear indefinitely or silence a concern.

If the central obstacle is an urgent medical need, missing income, inaccessible services, or immediate danger, a conversational variation may have little value without action on that obstacle.

## Tradeoffs

A focused change can make the work manageable while leaving substantial injustice or unmet need untouched. The person most willing to try something different may already carry too much responsibility. Willingness is not evidence that they should do more.

Even a small alteration can disturb an arrangement others rely on. A boundary may reveal work nobody has agreed to take over; a new role may require authority that has not been granted. Reconsidering the proposal is part of responsible work, not an admission that people failed the technique.

## Common mistakes and limitations

**Calling pressure an experiment.** A task is not collaborative because the therapist calls it optional. Refusal must be possible without humiliation or loss of support.

**Confusing an action with control over its result.** Someone can make a request clearly and still receive a refusal. The other person's response is not a performance they can guarantee.

**Removing support in the name of autonomy.** Beth's silence initially left Jin with less opportunity. Changes in roles need attention to what assistance remains necessary.

**Treating difficulty as resistance.** Time, fatigue, fear, unclear instructions, or disagreement may make a proposal unworkable. More insistence may repeat the very problem being addressed.

**Overclaiming what the attempt shows.** A rehearsal demonstrates what happened in that setting. A different evening suggests a possibility. Neither establishes a durable change or identifies a single cause of improvement. This editorial pattern is not an independently validated treatment protocol.
