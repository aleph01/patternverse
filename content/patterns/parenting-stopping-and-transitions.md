## 1. A recognizable situation

Your five-year-old is building an elaborate animal hospital. You announce that it is time to leave.

“No! I haven’t finished!”

You give them five more minutes. When those minutes end, another animal needs treatment. You explain that you must collect their sibling. They begin crying and scatter the animals when you reach for their shoes.

From your perspective, you gave fair warning. From theirs, something absorbing and unfinished is being taken away.

Changing activities involves several demands: stopping what you are doing, accepting its interruption, understanding what comes next, and beginning a new sequence of actions. A child can struggle with any part of that process.

**The goal is to help them move on with appropriate support, while gradually learning how to handle endings themselves.** They do not have to like every ending for progress to occur.

## 2. What might be happening

Start by comparing transitions rather than treating them all as the same problem.

**Stopping may be the difficult part.** Does the child resist leaving an enjoyable activity even when the next activity is also appealing? They may be absorbed, concerned about unfinished work, or reluctant to lose access to something important.

**The next activity may be difficult or unpleasant.** Moving from play to homework differs from moving from play to a favorite outing. Resistance before bathing might involve uncomfortable water, hair washing, or temperature changes. Resistance before school might involve worry about school itself.

A small experimental study involving two preschoolers illustrates this distinction: one child’s difficulties were linked to losing access to the previous activity; the other’s were linked to beginning particular tasks. That finding helps frame questions, but two children cannot establish how all transition difficulties work. [Wilder and colleagues, 2006](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1389600/).

**Uncertainty may matter.** Compare familiar routines with unexpected changes. Does the child cope better when they know where they are going, who will be there, and what happens afterward? “We’re going out” may leave important questions unanswered.

**The instruction may conceal too many steps.** “Get ready” can mean stop drawing, put materials away, find shoes, use the toilet, collect a bag, and reach the door. Watch where the child stalls. They may need help beginning or sequencing those actions.

**The transition may arrive when little capacity remains.** Hunger, fatigue, noise, crowded spaces, and repeated interruptions can make an otherwise manageable change difficult. Notice whether transitions deteriorate later in the day or after demanding settings.

Patterns suggest explanations; they do not establish intent or a diagnosis. The same child may need predictability in one situation, practical assistance in another, and help tolerating disappointment in a third.

## 3. How development changes the picture

**Toddlers and preschoolers usually need adults to organize the transition.** A child may understand “five minutes” as a warning without having a useful sense of its duration. Recognizing a picture of shoes does not mean being able to stop playing, find the shoes, and put them on independently.

Use concrete information and immediate assistance: “One more trip down the slide, then shoes.” Approach when the ending arrives and help initiate the next action. Explaining the whole afternoon while the child is distressed adds demands.

**School-age children can increasingly participate in planning.** They can help choose stopping points, learn how long getting ready takes, and make arrangements for unfinished projects. However, these skills still require teaching. Ask them to identify the next action rather than assuming they can translate a general instruction into a sequence.

Abilities involved in holding goals in mind, inhibiting an ongoing response, and switching tasks develop across childhood and adolescence. Laboratory research on these abilities helps explain developmental differences, but it does not directly prove which playground departure strategy will work. [Developmental review](https://doi.org/10.1080/15248372.2022.2160719).

**For older children and adolescents**, respect the activity’s meaning and give them increasing responsibility for its ending. A collaborative game may involve commitments to friends; music practice or creative work may need a sensible stopping point. Agree on the departure deadline, then let them propose how to meet it.

Support should follow demonstrated abilities. An older child may still need a visible sequence or a reminder. Some autistic children, children with attention difficulties, and children with communication differences need more individualized arrangements. Transition difficulty alone does not identify any of those conditions.

## 4. What needs balancing

Children need sustained opportunities to become absorbed in play, relationships, and projects. Continually interrupting them has costs. Their wish to finish something deserves consideration.

Parents also have deadlines, work, transport arrangements, other children, and responsibilities for sleep and safety. Sometimes departure cannot wait for satisfaction.

Autonomy can involve choosing *how* to stop, preserving unfinished work, or helping plan the schedule. It cannot always include deciding *whether* a necessary transition happens.

The environment deserves scrutiny too. A school day with frequent room changes may be demanding. An activity that automatically starts another round makes its endpoint harder to manage. A family timetable that permits no margin leaves little room for learning.

Where possible, reduce avoidable transitions and unnecessary waiting. Where change is necessary, make the demands understandable and achievable.

## 5. Possible responses

The options below combine professional guidance, limited intervention research, and practical synthesis. Much of the direct research concerns small studies involving people with developmental disabilities; evidence about ordinary family transitions and lasting independence is thinner.

### A. Make the change understandable

**This fits when** unexpected timing, unclear plans, or difficulty understanding spoken instructions appears central.

Explain both the ending and what follows. For a young child, use a short sequence: “Blocks, shoes, car.” A photograph or familiar object may communicate more clearly than a detailed verbal timetable. For an older child, a written plan or shared calendar may be more appropriate.

Teach the cue during an easy transition. Show what the timer or picture means and help the child carry out the next step. A tool cannot organize behavior if its meaning has never become clear.

Give useful notice rather than a stream of countdowns. If plans change, identify the change explicitly: “We expected the park. It is closed, so we’re going home.”

The [National Center for Pyramid Model Innovations](https://www.challengingbehavior.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/helping-children-transition.pdf) recommends warnings, visual information, and simple sequences for young children. These are professional recommendations, not guarantees.

The research is mixed. A [review of advance-notice interventions](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4711751/) suggests that notice may be particularly relevant when unpredictability drives difficulty. It may accomplish little when the child already knows what will happen but strongly objects to it.

Likewise, a [2025 review focused on visual schedules](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/bin.70028) found insufficient evidence that the visual-schedule component itself caused improved transitions in autistic participants. Schedules were commonly combined with other interventions. This qualifies claims about their independent effects; it does not show that visual information is useless.

### B. Help the child reach a credible stopping point

**This fits when** interruption, unfinished work, or loss of access seems to drive the reaction.

Where time permits, identify a bounded endpoint: finish this page, complete the current round, or put the final animal to bed. Decide this before the deadline, with a latest finish time.

Help preserve what matters:

- Put an unfinished construction somewhere it can remain intact.

- Save the game or document.

- Leave a note showing where to resume.

- Arrange a realistic opportunity to continue.

A photograph may preserve a memory, but it does not preserve a construction. Ask which matters to the child rather than assuming the photograph solves the problem.

For a younger child, a familiar toy may accompany the move. An older child may prefer to manage saving and closing independently.

[Raising Children Network’s guidance](https://raisingchildren.net.au/toddlers/behaviour/behaviour-management-tips-tools/activity-changes-behaviour-management) recommends natural breaks and limited choices during activity changes. These are sensible practical options supported by professional guidance; strong evidence for each individual tactic is limited.

“Finish this” also needs definition. A child can keep expanding a project indefinitely. Respecting its importance does not require an unlimited extension.

### C. Make the next activity accessible and reduce the work of getting there

**This fits when** resistance clusters around particular destinations, tasks, or complicated preparations.

Examine the next activity directly. Does homework require skills the child lacks? Does getting dressed involve an uncomfortable garment? Does leaving mean entering a noisy corridor?

Then change the relevant obstacle. Prepare materials before calling the child away. Give one actionable instruction instead of “Get ready.” Help with a difficult fastening rather than repeatedly reminding them to hurry.

At school, possible adjustments include leaving before a crowded corridor fills, knowing who will meet the child, or keeping essential materials in a predictable place. These should be discussed with staff and assessed in practice.

[NAEYC’s transition guidance](https://www.naeyc.org/resources/pubs/yc/sep2018/reducing-challenging-behaviors-during-transitions) recommends examining unnecessary changes, preparing materials, and teaching the skills required for the next activity. These recommendations are professional synthesis rather than proof of one universal transition routine.

For autistic children with significant distress, [NICE guidance](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/QS51/chapter/quality-statement-7-assessing-possible-triggers-for-behaviour-that-challenges) specifically recommends assessing communication, pain, anxiety, sensory conditions, and environmental changes. Its scope is autistic children and young people; it supports investigating those possibilities rather than assuming all resistance requires firmer enforcement.

### D. Support follow-through and practise a manageable transition

**This fits when** the child understands the change and can perform its steps, but still needs help acting while disappointed.

At the endpoint, approach, briefly acknowledge the particular loss, and give the next action:

> “You wanted to keep building. We need to leave for pickup. Put the animals here, and I’ll help with your shoes.”

Then help with that action. Repeated explanations, new threats, and expanding negotiations can make departure longer without teaching anything new.

Offer choices that remain available: carrying the bag or holding your hand, saving the file themselves or asking for help. Avoid asking whether they want to leave when that decision is already made.

Outside the pressured moment, rehearse one manageable ending during ordinary life. Let the child help choose a cue and practise saving, putting something aside, or requesting a brief finishing step. Gradually reduce assistance when they demonstrate readiness.

This is practical skill-teaching, not an independently established treatment sequence. Structured parenting programs can help some families with substantial behavioral difficulties: a randomized trial involving 180 autistic children aged three to seven found benefits from a coached parent-training program compared with parent education. It tested the full program, not this dialogue or transition rehearsal alone, and did not establish equivalent effects for everyday reluctance across ages. [Bearss and colleagues, 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25898050/).

## 6. Examples in practice

### A three-year-old who leaves while still upset

Before the final playground activity, a parent says, “One more slide, then we walk to the gate.” Afterward, the child cries and asks to climb again.

The parent says, “You wanted more climbing. We’re leaving now,” and helps them begin walking.

The child continues crying. The transition has happened with support; emotional recovery takes longer. The parent does not require the child to appear happy before considering the departure successful.

### A seven-year-old whose visual plan fails

A picture sequence shows play, bath, and story. The child follows it until bath time, then becomes distressed.

Instead of making a larger chart, the parent investigates. The child dislikes water reaching their face during hair washing.

They agree on a more tolerable washing arrangement and explain which nights hair washing is needed. The pictures conveyed the plan accurately. They could not make the anticipated discomfort acceptable.

### A fourteen-year-old who repeatedly misses departure time

A teenager agrees to leave at five but starts a collaborative online activity shortly beforehand. The first attempt at using an alarm fails because they silence it.

Later, they examine the sequence together. The teenager proposes a cutoff for joining new sessions and a separate alarm for saving and getting ready.

They try that arrangement. The parent checks at the agreed time rather than repeatedly calling from downstairs. If it still fails, they revisit the activity’s duration and the support needed.

## 7. Common mistakes and limitations

**Assuming a warning should remove disappointment.** Knowing an enjoyable activity will end does not make its ending welcome.

**Adding more instructions when the child is already struggling.** A detailed explanation can obscure the immediate action. “Put the pen down and save your page” may be more usable than another speech about punctuality.

**Turning every ending into a negotiation.** If an extension is possible, define it. If a deadline is fixed, keep choices within that constraint.

**Treating every adjustment as rewarding resistance.** Some patterns of postponement can maintain difficulties, but stopping to address pain, confusion, or an inaccessible task serves a different purpose. Observe what changes rather than assigning a motive.

**Practising flexibility through unnecessary surprises.** There is no strong basis here for deliberately disrupting enjoyable activities to “toughen up” a child. Necessary changes already provide learning opportunities. Additional practice should be manageable and transparent.

**Withdrawing a useful support merely because of age.** A checklist or visual reminder can support independence. Success need not mean remembering everything without tools.

**Equating fewer visible protests with better wellbeing.** Many transition studies measure observable behavior. Less protest alone does not establish reduced anxiety, greater trust, or lasting ability to manage unfamiliar changes.

## 8. What to observe over time

Choose one recurring transition and notice:

- Whether the child understands what is happening.

- Where the sequence stalls.

- How much prompting or practical help is needed.

- How intense distress becomes and how recovery goes.

- Whether the child increasingly saves work, requests help, or proposes workable endings.

- Whether improvements appear with other caregivers and in other settings.

Distinguish stopping the activity, reaching the next one, and recovering emotionally. Those can improve at different rates.

Reconsider the explanation if warnings increase anticipatory distress, if difficulties occur mainly before one destination, or if the child manages at school but struggles after an exhausting day. Those patterns may point toward different supports.

Seek help from the child’s clinician, school, or an appropriate developmental professional when transitions repeatedly prevent attendance or ordinary participation, involve aggression, self-injury, or running into danger, or require levels of adult assistance the family cannot sustain.

A sudden change, signs of pain, or loss of previously established abilities warrants assessment. Persistent difficulty deserves attention without requiring a parent to decide on a diagnosis first.
