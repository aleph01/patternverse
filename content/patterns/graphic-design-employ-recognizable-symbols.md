A visitor leaflet puts a branded rosette beside “Visitor information.” It fits the identity but could indicate a featured attraction or simply the organization. Its drawing supplies little help finding the service.

Use a convention the intended audience has reason to know, with meaning consistent with the service or action. Identifying what a symbol depicts differs from understanding what it does. A familiar object can still have several interpretations.

## What to notice

![Two matching Common visitor guides show the same museum photograph and Visitor information label. Before, an eight-point rosette appears beside the service text. After, a circled lowercase i replaces it in the same icon window. All copy, typography, image crop, positions and paper presentation remain fixed.](../assets/graphic-design/symbols-information.svg "Only the symbol changes, brand rosette→conventional information cue. Frame, text and imagery stay fixed; neither drawing is a certified standardized sign.")

[Full-size comparison](../assets/graphic-design/symbols-information.svg) · [Stacked narrow-screen view](../assets/graphic-design/symbols-information-mobile.svg)

Replace the rosette with a circled lowercase i in the same 80-unit window, retaining color, placement and “Ask at the entrance desk.” Hours, address and photograph stay fixed.

The symbol offers a familiar information association, confirmed by the words. It is less distinctive, which can be useful for practical help; the rosette can sign the brand elsewhere. The i is not universal, and this leaflet says nothing about a desk sign’s visibility across a room.

Nielsen Norman Group distinguishes icon recognizability from interpretation. W3C’s supplemental cognitive-accessibility guidance recommends familiar, clear symbols with attention to cultural context; it is not a standalone conformance test.

## Two variations

### Make icon and action agree

![Two matching phone-page mockups show Studio review 2026, the same studio photograph, PDF metadata and Download PDF button. Before, the button’s tray-and-arrow icon points upward. After, its arrow points downward. The icon window, button, label, image, device and lighting remain fixed.](../assets/graphic-design/symbols-download.svg "Only the arrow changes from upload to download. Tray, symbol window, Download PDF label, metadata and interface stay fixed.")

[Full-size comparison](../assets/graphic-design/symbols-download.svg) · [Stacked narrow-screen view](../assets/graphic-design/symbols-download-mobile.svg)

Replace an upward arrow with a downward arrow entering the same tray beside “Download PDF.” Preserve the icon window, button and file details. Do not rotate the whole mark, which would invert the tray.

The cue and words now propose retrieval rather than upload, consistent with established interface usage documented by Canada’s design system. The PDF’s eight pages and 1.2 MB size still require words. A labeled text button could suffice; a decorative icon must not create a duplicate announcement in the live control.

### Name the service behind the object

![Two matching collection leaflets show the same library photograph and headphone pictogram. Before, the space beside the pictogram is blank. After, Audio guide appears there. Ask at the desk, Free to borrow, hours, website, icon, typography and layout remain unchanged.](../assets/graphic-design/symbols-audio-label.svg "Only Audio guide appears in reserved space. Symbol and layout stay fixed; the icon is not a standardized accessibility-service mark.")

[Full-size comparison](../assets/graphic-design/symbols-audio-label.svg) · [Stacked narrow-screen view](../assets/graphic-design/symbols-audio-label-mobile.svg)

Reveal “Audio guide” beside the unchanged headphone pictogram, keeping “Ask at the desk · Free to borrow” and opening hours. The object suggests listening; the label identifies this particular offer.

The words cost room and translation. Headphones still do not specify languages, transcripts or hearing-device compatibility. An already learned icon-only system might need less explanation, but a first-time visitor leaflet cannot assume that exact association.

## Useful when

Use familiar symbols to support finding services, recognizing states or anticipating ordinary actions. Start from the intended meaning and relevant medium, then protect the features carrying the convention while adapting its styling.

A learned download cue differs from a new bridge metaphor for collaboration. Some conventions began figuratively, but the practical question is how much fresh interpretation this audience needs. A visible label can confirm the association and supply precision the symbol lacks.

## Watch out for

Making every cue proprietary can erase its useful familiarity. Conversely, a recognizable symbol with the wrong meaning creates a semantic contradiction, not merely a style problem.

Avoid pictograms beside every sentence or for unusual concepts plain words express better. Evaluate both what people see and what they expect in the actual layout. Familiarity depends on experience and context; a designer’s successful guess is not audience evidence.
