Mara wants to understand why asking for help feels so difficult. She begins describing an evening when she badly needed company, then laughs and adds that everybody has problems. Her therapist invites her to stay with the sadness. Mara becomes more articulate, more agreeable, and harder to reach. She explains that she knows she should let herself feel it.

The invitation has become another task she can perform correctly. What has not yet received attention is the effort that keeps the sadness manageable, and what Mara expects would happen if that effort stopped.

A different beginning would make room for the laugh, the explanation, and the reluctance to need anyone. Something in this response may be doing an important job. Before asking it to change, the two people could discover whether that job can be understood and supported.

## The difficulty this pattern addresses

A person can sincerely want change and also rely on the response they want to change. Someone longs to speak freely while carefully monitoring every sentence. Someone wants to stop overworking while expecting rest to expose them to criticism. Someone wishes they could cry but anticipates that crying will make it impossible to leave a relationship.

From outside, the response may look like an obstacle. From within the situation, it may be the available means of preserving connection, dignity, composure, safety, or the ability to get through the day. Its costs can be substantial without making its function meaningless.

This creates a particular difficulty in experiential work. A therapist notices that a person changes the subject whenever grief approaches and tries to bring them back. The person becomes more distant. The therapist concludes that more encouragement is needed. Each effort to restore contact adds to the pressure that makes distance necessary.

The difficulty can also arise inside the person. They condemn themselves for being guarded and then become guarded about that condemnation. An internal demand to be open can reproduce the same struggle as an impatient helper. Even apparently kind language can carry the demand: “I am being compassionate toward this part so it will finally let me move on.”

Support What Protects changes the relationship to this impasse. It makes the managing response a possible subject of collaboration, rather than treating it as an interruption that must first disappear.

That possibility remains a hypothesis. A pause is not automatically avoidance, analysis is not automatically a defense, and tension is not automatically a stored emotional history. Someone may be thinking, experiencing pain, finding the question irrelevant, or declining the therapist's proposal. A useful account begins with what the person actually notices and recognizes. It does not require them to accept a protective explanation.

## The core move

**Recognize the function a response appears to serve, and offer support that preserves the person's agency while making its effort, concerns, or conditions more available for exploration.**

Support can take several forms. A therapist might help someone articulate the feared consequence of letting go, agree that a subject will remain closed, or share an effort through a carefully negotiated experiment. A person might develop a less hostile relationship with their own caution. Sometimes the fitting support is practical: a clear ending time, privacy, a more comfortable position, or a reliable way to pause.

The organizing question concerns what must remain possible. If staying composed allows someone to leave the session and collect their child, support includes taking that obligation seriously. If a joke lets them approach something painful without feeling exposed, support might mean recognizing both the humor and the exposure it manages. Neither case requires deciding that the protection is obsolete.

Supporting a function is different from prescribing a strategy forever. A response can preserve something important through a costly means. Constant self-criticism may be someone's attempt to prevent humiliation; recognizing that concern does not establish that humiliation is inevitable or that criticism is the best protection. The work can hold the concern and the cost together.

Nor does support require treating an internal warning as a fact. “If I need you, you will control me” can be an emotionally compelling expectation worth understanding. It is still an expectation, rather than proof of another person's intention.

A decisive feature is what happens when support does not lead to greater openness. Can the person keep the protection, reject the formulation, or stop? If every acceptable outcome involves dropping the defense, the offer contains a hidden condition. This pattern becomes more credible when the therapist's conduct demonstrates that refusal changes the work.

## How the experience unfolds

The following movements describe possible developments, rather than steps that must occur in order. The comparison across approaches later in this section shows how differently they can be organized.

### Recognize the managing response without settling its meaning

What first becomes noticeable may be small: a smile after an angry sentence, a sudden shift into explanation, or an impulse to make sure the therapist is comfortable. Description leaves more room than interpretation. “You began explaining their reasons just then” can invite inquiry; “You are defending against your anger” already announces a conclusion.

The person may recognize the shift, offer another explanation, or find that attending to it is unwelcome. Their answer affects whether there is anything to explore. Curiosity about protection should not become a requirement to justify ordinary behavior.

### Make room for the concern behind continuing

When the person recognizes a managing effort, its stakes can become more specific. “I cannot let go” might develop into “If I start crying, you will assume I want to go back,” or “If I sound angry, I will become unfair.” These formulations distinguish what is feared from the mere presence of emotion.

Support becomes concrete when it addresses that specificity. Agreeing that tears will not determine a relationship decision differs from saying that it is safe to cry. Agreeing to revisit a proposed experiment later differs from repeatedly reassuring someone until they consent now.

Sometimes the relevant concern is about the therapist's actual behavior. The therapist may have interrupted, offered an interpretation too quickly, or treated a tentative wish as a treatment goal. Acknowledging and changing that contribution may matter more than exploring the client's history.

### Discover whether support fits

A supportive intention does not establish a supportive experience. Giving permission to keep a boundary may feel relieving, patronizing, or implausible. Speaking a protective phrase aloud may feel accurate in one tone and threatening in another. Even a request for feedback can become burdensome if the person feels responsible for reassuring the therapist.

The experiment therefore includes correction. The person can specify different words, ask for more distance, or choose no experiment at all. The therapist can accept that a promising idea did not fit without recasting the objection as another layer to get through.

### Notice what becomes possible, including a clearer limit

When support fits, several outcomes are possible. The managing effort may become less exhausting. A different feeling may emerge alongside it. The person may understand the cost more clearly, or become more definite about maintaining the boundary.

A change from polite agreement to an unmistakable “no” can be meaningful. So can discovering that a response has no recognized protective meaning. The outcome worth attending to is more accurate contact and usable choice. Relaxation, tears, and apparent emotional depth do not establish those outcomes by themselves.

### How different approaches implement the move

These accounts compare selected expressions of the pattern. The core-pattern family is an editorial synthesis; the approaches retain their own theories and purposes. All encounters below are fictional illustrations, not reports from the linked sources or complete treatment demonstrations.

#### Hakomi: support the way experience is being managed

Hakomi provides the most direct expression of the title. Ron Kurtz describes taking over as support for an existing way of managing experience. His account includes both bodily support and voicing an internal management message, with permission. The person observes what happens in mindfulness. The proposed opportunity is that some effort can be shared, allowing other responses to become noticeable without requiring the person to abandon control. [Kurtz's account of taking over](https://hakomi.com/a-little-history/).

Imagine someone who silently repeats, “Keep it together,” whenever disappointment appears. They recognize the phrase and are curious about hearing it voiced by the therapist. The first attempt sounds stern. The person immediately objects: “It isn't angry. It's frightened.”

They stop to clarify. The phrase is trying to ensure that they can leave on time and return to work. With the person's agreement, the therapist tries a quieter version, using the person's wording and concerned tone. The person notices both how tiring the warning is and how relieved they feel that somebody has understood the obligation. Disappointment becomes a little more available, while the agreement about time remains in force.

The useful feedback is the correction and its consequences. A dramatic delivery would have misrepresented the response. Continuing despite the objection would have added another demand to stay composed. The experiment becomes informative because the person can shape it.

The Hakomi Institute's overview emphasizes matching the supported action precisely and following the client's pace. Its clinical illustration also includes physical intervention. That historical example does not make hands-on support interchangeable with the verbal example here. Bodily techniques require appropriate professional competence and ongoing consent; this article does not teach pressure, restraint, or interventions affecting breathing. [Hakomi method overview](https://hakomiinstitute.com/overview-of-the-method/).

#### Internal Family Systems: build a relationship with protective concerns

Internal Family Systems organizes this work through relationships among parts and Self. Its model outline gives priority to protective parts, their fears, and negotiated pacing. The framework treats protective intentions as meaningful even where behavior has become extreme. These are assumptions and working concepts of IFS, rather than conclusions that every client must adopt. [IFS model outline](https://ifs-institute.com/resources/articles/internal-family-systems-model-outline).

In a fictional encounter, a person wants to revisit a painful separation but immediately becomes absorbed in analyzing the former partner. They also feel impatient with the analysis. Attending only to the wish to go deeper would miss the conflict already present.

The analytical response fears that feeling affection will lead to reconciliation. The impatient response fears spending another year circling the same story. Neither concern is solved by declaring the other irrational. The person and therapist establish that feeling something will not commit them to contacting the former partner. The analytical response remains uncertain, and they spend the rest of the session understanding that uncertainty rather than pursuing the separation scene.

The support here is a relationship and a negotiated boundary. No one performs the analytical action on the person's behalf. The therapist's willingness to remain with the concern matters more than obtaining an immediate concession.

IFS training objectives explicitly include unblending, attention to protector fears, and obtaining permission before approaching exiled experience. Within this framework, appreciating a protector is part of building trust; it should not be presented as a courtesy before inevitable access. [IFS Level 1 learning objectives](https://ifs-institute.com/sites/default/files/inline-files/L1-15%20LOs%20nocereview%20Final%208.2.21_0.pdf).

#### Coherence Therapy: encounter the necessity of the symptom

Coherence Therapy's contribution is a shift from opposing a symptom to discovering the emotional learning that makes it necessary in the person's world of meaning. Its account emphasizes experiential discovery of that learning. Supporting protection, in this comparison, means taking a possible necessity seriously enough to investigate it without first arguing the symptom away. [Coherence Therapy overview](https://www.coherencetherapy.org/discover/what.htm).

Consider a fictional person who repeatedly postpones submitting finished work. Encouragement to tolerate imperfection has not fit: they are comfortable making mistakes in private. What feels dangerous is becoming visible as capable. In an agreed imagined situation where they submit the work promptly, they notice dread about being assigned every difficult task afterward.

The formulation becomes more precise: remaining underestimated preserves the possibility of refusing without disappointing anyone. The person feels both recognition and frustration. They want recognition for their ability, and they rely on concealment to keep demands manageable.

The therapist does not respond by declaring visibility safe. The feared demand may be realistic in this workplace. They distinguish the emotional rule that recognition removes the right to refuse from the practical fact that a manager sometimes overallocates work. Those are related concerns that may require different responses.

Ecker and Hulley's published panic case illustrates their inquiry into the symptom's necessity and the distinction between wanting relief and experiencing a reason the symptom must continue. The submission example here is invented and makes no claim about treating all procrastination this way. Discovery of a compelling necessity is also not, by itself, evidence that the learning has changed. [Coherence Therapy case account](https://www.coherencetherapy.org/files/CaseEx-PanicAttacks.pdf).

#### Gestalt therapy: inhabit the actual position, including reluctance

Arnold Beisser's paradoxical theory of change challenges attempts to force someone into a preferred version of themselves. Its relevance here is permission to encounter the position already being lived, including reluctance, rather than perform the opposite. This is a theoretical orientation, not a guarantee that acceptance automatically produces change. [Beisser's original essay](https://gestalt.org/arnie.htm).

Imagine a person who says, “I want to be more spontaneous,” while sitting carefully and asking what exercise they should do. When the therapist proposes an experiment, they agree quickly. Asked how the proposal actually lands, they admit that they dislike it and think refusing would make them a disappointing client.

The useful subject becomes the agreement happening between them. The therapist acknowledges how readily the invitation became an expectation. The person decides not to do the exercise. They stay with the difference between wanting more freedom and being willing to demonstrate freedom on demand.

Here, supporting protection does not involve endorsing the belief that disagreement always threatens a relationship. It involves making room for the person's actual reluctance in this relationship. A first expression of greater freedom might be declining the proposed route toward it.

Yontef and Simkin describe a combination of awareness, experimentation, acceptance, and honest contact with limits. That matters because a Gestalt expression of this pattern need not satisfy every request or share every protective strategy. The therapist can remain engaged while being clear about their own participation. [Gestalt therapy introduction](https://gestalt.org/yontef.htm).

#### Inner Relationship Focusing: include what seems to interrupt

Ann Weiser Cornell's account of radical acceptance brings apparently obstructive experiences into the Focusing relationship. She describes attending to the emotional quality within criticism rather than routinely setting the critic aside. The contribution to this pattern is inclusion: the response that seems to interrupt may itself be something needing company. This describes a particular development of Focusing, rather than every Focusing teacher's method. [Cornell's account](https://focusingresources.com/the-radical-acceptance-of-everything/).

In a fictional encounter, someone notices warmth toward a friend and immediately thinks, “Don't be pathetic.” They then criticize themselves for being unkind. Trying to welcome the warmth while eliminating both criticisms creates a crowded struggle over which experience may remain.

With support, they become interested in the urgency carried by the first phrase. “Pathetic” initially sounds contemptuous; closer attention brings a worried quality. They sense a fear of offering more affection than the friendship can hold. The second criticism is also impatient for a life with less self-punishment. Neither response has to be expelled for the wish for closeness to matter.

The emerging formulation is ordinary and tentative: they want contact and are frightened of misjudging its welcome. That fits better than either the initial insult or a demand to become more accepting. Nothing requires an immediate decision about what to say to the friend.

The support lies in the manner of being with these responses. There is no need to perform a protective voice externally or to secure permission for a predetermined next task. What was called an interruption becomes part of the available experience.

#### Individual Emotion-Focused Therapy: make self-interruption available to work with

Individual Emotion-Focused Therapy supplies a related but distinct case. The Greenberg Institute describes self-interruptions as originally protective ways of blocking emotion and describes a two-chair task that activates the interrupting process and its painful impact. The task aims toward greater access to blocked emotional experience. It therefore overlaps with this family without being identical to Hakomi taking over or an open-ended decision to maintain protection. [EFT account of self-interruption](https://www.greenbergiseft.ca/eft-two-chair-self-interruption).

Imagine someone whose sadness is followed by a rapid instruction to get on with things. In an agreed chair task, they give that instruction a voice and then encounter what receiving it is like. The command feels protective against becoming overwhelmed, while its repeated effect is loneliness: distress is never allowed to have company.

The important distinction is that concern and impact can both enter the work. Understanding why the instruction developed does not require leaving its harshness unexamined. Equally, the therapist should not treat the ability to voice it as consent to pursue every feeling it has been containing.

This fictional illustration leaves the outcome open. The person might recognize a need for comfort, decide the task is too exposing, or need a different pace. The named source outlines a clinical task; it does not establish that all emotional restraint calls for that task. This is individual Emotion-Focused Therapy, distinct from Emotionally Focused Couple Therapy.

#### Comparing the implementations

The resemblance concerns the significance given to an apparently obstructive response. The differences concern what is offered and what the work is trying to accomplish.

| Approach | Expression of support | Distinction to preserve |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hakomi | Share a recognized management effort through an agreed experiment. | Taking over is more specific than general validation. |
| Internal Family Systems | Relate to protective concerns and negotiate the pace. | Permission involves an ongoing relationship, not a one-time clearance. |
| Coherence Therapy | Investigate the experienced necessity of a symptom. | Discovering necessity differs from changing the learning that sustains it. |
| Gestalt therapy | Make the actual position available in awareness and relationship. | Acceptance can coexist with honest limits. |
| Inner Relationship Focusing | Include the felt quality of criticism or apparent interruption. | Inclusion does not require assigning every experience a protective role. |
| Individual Emotion-Focused Therapy | Encounter an interruption and its impact within a defined task. | The task has a change aim that differs from simply supporting continued management. |

These distinctions prevent the unifying pattern from swallowing the methods it brings into conversation. A therapist sharing effort, negotiating with a protector, or helping enact an interruption may look equally attentive while pursuing different immediate purposes.

## A worked example

The following fictional encounter develops the opening situation. It combines the core pattern's relational and verbal possibilities rather than demonstrating one school's protocol.

Mara describes needing company after a difficult medical appointment. She considered calling her sister, then spent the evening reading online instead. When she reaches the moment of wanting to call, she laughs and says that her sister has enough to deal with.

The therapist asks whether she can stay with how lonely the evening felt. Mara agrees. Her voice becomes smooth and explanatory. She outlines her family history, names her tendency to minimize, and says she understands that vulnerability is important.

The therapist notices a mismatch between her earlier hesitation and this fluent account. They ask how the invitation felt. Mara says it was fine, then adds, “I suppose I'm not doing it.”

This is a consequential moment. Encouraging her to try harder would make the session another place where she must deliver the right response. The therapist acknowledges that the request may have sounded like an instruction to produce sadness. They offer to leave that request aside. Mara can continue talking, change subjects, or explore what made the invitation difficult.

After a pause, she says, “If I start crying, you'll want to comfort me.” The therapist had imagined that comfort was obviously desirable. Mara explains that comfort often arrives with insistence: someone asks her to stay, call more often, or explain why she did not tell them sooner. Being upset can make it harder to keep ownership of what happens next.

The therapist first offers, “You don't have to let anyone in.” Mara frowns. The sentence sounds like confirmation that she should remain alone. The proposed support has preserved one concern while losing another.

They stop and revise the understanding. Mara does want company. She wants company without being required to surrender decisions. “I can want you here and still decide what happens,” she says. The therapist checks whether that describes what matters in this room too. It does.

The support now becomes an agreement with consequences. Mara need not tell more of the medical story. Comfort will not mean unsolicited advice or a decision to contact her sister. The therapist will check before proposing a different direction. The session still ends at the agreed time. These are specific commitments the therapist can follow, rather than a promise that difficult feelings will have no consequences.

Mara notices an internal phrase: “Don't make it a whole thing.” She would like to hear it aloud. They agree to try it once and stop for her response. The therapist's first delivery is too brisk. Mara corrects the tone: the phrase is anxious, as if trying to keep a small request from becoming an obligation for everyone.

With her agreement, the therapist repeats the revised phrase. Mara notices how much work goes into keeping every need small. There is sadness, but also irritation. “I shouldn't have to make a perfect request before somebody can sit with me.” She does not cry. The therapist does not redirect the irritation toward tears.

What changes is the available distinction. Mara can want contact without treating contact as a contract. She can also recognize that the warning has been trying to preserve something she still values: the right to choose. They finish by checking how the session has been for her. She says that correcting the therapist's comforting sentence mattered more than hearing the warning aloud. Her objection had changed the encounter.

Nothing here proves that she is now comfortable asking her sister for company, or that the family history has been established accurately. The immediate outcome is narrower: a protective concern became speakable, an offer of support was corrected, and Mara retained authorship of the work while encountering more of what she wanted.

## Why this movement may help

One plausible contribution is a reduction in the struggle added by the helping situation. If someone expects their caution to be challenged, they may spend the encounter preparing to defend it. When the caution can remain, attention may become available for its details, its costs, and other wishes. This is a clinical rationale for the editorial pattern, not an established account of a single mechanism shared by all six approaches.

A second contribution is more accurate information. “I am afraid of sadness” may turn out to mean “I am afraid that sadness will be taken as consent.” Those concerns invite different responses. Without the correction, repeated encouragement to feel could address the wrong difficulty and inadvertently reproduce it.

A third contribution is a different relational experience. Mara is able to object, and her objection changes the therapist's behavior. The significance comes from what actually happens between them. A declaration that all parts are welcome would carry little weight if the therapist continued toward the same destination after every refusal.

A fourth possibility is a change in how effort is distributed. In the Hakomi rationale, welcome support may reduce the burden of maintaining an action alone. In other expressions, a shared agreement may make it less necessary to monitor every moment for an unwanted demand. Whether either occurs must be assessed through the person's response, rather than inferred from the therapist's technique.

These possibilities can support discovery without settling the larger question of change. Feeling understood may matter while the response remains necessary. A meaningful session may have no immediate effect on a longstanding symptom. The sources establish recognizable clinical concepts and rationales; they do not validate this synthesized pattern as a standalone treatment or demonstrate that every protective response will soften when supported.

## Conditions, pitfalls, and limits

### Support has to be specific enough to be credible

Broad reassurance can miss the concern. If the fear is losing the right to leave, kindness alone may not help; a respected ending can. If the difficulty involves being misunderstood, more warmth may be less important than a corrected formulation. Support is judged partly by whether it addresses what the person says is at stake.

The therapist also has to be able to offer what they propose. Promising unrestricted availability, perfect safety, or complete control over emotional aftereffects can create expectations the relationship cannot meet. Clear limits can be part of trustworthy support.

### Protection is a possible function, not an explanation for everything

People can decline because they dislike an exercise, disagree with an interpretation, or have different goals. Their refusal need not conceal vulnerable material. Repeatedly asking what a refusal protects can make ordinary disagreement impossible.

Similarly, a posture or physical symptom does not establish psychological causation. Discomfort, illness, disability, sensory needs, medication effects, and fatigue can affect what someone can do. Practical accommodation and appropriate care remain relevant even when an experience also carries personal meaning.

### Respect the concern while examining costs and consequences

Understanding protection need not erase harm. An angry outburst may be experienced as a way to prevent humiliation while frightening other people. A therapist can take humiliation seriously, maintain a limit around threats, and help explore other responses. Supporting the person does not require participating in intimidation.

The same distinction applies inwardly. A harsh warning can have a recognized purpose and a painful impact. Both deserve attention. Replacing condemnation with compulsory gratitude can silence the person being hurt by their own response.

### Do not make acceptance a performance requirement

Someone may hate a protective response, feel nothing toward it, or find parts language alien. Asking for compassion too soon can create another standard they cannot meet. The frustration also belongs in the conversation.

Nor should support become an endless preliminary stage. A person may reasonably want direct work on a behavior, skills, or a practical decision. This pattern can inform that work without requiring a prolonged search for hidden protective meaning. Collaboration includes the chosen treatment goal and a discussion of whether the current focus is helping.

### An objection must be able to change the plan

An apparent yes can coexist with uncertainty or habitual accommodation. What matters is not a single assent but the continuing opportunity to revise the agreement. A person who becomes quiet, distant, or unusually compliant may need a pause and a direct check; the therapist cannot infer permission from the appearance of calm.

A no can remain a no. If every refusal initiates further negotiation, the invitation to choose is hollow. This is particularly important when the therapist's expertise or authority makes disagreement difficult.

### Current conditions may need to change

A response may be managing an ongoing problem rather than an outdated expectation. Careful speech around a punitive manager, for instance, may reflect a realistic assessment. Understanding an inner response cannot substitute for attending to power, material constraints, or actual consequences.

In some situations the most useful support is external: a boundary, an accommodation, a different arrangement, or help deciding which risks are acceptable. The article's focus on experience should not turn a difficult environment into a failure of emotional openness.

## Its place in the wider process

[[hear-the-whole-response|Hear the Whole Response]] makes room for the different wishes, fears, and objections involved in an encounter. **Support What Protects** takes a more particular interest in what allows a person to manage it and whether that function can receive fitting support. [[discover-what-makes-the-response-understandable|Discover What Makes the Response Understandable]] follows the meanings and expectations that emerge. These movements can overlap; understanding can precede support or arise through it.

[[find-a-workable-amount-of-contact|Find a Workable Amount of Contact]] concerns how much experience can be approached and from what distance. Supporting protection may help establish those conditions, but their purposes differ. Changing the amount of contact does not necessarily recognize the concern that makes contact difficult.

[[give-experience-a-form|Give Experience a Form]] can make a protective message or action available through words, gesture, or a symbolic scene. Giving it form is an expressive choice; supporting it concerns the relationship to its function. A person can voice a warning while attacking themselves for having it, or receive support without enacting anything.

[[make-a-new-experience-possible|Make a New Experience Possible]] may become relevant when the person encounters support that differs from what they expected. [[let-the-change-register|Let the Change Register]] attends to whatever difference is actually experienced. Neither requires claiming that the protection has disappeared. In Mara's encounter, the significant difference was that her correction counted.

The earlier method-oriented articles give more space to specific implementations: [[taking-over-the-defense|Taking Over the Defense]], [[getting-permission-from-the-protector|Getting Permission from the Protector]], [[unblending|Unblending]], and [[two-chair-dialogue|Two-Chair Dialogue]]. They remain distinct techniques within the broader collection.

## Expressions and sources

Support What Protects names an editorial family of moves, rather than a technique formally shared by all the approaches discussed. Their differences remain clinically and conceptually important. The linked sources describe their own frameworks; the comparisons and fictional encounters are original educational synthesis.

Ron Kurtz's [A Little History](https://hakomi.com/a-little-history/) gives the clearest source for Hakomi taking over, including verbal work and permission. Cedar Barstow's [Overview of the Method](https://hakomiinstitute.com/overview-of-the-method/) supplies a clinical account and the rationale for carefully matching an existing effort. These sources explain a trained method, not a set of physical instructions to reproduce from this article.

The IFS Institute's [Model Outline](https://ifs-institute.com/resources/articles/internal-family-systems-model-outline) and [Level 1 learning objectives](https://ifs-institute.com/sites/default/files/inline-files/L1-15%20LOs%20nocereview%20Final%208.2.21_0.pdf) ground the account of protective concerns, unblending, pacing, and permission. Parts and Self are presented as model-specific concepts.

The Coherence Psychology Institute's [overview](https://www.coherencetherapy.org/discover/what.htm) and Bruce Ecker and Laurel Hulley's [panic case account](https://www.coherencetherapy.org/files/CaseEx-PanicAttacks.pdf) ground the distinction between consciously wanting relief and experiencing a necessity for the symptom. The article does not infer a neural mechanism or lasting symptom change from a moment of recognition.

Arnold Beisser's [Paradoxical Theory of Change](https://gestalt.org/arnie.htm) and Gary Yontef and James Simkin's [Gestalt Therapy: An Introduction](https://gestalt.org/yontef.htm) ground the discussion of actual positions, awareness, acceptance, and contact with limits. Ann Weiser Cornell's [The Radical Acceptance of Everything](https://focusingresources.com/the-radical-acceptance-of-everything/) grounds the narrower account of including criticism in an Inner Relationship Focusing process.

The Greenberg Institute's [account of two-chair work with self-interruption](https://www.greenbergiseft.ca/eft-two-chair-self-interruption) supplies the individual Emotion-Focused Therapy comparison. Its task orientation is retained rather than recast as identical to supporting a defense. All examples in this article are fictional, and no interactive practice is included.
