A paragraph can contain a clear claim and reliable evidence while leaving the connection between them unexplained. A statistic appears, a quotation follows, and a conclusion is announced. The reader may see why the material interests the writer without seeing why it supports this particular conclusion.

This pattern helps when evidence is presented as though its meaning were self-evident, readers draw a different inference from the same material, or the argument depends on an assumption that remains hidden. **Write the relationship that lets the evidence count as support.**

That relationship is sometimes called a _warrant_: the reasoning or assumption connecting the evidence with the claim. It may be straightforward enough to leave implicit. Where the connection is uncertain or consequential, making it visible gives readers something they can assess rather than merely accept.

## Identify what the evidence establishes

A piece of evidence can support several possible lines of inquiry. Its presence does not determine which conclusion follows.

Imagine a hypothetical museum reviewing directions to its cloakroom. During one Saturday observation, a volunteer follows the first twenty new arrivals. Twelve ask where the cloakroom is at the first corridor junction. The existing direction sign is farther down the right-hand corridor, beyond the point where visitors must choose which way to go. These counts and circumstances are invented for this example.

An initial paragraph reads:

> Twelve of twenty new arrivals asked where the cloakroom was. We should move the direction sign.

The claim is visible, and the observation is relevant to finding the cloakroom. The connection to moving the sign remains incomplete. Why would a different location address these questions? Why not change the wording, provide a floor plan, or have a volunteer answer them?

A developed version makes the relationship available:

> Twelve of twenty new arrivals asked about the cloakroom at the first junction. The existing direction appears only after that choice, farther down the right-hand corridor. Put a direction at the junction so visitors can use it when they decide which way to go.

The added reasoning concerns timing and location: information intended to guide a choice needs to be available where that choice is made. The observation identifies a decision point at which people seek guidance, and the sign's location explains why they cannot use it there.

That is more than repeating the number. It gives the proposed change an inspectable basis.

## Expose the assumption where it matters

The museum paragraph assumes that a direction at the junction could help visitors who are seeking guidance there. It does not establish that every question results from the current sign or that a new sign would remove all need for assistance.

Readers may have relevant information that challenges the assumption. Perhaps many visitors prefer asking someone, or the junction has so much competing information that another sign would be difficult to notice. Those possibilities bear on the proposed connection.

Making the reasoning explicit does not guarantee agreement. It clarifies what disagreement would need to address. Someone can accept the observation while questioning whether the proposed intervention is likely to help.

If the writer needs to support the assumption further, additional observation or a comparison may be necessary. A more confident connective cannot replace that work. The useful result of exposing a gap may be a revised conclusion or a question to investigate.

## Distinguish analysis from repetition

An explanatory sentence should add what the reader needs to understand about the evidence. Restating it with an emphatic adjective often leaves the same gap.

_These figures clearly show a significant problem_ says little about the role of the sign. _Visitors are requesting directions before they can see the current sign_ identifies the relation that matters to the proposal.

Likewise, adding _therefore_ between the observation and the recommendation labels an inference without supplying it. A reader still needs access to the premise on which the label depends.

The bridge need not be long. Where the spatial arrangement is already established, a sentence may be enough. Where the evidence is complex or several explanations are plausible, more analysis may belong. Length follows from the reasoning the reader needs, rather than a quota of commentary after every quotation or number.

## Make interpretive connections visible too

The same task arises when evidence consists of language or conduct rather than counts. A quotation does not come with a single interpretation attached.

Consider this separate invented passage from a fictional story:

> “Our workshop,” Mara said. Leon put the tools on the bench. She pointed out where each one should go, then slipped the new key into her pocket.

An essay might claim that the passage makes shared ownership look uncertain. Simply quoting _our workshop_ would not explain why. The plural phrase appears to affirm sharing.

The analysis needs to identify the contrast: the language names a shared place, while the arrangement of tools and possession of the key centre practical authority on Mara. That contrast is the reason the details bear on the claim about uncertainty.

The interpretation still depends on how those actions function in context. Pocketing a key does not by itself prove exclusion, establish legal ownership, or reveal a complete motive. Another passage might show Mara giving Leon a second key. The writer needs to examine the relationship the text supports rather than treating one object as an automatic verdict.

Explaining the inference lets the reader consider that alternative. It is stronger than assuming the quotation speaks for itself.

## Recognize the value judgment within a recommendation

Practical arguments often connect evidence to a choice through an objective or a value. In the museum example, giving visitors usable directions at their decision point is treated as worthwhile.

If another proposal prioritizes a clear historic interior over adding signs, that concern cannot be settled by repeating the count. The disagreement includes what the arrangement should optimize and what alternatives can meet those aims.

A writer can state the objective and explain how the evidence bears on it. Hiding the objective inside a supposedly inevitable conclusion makes the choice harder to assess.

The claim may remain modest: place a temporary direction at the junction and examine what changes. The bridge explains why that is a relevant response to the observation. The appropriate confidence, cost, and wider scope remain further questions the argument may need to address.

## Stop where explanation becomes needless

Some links are already clear to the intended reader. Explaining every ordinary connection can make prose laborious or patronizing. A familiar procedure within a specialist audience may need less unpacking than the same proposal for a mixed group.

The aim is to expose the consequential joins, especially where the reader could reasonably infer something else. It does not require attaching a formal reasoning label to every sentence.

This pattern also does not organize the whole argument or certify the evidence's origin. It explains why particular support is relevant to a particular claim. A source can be accurately attributed and still irrelevant; a relevant fact can still be connected to the conclusion by reasoning that needs further support.
