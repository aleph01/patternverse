**Musical foundation · Chapter 13 of 49 · Part 3: Notes, Keys, and Chords**

**Use when:** A hummed idea loses its shape when moved to another instrument, register, or starting note.

A melody moves from E to G, then to A. Calling that movement “up a little, then up again” preserves the general contour but loses the distances that identify the gesture. Interval names and keyboard positions let another musician reconstruct the same idea in a different register or key.

## The foundation

An interval is the distance between two notes. It is a building block for understanding melody, rather than a rule that every melody should preserve its distances. The practical application here is to compare a phrase with a moved version and a changed version. Moving every note by the same amount is transposition; choosing when to transpose a whole song belongs to [[songwriting-circle-of-fifths|Transpose the Song to Fit the Voice]].

Track the distances and directions between notes so a melodic gesture survives a new starting pitch.

Keep the seed's one–one–two rhythm and move E4–G4–A4 to A4–C5–D5. Both rise by three semitones, then two. Compare this with A4–B4–C5: its rising contour is similar, but the smaller distances make a different gesture. Each of these counted phrases lasts four beats. The guided Lean on Me study above makes the same comparison with a recognizable melody. Begin by hearing the size and direction of the moves; their formal names are explained below.

Preserving only 'up, then up' loses interval size; preserving intervals alone still leaves rhythm and delivery to decide.

## Read the keyboard as a continuous sequence

Pitch describes a sound's perceived highness or lowness. An interval describes the distance between two pitches. This foundation uses the twelve equally spaced semitone steps within an octave that underlie the course's keyboard examples. Other tuning systems exist; this is a working scope, not a claim about all music.

The natural note names are A, B, C, D, E, F, and G. On a keyboard they repeat in each octave. Black keys occur in groups of two and three, which help locate the white keys. C sits immediately to the left of a group of two black keys.

Adjacent keyboard pitches are one semitone apart, including E to F and B to C. There is no black key between either pair. A whole tone spans two semitones. A sharp raises a written pitch by one semitone, and a flat lowers it by one. Those signs do not simply mean “use a black key”: E-sharp is the same keyboard pitch as F in this tuning.

![A labeled octave shows natural notes, semitone gaps, and the E–G–A seed.](/diagrams/songwriting/keyboard-and-intervals.svg 'Count every adjacent keyboard pitch when measuring semitones, including the white-key pairs E–F and B–C.')

Names matter because notation describes relationships as well as keyboard locations. F-sharp and G-flat can sound on the same key while having different roles in a written scale or chord. The circle-of-fifths pattern will use this distinction without requiring advanced spelling rules.

## Count distance in two ways

For a first pass, being able to count half steps is enough to continue to [[songwriting-build-a-scale|Build a Scale from Steps]]. The names below help explain how musicians describe the same distances.

From C to E spans three letter names inclusively: C, D, E. That makes it a third. The distance is four semitones, so it is a major third. C to E-flat is also a third by letter names but spans three semitones, making it minor.

C to G is a perfect fifth, seven semitones. C to the next C is an octave, twelve semitones. “Major,” “minor,” and “perfect” here name interval qualities; they are not judgments about whether an interval is successful or pleasant.

The seed E4–G4–A4 therefore rises by a minor third and then a major second. Its overall span, E4 to A4, is a perfect fourth. A writer can preserve those distances starting elsewhere: A4–C5–D5 has the same interval pattern.

## Hear the difference between contour and distance

Bill Withers' [Lean on Me, official audio](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gOZgo8gMIoM) provides a familiar context. In the local keyboard reduction, the upper melody begins C–C–D–E–F. The repeated C stays at the same pitch. C–D and D–E each rise two semitones; E–F rises one. Describing all three moves as “up a step” would hide that difference. The [licensed piano/vocal/guitar score preview](https://www.musicnotes.com/sheetmusic/bill-withers/lean-on-me/MN0072685) supplies a reference for the C-major setting and stepwise material; the local player uses a short provider arrangement with its own timing.

The guided listening study separates the melody from its accompaniment before comparing transformations. Moving every melody note up two semitones begins the gesture D–D–E–F♯–G. The starting pitch changes, but the repeated note and the two–two–one ascent survive. A different version keeps the C start and raises each F to F♯. Its broad shape is still recognizable, while E–F♯ now spans a whole tone. These alternate versions are teaching transformations of the reduction, rather than additional Withers performances.

The original E4–G4–A4 seed makes the same distinction with a leap. Moving it to A4–C5–D5 preserves a minor third followed by a major second; A4–B4–C5 preserves only the broad upward direction. The song passage and the seed therefore answer the same practical question at different scales: which distances make this gesture itself? Their actual note patterns remain distinct.

## Register is a separate choice

C4 means middle C in this course. C5 is the C one octave higher; C3 is one octave lower. Some software labels octaves differently, so a collaborator should confirm the convention rather than assume an octave number always refers to the same register.

Moving every note of the seed up an octave changes its register without changing its interval relationships. A singer may find the higher version harder, and an instrument may sound quite different there. The compositional identity can remain recognizable while the physical delivery changes.

Moving only its last A4 up to A5 creates a much larger leap. That is a melodic alteration rather than moving the entire phrase to a new register. The distinction becomes useful when a chorus needs a focal point but the rest of the melody should remain comfortable.

## Use distance to explain a choice

An original answer to the seed is G4–E4–D4. It reverses the broad direction while preserving the pattern of a third followed by a second. The rhythm can remain one–one–two beats. Similarity in distance and timing connects the gestures even though their directions differ.

Interval vocabulary is particularly helpful when transposing, diagnosing an awkward leap, or discussing a melody without replaying it constantly. It can become a distraction when a clear sung example already communicates the idea. The label should support hearing rather than replace it.

The E–G–A seed and its counted variations are original; the Lean on Me study identifies its repertoire source separately.
