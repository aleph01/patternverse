### A statement inside its own scope

Someone insists that no one should tell other people what to do. The statement expresses a concern about control, but it also tells other people to refrain from a kind of behavior. If its scope includes every instruction, it appears to include itself. The difficulty may be resolved by clarifying what the person means: perhaps they object to coercive direction, unsolicited advice, or instructions from people without relevant responsibility. The self-application brings missing boundaries into view.

Robert Dilts calls this pattern Apply to Self in Sleight of Mouth. A statement is examined using the relationship or criteria it expresses. A rule about language is applied to the language of the rule; a standard for criticism is applied to the criticism that states it. This makes the statement an instance within the domain it claims to govern, rather than allowing it to hover outside that domain without explanation.

Some cases reveal a contradiction, but contradiction is only one possible result. A principle may survive its own test, need a qualification, or expose an exception that requires justification. A self-referential question can also make a belief easier to observe without refuting it. Asking how difficult it would be to revise a belief about the difficulty of revising beliefs creates a new object of inquiry, but the answer could remain that revision is difficult.

### Finding the scope a rule requires

An editor tells writers that every sentence should be short, then explains the policy in a sentence containing several qualifications. The policy's own expression suggests why an absolute rule may be too crude. Some ideas require relationships that are difficult to express through a sequence of very short sentences. A more defensible standard might ask writers to keep sentences as clear as the content permits and vary their length appropriately. Self-application exposes a need that the original rule ignored.

A workplace example concerns transparency. A committee requires every decision to have a written rationale, but the transparency policy itself was adopted without one. Applying the standard to the policy reveals a missing justification. The committee can document its reasons or explain a principled exception. The rule is not necessarily wrong because its creators failed to follow it. Their inconsistency shows a gap in compliance; whether the policy is sound requires further examination of its purposes and effects.

That distinction matters in personal conversation as well. Someone who criticizes contempt while speaking contemptuously may be inconsistent, but their observation about another person's contempt can still be correct. The pattern does not license dismissing a valid criticism because the speaker has a similar fault. It asks whether the standard is being applied consistently and whether the speaker's own conduct also deserves attention. Both questions can be relevant without one cancelling the other.

### Cleverness can obscure the conversation

The easiest version of this pattern is a quick retort. A person says they dislike judgmental people, and someone immediately points out that this is a judgment. The response may be technically relevant and still fail to understand the concern. The person may be using judgmental to mean habitual contempt, rather than every act of evaluation. Treating ordinary language as though it were a perfectly specified formal rule can manufacture contradictions that disappear when the meaning is clarified.

A productive self-application therefore begins by reconstructing the claim fairly. It distinguishes a universal statement from a shorthand expression and gives the speaker room to specify its scope. This avoids turning the pattern into a contest in which the person quickest to find a verbal inconsistency appears to have settled the underlying issue. A better result is a formulation that the participants can evaluate and use.

There are also legitimate limits on self-application. A policy about how customers should use a service need not apply identically to the software implementing it. A request directed at one role may not govern every role. The question is whether the statement claims a scope that includes itself, or whether applying it to itself reveals a useful analogy rather than a formal requirement. Making that distinction explicit prevents reflexivity from becoming an all-purpose objection.

### A principle that can account for its own use

Apply the Rule to Itself helps when a statement exempts itself without explanation or a broad principle seems too easy to announce. Look for the Exception tests it against another relevant case. Inspect How You Know examines the basis for accepting it. Put a Frame Around the Belief introduces a separate interpretation of why the rule is held, whereas self-application uses the rule's own terms to investigate it.

The most useful outcome is often a better principle. The editor gains a standard that can accommodate clear complexity. The committee brings its own decisions within the transparency it expects. The person concerned about control can distinguish coercion from requests and advice. A rule that can explain its own application is easier to trust, partly because its boundaries are visible rather than being improvised whenever the rule becomes inconvenient.

## Pattern details

### Use when

- A broad rule appears to exempt itself.
- Self-application may reveal missing scope or inconsistency.

### Goals

- Clarify a principle’s boundaries.
- Distinguish contradiction from noncompliance and useful qualification.

### Mechanisms

- self-reference
- consistency checking

### Not ideal when

- A clever retort would misrepresent ordinary language or distract from a valid concern.

### Difficulty

medium
