**Pattern 22 of 49 · Part 4: Melody and Hooks**

**Use when:** A tune feels lifeless over its chords, or a substitution changes the phrase in a way the chart does not explain.

The note A sounds settled over an F-major chord and more colorful over C major. Its pitch has not changed. Its relationship to the notes beneath it has. A melody therefore needs to be heard both as a line and as a series of vertical relationships with harmony.

## The move

Keep a melodic note fixed while comparing harmony that settles it, colors it, or delays its resolution.

Hold the seed's final A4 for two beats. Hear it first against C–E–G, then against F–A–C. Its duration and pitch stay fixed; its relation to the backing changes from added sixth to chord tone. Choose whether that moment should remain colored or feel more supported. Then reconnect it to the full phrase. This isolates a useful decision before replacing the melody and all the chords at once. The passing-note and suspension examples below offer other ways to shape the same stability–movement relationship.

A note outside the named chord is not automatically a mistake; duration, placement, approach, and the desired effect matter.

## Hear the seed in context

A chord tone belongs to the underlying chord. A non-chord tone lies outside the chord as currently described. The second term does not mean wrong note. It asks how the note arrives, how long it stays, and where it goes next.

Porch Light's E4–G4–A4 seed first appears over C. E and G belong to C–E–G. A is an added sixth above C, giving a different color during its two-beat duration. Over F–A–C, A becomes a chord tone while E and G create other relationships.

A writer can use that change without replacing the melody. The second appearance may seem more settled at its end because the harmony now supports the held A directly. This is an original example of harmonic context developing a repeated idea.

The simple chord chart does not necessarily name every passing vocal note. A chart marked C may still accompany a singer using A. If the accompaniment is meant to sustain A as an important part of its voicing, C6 may become a useful instruction. Chart detail depends on what performers need to reproduce.

## Connect stable notes with motion

An original phrase E4–F4–G4 over C can use F as a passing note between two chord tones. Its stepwise movement and relatively short duration help explain its role. Hold the F for a full bar and the relationship changes: it becomes a prominent tension that deserves a different account.

A neighbor figure leaves a note by step and returns, such as E4–F4–E4. These descriptions concern how the line moves. They are useful patterns, not permission slips that legalize only certain pitches.

Rhythmic position also matters. A brief note between beats may attract less attention than the same note on the strongest accent. That difference can be useful when a lyric needs either smooth motion or a pointed emphasis.

## Delay a resolution

Consider F4 sounding over an F chord, then continuing as the harmony changes to C. When the melody moves down to E4, the earlier F has been held across a change and then resolved into the C triad. This prepared 4–3 relationship is a classic suspension model.

A chord symbol such as Csus4 requests a suspended chord quality, usually replacing E with F. The symbol does not by itself guarantee the melodic preparation and resolution just described. The chord name and the voice-leading event are related but distinct.

The important experience is the timing of the move. Resolving F to E immediately creates one phrase; delaying it can extend expectation. Never resolving can establish a persistent suspended color. The actual song determines whether an arrival is wanted.

## Reharmonize a held note

A sustained E4 can belong to C major, Am, and Fmaj7 in different ways. Over C it is the third, over Am the fifth, and over Fmaj7 the major seventh. The same vocal note can therefore connect a progression while the harmony changes around it.

This can help a repeated melodic note remain expressive. It can also introduce a clash if a substituted chord contains a close incompatible pitch. Changing F to Fm brings A-flat into the accompaniment; a held A natural in the melody now requires deliberate consideration.

The most useful question is what relationship is wanted at that moment: stability, movement, friction, or ambiguity. Neither “always use chord tones on strong beats” nor “add tensions everywhere” is a universal songwriting rule. Those strategies can be studied and compared without becoming compulsory.

The numbered Porch Light note comparisons are original. Technical chord vocabulary connects to Hamm's [Triads](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/triads/) and Ableton's [Seventh Chords](https://learningmusic.ableton.com/advanced-topics/seventh-chords.html). ## Love Is a Losing Game: follow the voice through a harmonic change

Amy Winehouse's **Love Is a Losing Game**, in the [Back to Black album recording](https://music.apple.com/us/song/1422677786), places an exposed vocal against changing accompaniment. Follow a complete opening vocal phrase, listening beyond each new chord to where the sung line continues and settles. The melody remains a line with its own pacing, rather than a succession of notes selected only because a new chord has begun.

That gives the original held-note comparison a real listening context. Over C–E–G, the seed's final A is an added sixth; over F–A–C, it belongs to the triad. The note stays fixed while its support changes. Those labels describe Porch Light, not a supplied transcription of Winehouse's phrase. In the recording, an exact chord-tone claim would require identifying the sounding melody and harmony at the same moment. The broader writing decision is already available: let the line continue across a change when that continuity serves the sentence, and choose deliberately where the accompaniment should make it feel supported or unsettled.
