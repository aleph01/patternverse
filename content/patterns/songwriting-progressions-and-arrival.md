**Pattern 19 of 49 · Part 3: Notes, Keys, and Chords**

**Use when:** A pleasant loop never seems to arrive, or a firm chord close stops a phrase that needs to keep moving.

A sequence of chords can keep circulating pleasantly without ever feeling like a conclusion. Another sequence can create a strong arrival in only a few changes. The difference is not simply the number of chords or how unusual they are. Their order, duration, bass motion, melody, and placement within a phrase all matter.

## The move

Coordinate chord order, change rate, and phrase endings to make a passage settle, stay open, or continue.

Compare the verse's final two bars as G–G, G–C, and G–Am, keeping the earlier phrase fixed. For the C ending, let the tune settle on C as well; then compare a continuing melody over the same chords. Decide whether the next section needs openness or a close. Only then compare longer and shorter chord durations. Platform Three can instead mark arrival with the full groove's return: it does not need a functional cadence just because the vocal example uses one.

A cadence belongs to a phrase in context; a dominant-to-tonic pair inside a loop need not end anything.

## Give movement a context

A chord progression is a succession of harmonies. Harmonic rhythm is the rate and pattern of their changes. A cadence is an articulated phrase ending, often involving recognizable harmonic and melodic relationships. A repeating loop and a cadence can coexist, but they are not the same thing.

In C major, a C chord can establish home. A move toward F or Dm can lead elsewhere, and G or G7 can point back toward C. This tonic–predominant–dominant vocabulary is especially useful in functional tonal contexts. Pop, rock, and modal writing do not all use the same directional grammar in every passage.

A G chord at the end of a phrase may feel open because the surrounding music has made C an expected destination. G followed by C can support arrival. The same pair inside a continuing loop may not feel like the end of anything because rhythm, melody, and repetition encourage continuation.

An arrival is therefore more than a chord label. The singer's phrase, a sustained bass note, a rest, or an arrangement change can make the boundary clearer.

## Compare three endings

Porch Light's verse ends with two bars of G. That leaves a useful openness before its chorus begins on F. The transition does not immediately resolve G to C; instead it redirects the next section. The result depends on the phrase connecting those events.

A firmer alternative changes the final two bars to G followed by C and lets the melody settle on C. Another alternative moves G to Am. In a C-centered functional setting, the latter can defer an expected tonic return while retaining some shared tones between Am and C.

| Ending    | Chords | Possible role in this example                     |
| --------- | ------ | ------------------------------------------------- |
| Open      | G · G  | Leave room for a next section                     |
| Settled   | G · C  | Support a definite C-centered close               |
| Deflected | G · Am | Continue rather than complete the expected return |

These are original options, not fixed emotional definitions of the progressions. A melody that strongly asserts C over Am may complicate the apparent deflection; a rapid G–C inside a busy phrase may not sound final.

## Change how long a chord stays

The full verse gives each of C, Am, F, and G two bars. A quicker alternative gives each one bar and repeats the cycle. The eight-bar length remains, but the harmonic activity doubles. A line may now encounter two different chord changes where it previously rested over one harmony.

A slower alternative holds C for four bars, Am for two, then F and G for one each. The arrival rate accelerates near the end. This can create direction without adding new chord types.

The musical question is whether the melody and words benefit from that motion. A dense line may need the support of a stable harmony; a held note may become more expressive as chords change beneath it.

## Let the loop do its own work

Platform Three initially stays around D with a persistent bass reference. Its development does not require a classical cadence every few bars. The return of a full rhythmic texture can mark arrival instead. Harmonic stability can give another dimension room to change.

The common mistake is applying one harmonic grammar as a universal rule. Another is using functional names so loosely that every chord becomes “tension” or “release” without explaining its relation to the actual phrase.

## Mozart: an arrival can leave room to continue

The opening of Mozart's **Piano Sonata in C major, K 545, first movement**, gives harmonic arrival a scale smaller than a whole song. In the [score and MIDI edition](https://www.mutopiaproject.org/cgibin/piece-info.cgi?id=998), the opening four bars return to C harmony, with E in the upper melody at the end of bar four. The phrase has a point of repose, but its melodic ending on the third does not make the entire movement feel finished. Continuing music can follow a local arrival.

That is the useful connection to Porch Light. Returning to a tonic chord and writing a decisive final ending are related choices, not the same decision. A verse can reach C and still invite the chorus through its melody, rhythm, or incomplete thought. The miniature's sustained final C makes a more explicit closing choice. Mozart's accompaniment and formal language differ from the course's pop examples; the comparison concerns the degree and scale of arrival. Read the four bars in the linked edition rather than treating a timestamp from another pianist's performance as a fixed measure boundary.
