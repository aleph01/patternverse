*Build a performance in the intervals between photographs.*

A skeleton picks up a sword. A wooden face begins to speak. A lump of clay hesitates, looks embarrassed, and changes its mind.

These objects cannot perform those actions by themselves. Moving them with a visible hand would break the illusion, while strings or mechanisms might restrict the movement.

Stop motion gives the filmmaker time to intervene between images. The animator arranges a pose, takes a photograph, changes something, and photographs it again. Played in sequence, the separate arrangements become a performance.

**Replacement animation** adds another possibility: exchange a physical part—or an entire figure—for another version shaped for the next moment.

Imagine a sequence of poses in which a puppet’s arm progresses from resting to raised.

Between the photographed poses, an animator has reached into the set and adjusted the puppet. The camera records only the arrangements selected for the finished movement. The handling happens outside those exposures.

A few widely separated poses would only sample the movement. A finished wave would usually contain additional positions, with their spacing chosen to shape the gesture.

Another intervention changes the expression. Instead of reshaping the puppet’s mouth, the animator can attach another mouth piece before taking the next photograph.

Both methods construct change between exposures. [Dragonframe’s illustrated introduction to stop motion](https://www.dragonframe.com/introduction-stop-motion-animation/) demonstrates the basic process with ordinary physical objects.

## Two ways to change the subject

An articulated puppet usually contains an **armature**: a framework whose joints can be adjusted and hold their positions. The animator moves its limbs, torso, or head incrementally.

Other materials allow different approaches. Clay can be reshaped. Paper cutouts can slide or pivot. Everyday objects can move across a tabletop.

Replacement animation uses a prepared series of alternatives. A puppet might have interchangeable heads, mouths, hands, or body shapes. Each replacement supplies a particular stage of an expression or action.

The choice depends on the performance. A joint works well for bending an elbow. A replacement can provide a dramatic mouth shape or deformation that would be difficult to obtain by manipulating the same piece.

The two approaches often operate together: pose the body, change the expression, photograph the result.

### Puzzle one: how do miniature skeletons fight live actors?

In *Jason and the Argonauts* (1963), Ray Harryhausen’s skeleton warriors attack Jason and his companions.

The skeletons were articulated miniatures, animated to correspond with previously photographed human action. The finished images combined the models with the live-action performances.

Harryhausen describes rehearsing the fight with numbered stunt performers representing the skeletons. The choreography established where the opponents would be and how their attacks would connect. During animation, he had to keep the skeletons’ movements synchronized with the actors’ movements.

When several skeletons appeared together, each frame required many coordinated adjustments. Harryhausen recalled days producing only about thirteen frames. [Harryhausen’s recorded account, British Entertainment History Project](https://historyproject.org.uk/interview/ray-harryhausen)

The important trick is more precise than making a model move. An animated sword must arrive where the human performance expects it. Timing and contact make separately created actions feel like one encounter.

The National Galleries of Scotland’s [Harryhausen feature](https://www.nationalgalleries.org/art-and-artists/features/art-and-ray-harryhausen) includes photographs of his creatures and original design work.

### Puzzle two: how can Jack Skellington sing with a rigid head?

In *The Nightmare Before Christmas* (1993), Jack’s mouth and expression change throughout his dialogue and songs.

The filmmakers prepared replacement heads representing different expressions and mouth positions. Animators selected and exchanged them as the performance progressed.

David Bossert’s production account describes nearly four hundred different expressions. Each was drawn, sculpted in clay, molded, cast in resin, and painted. Duplicate sets supported the production’s multiple puppets. [Bossert: puppet fabrication and photographs of Jack’s replacement heads](https://davidbossert.com/assets/article-nightmare_before_xmas-puppets.pdf)

This shifts part of the animation work into fabrication. A sculptor creates a library of possible expressions; the animator determines how those expressions unfold through time.

A speaking performance also needs transitions. Moving between a closed mouth, a rounded vowel, and a smile requires choices about how long each shape appears and how it relates to the character’s head and body.

### Puzzle three: how does Norman pull such an exaggerated face?

Early in *ParaNorman* (2012), Norman makes a “zombie face” while brushing his teeth.

According to the film’s production account, the sequence used **150 replacement faces**. Different physical pieces supplied the changing shapes, while the puppet’s body was animated through the action.

LAIKA had already developed 3D-printed replacement faces for *Coraline* (2009). Digital design and printing allowed artists to manufacture extensive sets of physical expressions for photography. The [LAIKA and Museum of Pop Culture exhibition](https://www.laikahiddenworlds.com/coraline) shows examples of Coraline’s facial system.

*ParaNorman* also used mechanical facial systems for some characters. Its zombies had flexible faces manipulated by mechanisms underneath. Puppet supervisor Georgina Hayns explains that the appropriate system depended on the character and the qualities its face needed. [Focus Features: *ParaNorman*’s puppet and replacement techniques](https://www.focusfeatures.com/article/two__years_in_the_making)

A printed face remains a physical object placed under the set’s lighting. The technology supplies more controllable shapes; the photographed performance still depends on their selection, placement, and timing.

## Replacement can involve the whole figure

The technique extends beyond facial animation. George Pal’s *Puppetoons* used multiple figures and parts to create movement through replacement.

A sequence of differently shaped bodies can make a rigid material appear to bend, stretch, or dance. The National Film Institute’s [George Pal retrospective](https://nfi.hu/en/budapest-classics-film-marathon-2024/screenings-2024/programme-sections-2024/george-pal-a-cinema-of-miracles) describes this approach.

The general principle is useful whenever the desired change is easier to prepare as several objects than to obtain by deforming one.

## The performance lives in timing and spacing
Small movements alone do not produce convincing animation.

**Timing** concerns how long an action takes. **Spacing** concerns how far the subject changes between images.

Closely spaced positions can ease a hand toward a delicate touch. Larger changes can produce a sudden strike. A held pose can suggest anticipation, thought, or disbelief.

At a playback rate of 24 frames per second, animation “on ones” can introduce a new pose every frame. Animation “on twos” holds each pose for two frames, yielding twelve pose changes per second while retaining 24-frame playback.

Those are creative choices. The rhythm of the performance matters alongside its smoothness.

## What can expose the trick?
A loose joint can drift after the animator releases it. A replacement face can sit slightly differently from its predecessor. Unintended changes in clothing, lighting, or background objects can become distracting flicker.

Physical registration—the repeatable placement of replacement parts—is therefore crucial. So is preserving everything that should remain still.

Modern capture tools help animators compare the current view with earlier frames, including through translucent overlays called **onion skinning**. [Dragonframe explains these frame-comparison tools](https://www.dragonframe.com/introduction-stop-motion-animation/).

The process remains demanding because a small error may affect a long sequence of carefully coordinated changes.

## When the pattern is useful
Stop motion lets filmmakers give physical materials impossible behaviour while retaining their photographed texture, lighting, and presence. It suits creatures, puppets, clay, paper, food, tools, and ordinary objects.

Replacement animation is especially useful for precise expressions, repeatable shapes, and transformations that exceed a puppet’s mechanical range.

## How it relates to neighbouring tricks
**[Substitution splices](/pattern/film-substitution-splices)** use a replacement to create an instantaneous change within apparently continuous action. Replacement animation can supply a whole progression of changes that reads as movement.

**Time-lapse** samples change occurring over time. Stop motion usually constructs the change between exposures.

**[Motion control](/pattern/film-motion-control-and-repeatable-camera-movements)** can advance the camera in coordinated increments, allowing the viewpoint to travel while the animator builds the subject’s performance frame by frame.
