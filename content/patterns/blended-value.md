## What it is for

Use this pattern when financial performance alone gives an incomplete picture of what a business creates—and when social or environmental goals need to influence everyday commercial decisions.

A repairable phone can generate revenue, help customers keep their devices longer, and reduce demand for replacement materials. Its production also affects workers, communities, and the environment.

Those effects belong to the same business model.

Blended Value brings them into one view. It asks the business to manage financial, social, and environmental performance together, including the tensions between them.

The aim is a viable enterprise whose contribution extends beyond the money it earns.

## The core idea

Every organization produces a mixture of economic, social, and environmental effects. Blended Value makes that mixture explicit and uses it to guide decisions about products, operations, investment, and growth.

Jed Emerson’s framework treats these dimensions as connected components of value. It applies to businesses, nonprofits, and hybrid organizations. [Jed Emerson: The Blended Value Framework](https://www.blendedvalue.org/framework)

As a business pattern, this means building desired outcomes into how the company earns and operates.

A business might generate revenue by recovering useful materials, employ people excluded from conventional hiring, or design products that customers can maintain for longer.

The strength of the model depends on the connection between its commercial activity and its intended contribution. That connection needs to survive difficult decisions about costs, prices, suppliers, and expansion.

## How it works

The business identifies a useful connection between what customers will pay for and an outcome that matters beyond the transaction.

Recovering discarded materials might create a saleable input. Better working conditions might require a different sourcing arrangement. A durable product might serve customers through repair and spare parts as well as initial sales.

The business then gives those outcomes practical influence: through product requirements, purchasing rules, budgets, incentives, ownership, and reporting.

This influence matters when choices become uncomfortable. A commitment to worker welfare has little operational meaning if purchasing targets consistently make it impossible for suppliers to improve pay or conditions.

Blended Value therefore involves both design and accountability. It connects the commercial mechanism to the intended outcome, then checks whether that outcome occurs.

## Famous example: Fairphone

Fairphone sells electronics while working on product longevity, materials sourcing, and conditions in its supply chain.

Its offering includes repair support and spare parts. Its impact reporting describes efforts involving recycled and responsibly sourced materials, worker representation, and living-wage bonuses at supplier factories. [Fairphone: Impact Report](https://www.fairphone.com/impact-report)

The blend appears in the product and its production. Customers buy a functioning device; the business also seeks to change how that device is made and how long it remains useful.

The commercial tension is substantial. Supporting longer ownership requires parts, software, service, and a viable business even when customers replace devices less frequently.

The environmental outcome also depends on actual use. A repairable phone creates more benefit when customers repair it and keep it longer. Repairability is an enabling feature; extended use is the outcome to establish.

## Famous example: Too Good To Go

Too Good To Go connects businesses with surplus food to customers willing to purchase it at a discount.

Stores assemble variable contents into “Surprise Bags,” customers reserve and collect them, and the platform earns a fee on sales. Businesses recover revenue from stock that might otherwise be written off. [Too Good To Go: How the Marketplace Works](https://www.toogoodtogo.com/en-us/blog/what-too-good-to-go-partners-recover)

One transaction can therefore provide several benefits: recovered income for the seller, a lower purchase price for the customer, platform revenue, and less discarded food.

The important condition is what would otherwise have happened. Food that replaces a disposal has a different impact from food that would have been sold or donated anyway. Additional production, packaging, and collection travel also affect the result.

The model illustrates a strong connection between earning revenue and addressing waste, while leaving room to examine its wider effects.

## Famous example: Patagonia

Patagonia combines commercial clothing sales with repair, resale, and environmental commitments.

Its Worn Wear program offers used products, trade-ins, repair services, and care guidance. These activities extend the company’s relationship with products beyond their first sale. [Patagonia: Worn Wear](https://www.patagonia.com/worn-wear/)

Its ownership arrangement adds another mechanism. The Patagonia Purpose Trust holds the voting stock, while the Holdfast Collective holds the nonvoting stock. Patagonia states that excess profits, after reinvestment and reserves, are distributed to the Collective for its environmental work. [Patagonia: Ownership and Purpose](https://www.patagonia.com/ownership/)

This connects operating choices, governance, and the destination of financial returns.

It also leaves a real tension: manufacturing and selling clothing consumes resources. Patagonia explicitly acknowledges the conflict between growth and environmental impact. Funding environmental work does not remove the need to reduce harm within the business itself.

## The economics

Blended Value can strengthen a commercial proposition. Customers may value durability, affordable access, responsible sourcing, or useful recovery of resources. Better processes may reduce waste or operating costs.

Other commitments require spending that customers do not fully reimburse. Higher supplier payments, accessible pricing, training, or environmental improvements can reduce margins.

The model needs an explicit way to fund those choices. That might involve higher prices, operating efficiencies, different revenue streams, patient investors, or a deliberate acceptance of lower financial returns.

Financial viability remains important because an enterprise needs resources to continue its work. At the same time, financial success alone cannot establish that its wider contribution is positive.

The commercial result and the social or environmental result each need evidence.

## What needs to be measured

A useful assessment follows outcomes as well as activity.

Selling a durable product measures a transaction. Knowing how long it stays in use says more about whether longevity improved. Paying for training measures an input; changes in employment, earnings, or working conditions reveal more about its effect.

The comparison also matters. A claimed benefit should make clear what it is compared with, over what period, and for whose benefit.

The effects may be distributed unevenly. Customers might gain lower prices while suppliers absorb extra costs. Emissions per product might fall while total emissions rise with sales.

A blended view keeps those differences visible. It does not require reducing every outcome to a single monetary score.

## Useful when

This pattern is especially useful when a business’s main activity directly affects resource use, access to essential goods, employment, working conditions, or community wellbeing.

It fits enterprises whose customers, owners, employees, or partners want a broader definition of success—and are prepared to let that definition shape decisions.

It is strongest when the connection between commercial activity and intended outcomes can be explained clearly and investigated with credible evidence.

## Common mistakes and limits

**Treating purpose as proof.** A mission explains intention. Product performance, working conditions, and environmental outcomes show what happens.

**Counting only positive effects.** A business can create benefits and harms simultaneously. Reporting needs to include both.

**Assuming every decision offers a win for everyone.** Some choices involve genuine conflicts. These require priorities, limits, and an explanation of who bears the cost.

**Confusing growth with impact.** More sales can expand a useful activity, increase harmful effects, or do both. The relationship needs checking.

**Allowing commercial pressure to weaken the mission.** Budgets, incentives, ownership, and decision rights help commitments endure when margins tighten.

## Related patterns

**Circular Economy** keeps products and materials useful for longer. It can provide the operating mechanism for a blended-value business.

**Inclusive Business** brings underserved people into markets as customers, workers, suppliers, or partners. It gives social goals a concrete commercial form.

**Cross-Subsidization** uses income from one activity or customer group to support another. It can fund access when beneficiaries cannot cover the full cost.

**Steward Ownership** can protect an enterprise’s purpose through ownership and control arrangements. It helps determine whose interests guide decisions when objectives conflict.
