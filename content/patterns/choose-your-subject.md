A café corner catches your eye. There is a red bicycle, a plant spilling from a weathered pot,
and a cup on a small table. You raise your phone and include everything. Later, the picture
seems oddly uncertain. You remember enjoying the place, but the photograph gives each object
roughly equal importance. You cannot tell someone what to look at without pointing.

Before changing a setting, choose what the photograph is about. It might be the bicycle, the
plant, the cup, or the relationship between all three. Choosing a subject means choosing an
intention for this particular frame. It does not mean finding the most objectively interesting
thing in the scene.

## Give the photograph a center of interest

Think of a photograph as a sentence with a main idea. Supporting details can enrich it, but
they need a relationship to that idea. A subject need not occupy the center, fill the frame,
or be a single object. A crowd, a patch of light, or the distance between two people can be
the subject. What matters is that you know what you are trying to make noticeable.

That choice helps because a photograph removes much of the experience that originally caught
your attention. The viewer cannot turn their head, hear the conversation, or remember the
walk that brought you there. They have the frame and the clues inside it. Relative size,
placement, light, color, sharpness, and gesture can suggest where attention belongs. None
works as an absolute rule: a small person can dominate a large empty landscape.

Choosing the subject gives those controls a purpose. It is different from
[[separate-subject-and-background|separating subject and background]], which concerns making
an already chosen subject distinguishable. It also comes before deciding
[[focus-on-what-matters|where to focus]]. The camera can accurately focus on something that
does not matter to your picture.

## Three photographs from the same corner

If the bicycle interests you because it suggests someone has just arrived, leave enough of
the café to establish that possibility. A frame containing the whole bicycle and the table
may say more than a close view of its shiny bell. You might exclude a parked car at the
other edge, because it adds a second visual story without helping the first. The resulting
photograph favors place and implication over mechanical detail.

If you notice the plant's unruly shape against the wall, move closer and let the leaves
occupy more of the picture. The bicycle can remain as a smaller contextual detail. Now the
texture of the pot and the spread of the foliage carry the photograph. You have lost some
information about the café, but gained a clearer observation about this particular plant.
That loss is a consequence of intention, not automatically a flaw.

If the quiet cup is what holds your attention, photograph it from near the table. Include
the tabletop shadow if that explains the quiet, and decide whether the bicycle still adds
anything. A tight frame may make the cup feel personal; a wider frame might suggest someone
absent. The subject can stay the same while the meaning changes. This is why “get closer”
is a possibility rather than a universal solution.

These examples involve different positions and framing, but the decisions begin with what
deserves attention. [[change-your-position|Change Your Position]] explains how moving changes
spatial relationships. Here, movement is a tool in service of a prior choice.

## When the subject is a relationship

At a bus stop, a person sits beneath an advertisement showing a person in a similar pose.
Neither element alone is the whole subject. Their resemblance is. A frame that tightly
isolates the real person would remove the observation that made you stop. Leave room for
both and make their connection legible. [[find-a-relationship|Find a Relationship]] develops
this way of seeing in more detail.

Likewise, a photograph of a small boat beneath a huge cliff may concern the contrast in
scale, rather than either object independently. “What is this about?” is a more useful
question than “Which single thing must I isolate?” Be careful about attributing feelings
or circumstances to strangers from appearance alone. A suggestive photograph is not proof
of the story you imagined.

## Let the choice remain provisional

This pattern helps most in busy places, during travel, and when an appealing scene produces
unfocused pictures. It is also useful with everyday objects: deciding whether you care about
the shape of a spoon or the breakfast around it changes almost every subsequent decision.
You do not need manual exposure or a special lens. On a phone, a small change in framing
and a tap on the intended subject may be enough to begin. Camera terms are explained in
[[photography-foundations|the foundations reference]].

Avoid turning intention into a demand that every photograph explain itself immediately.
An ambiguous image can reward slow looking. A deliberately crowded frame can convey a busy
market better than an isolated stall. The useful distinction is between complexity you
intend and competition you never noticed.

Your first choice may also be wrong for what you discover. Perhaps the plant initially
attracts you, then a shadow crossing its pot becomes more interesting. Change the intention
and make another photograph. [[work-the-scene|Work the Scene]] concerns developing these
variations while shooting. When reviewing them, judge whether each frame serves its own
idea before comparing them for a particular use in [[select-the-strongest-frame|Select the Strongest Frame]]. A good subject choice is a direction for attention, not a contract you
must obey for the rest of the afternoon.
