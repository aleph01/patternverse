**Pattern 37 of 49 · Part 6: Bass Movement and Harmonic Color**

**Use when:** A proposed 'key change' might actually be a new modal color, a brief emphasis, or a full move to another center.

A D bass persists while a high line uses the white notes of the keyboard. The passage can sound D-centered without behaving like a conventional D-minor progression. Later, changing B to B-flat alters its palette while the bass stays put. That is different from moving the whole song to another key.

## The move

Choose whether to keep the center, change its surrounding palette, or establish a new home during the song.

Hold Platform Three's D bass and compare B natural with B-flat in its upper line. This changes Dorian color toward natural minor while D remains the reference. Contrast that with a Porch Light passage Am–D7–G followed by phrases that continue to settle on G. The latter can establish a new home rather than only a new color. Decide which kind of change the section needs before adding a whole new progression. Moving the complete song to a singer's range is the separate transposition pattern.

A mode name does not create a center, and a chromatic chord does not by itself establish a lasting new key.

## Establish a modal center

Modes, drones, tonicization, transposition, and modulation describe different relationships. Grouping them together as “changing the mood” can hide which part of the music actually changed. This pattern provides a practical set of distinctions rather than an exhaustive study of modal traditions.

Platform Three uses D–E–F–G–A–B–C with D as its persistent center. This is D Dorian. Compared with D natural minor, its sixth degree is B natural rather than B-flat. Compared with C major, it shares pitches but organizes them around a different center.

Starting a scale on D is not enough by itself. Repeated bass D, phrase endings on D or A, and a characteristic use of B can make the center and palette audible. A passage that consistently settles on C may still be heard as C-centered despite beginning on D.

Other useful introductory comparisons include D Mixolydian, D–E–F-sharp–G–A–B–C, and D Lydian, D–E–F-sharp–G-sharp–A–B–C-sharp. These names describe interval relationships to D. They do not guarantee a fixed emotion or require every possible scale tone to be used equally.

## Hold a reference beneath movement

A drone is a sustained or recurring pitch reference; a pedal point can continue across changing harmony. In Platform Three, a D reference lets the upper figure change rhythm and register while the low center remains available.

An upper G-major triad, G–B–D, shares the Dorian palette. A B-flat in the upper line instead points toward D natural minor at that moment. The fixed bass helps isolate that color difference. The two versions are original alternatives, not a claim that one altered note determines every aspect of the passage.

Sustaining a note does not make every chord above it sound equally stable. A writer should hear the actual combined pitches and their register. Dense low chords over a drone can obscure the relationship that a sparse upper figure makes clear.

## Compare transposition with modulation

Transposing Porch Light from C to D moves all its material by the same interval. The whole song now sits at a different pitch level. No internal key change is required.

Modulation establishes a new tonal center during the course of the music. An original C-centered section could move through Am–D7–G, then continue with phrases and arrivals that make G the new home. Am can belong to both C and G, acting as a potential pivot; the later context makes the interpretation convincing.

A direct change could instead begin a new section immediately in D. That abrupt move may suit the song, but it changes the singer's pitch demands and the relationship to earlier material. The decision needs a musical reason beyond making the last chorus bigger by default.

## Distinguish temporary emphasis

A brief D7–G inside C may simply tonicize G. If the music promptly returns to its C-centered phrases, a new lasting home has not necessarily been established. Modulation and tonicization can lie on a contextual spectrum rather than being separated by one compulsory number of bars.

Likewise, Fm in a C-major phrase can be a borrowed chord while C remains home. A fixed D drone with modal upper material is another design again. Naming each relationship helps a writer avoid treating every chromatic note as the same event.

## Tomorrow Never Knows: change the world around a center

The Beatles' **Tomorrow Never Knows**, on **Revolver** ([official song page](https://www.thebeatles.com/tomorrow-never-knows)), keeps a strong C-centered reference while the recorded surface changes around it. The repeating rhythmic foundation, sustained tonal focus, and moving recorded textures create development without a conventional succession of large key changes. A stable center can make changes of register, density, and timbre easier to perceive.

That is the relevant comparison with Platform Three. Its low D can remain a reference while a higher line changes from B natural toward B-flat, or while the arrangement becomes thinner. The first choice alters the surrounding pitch palette; the second alters texture. Neither automatically changes the tonal home. Tomorrow Never Knows is therefore useful here precisely without inventing a modulation to make it fit the chapter. Its recorded sounds also belong to its identity; reducing everything to a scale name would omit much of the experience. Decide whether a new section needs a different center, a different collection around the same center, or simply a different sonic relationship.

## Choose a relationship that fits the song

Persistent centers suit some material because the interest lies in rhythm, texture, or an evolving line. Harmonic travel suits other material because destinations and returns form part of its expression. A song can combine both approaches across sections.

This vocabulary helps when a basic major/minor description seems insufficient, when a repeating bass supports changing color, or when a proposed key change needs evaluation.
