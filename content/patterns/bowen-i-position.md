Mara has promised to help her friend Lea move on Sunday afternoon. She also wants to visit her mother, Ellen, for lunch. Before Mara can explain her plan, Ellen says, “You must stay all Sunday. Family comes first.”

Mara considers cancelling on Lea. She also imagines snapping, “You don’t get to control my life.” Either response would relieve something immediately: giving in might end the pressure; attacking might discharge her frustration. Neither tells Ellen clearly how Mara intends to participate.

This invented situation illustrates the difficulty addressed by an **I-position**: defining a considered position and acting on it while navigating an important relationship. In Bowen’s terminology, principle and conduct belong together. [Bowen’s formulation, reproduced by Alaska Family Systems](https://alaskafamilysystems.com/research/what-is-bowen-theory/)

## When agreement becomes the price of connection

The recurring problem is that another person’s reaction starts governing the decision. Someone agrees to avoid disappointment, explains until agreement arrives, or opposes a request mainly to resist being directed. Their attention stays fixed on obtaining a different response.

Bowen’s account of differentiation includes the capacity to maintain judgment under relationship pressure. Compliance and automatic opposition can both leave someone organized around the other person’s position. Greater differentiation allows cooperation as a considered choice, alongside disagreement that does not require attacking the other person. This is an account within the theory, rather than a diagnosis established by one conversation. [Bowen Center](https://www.thebowencenter.org/differentiation-of-self)

An I-position can therefore be relevant to ordinary cooperation, disagreements about time, or a family expectation that has become difficult to sustain. It need not produce a refusal.

## How the familiar arrangement sustains itself

In our example, suppose Mara usually stays whenever Ellen protests. Ellen then expects a long visit; Mara expects that a shorter one will require a struggle. Each new invitation arrives inside that history. Agreement provides immediate relief while leaving Mara’s actual commitments unsettled.

That sequence is a possible formulation of this invented family, not evidence of Ellen’s motives. She might be lonely, accustomed to longer visits, or simply disappointed. Mara does not need to settle that question before explaining her own plan.

Bowen distinguished **solid self** from **pseudo-self**: more firmly grounded principles versus positions more dependent on the relationship environment. The distinction concerns how a position is formed and sustained, including under pressure. It does not make an emphatic opinion inherently more solid. [Bowen Archives](https://murraybowenarchives.org/bowen-theory/)

## Defining a position that can become action

A helpful response begins before the wording. Mara needs to consider what she values, what she has already promised, what Ellen is actually asking, and what she can reasonably do. Her position might be that she wants time with her mother and intends to honor her commitment to Lea.

Bowenian clinician Stephanie Ferrera describes the I-position as emerging from thought about what one is willing to do and where compromise is possible. Her account emphasizes recognizing reactivity, clarifying one’s thinking, and expressing it without attacking. Calm communication serves that work; sounding composed does not establish that the work has occurred. [Ferrera, 2018](https://thecenterforfamilyconsultation.com/a-few-simple-ideas-about-communication/)

Mara can then make an understandable plan. She can acknowledge Ellen’s wish without promising to remove the disappointment. New information may justify reconsideration; repeated pressure alone need not determine her answer.

Daniel Papero’s clinical exposition connects the I-position with responsibility for self and responsible conduct toward others. He emphasizes that the statement has little value without behavior reflecting it, and distinguishes it from an ultimatum aimed at making another person comply. [Papero, 2014](https://doi.org/10.1002/anzf.1079)

## Two versions of Sunday

**In the familiar sequence**, Mara says, “Fine. I’ll stay.” Ellen replies, “Good. Your sister is staying too.” Mara cancels on Lea and spends the afternoon resentful. Ellen continues to expect future Sunday visits. The immediate pressure has ended, but Mara has again left her actual position unexpressed.

**With a different participation**, Mara says:

> “I want to see you, and I’m keeping my promise to help Lea. I’ll come from noon to two.”

Ellen replies, “Your sister stays longer. Why can’t you?”

Mara feels the pull to defend herself or cancel. She listens, acknowledges that Ellen wanted more time, and keeps the plan. During lunch she takes an interest in Ellen’s life. At two she leaves to help Lea, and she remains in ordinary contact afterward.

Ellen is still disappointed. The example’s change is in Mara’s conduct: her visit and departure reflect a position she considered. She has not established that Ellen is unreasonable or made agreement a condition of contact.

These are alternative teaching sequences, not predictions. A single visit also cannot establish a lasting change in differentiation.

A similar distinction could arise when Mara agrees to stay because she learns Ellen genuinely needs practical help. A revised decision can express an I-position if it follows considered information and responsibility. Staying is not inherently compliance, just as leaving is not inherently differentiation.

## What this approach can be mistaken for

**An accusation beginning with “I.”** “I feel that you’re selfish” still assigns a verdict to the other person. Its grammar tells us little about how the position was reached.

**A rule that must never change.** Firmness can become a way to avoid considering another person. A 2026 network study of 400 married South Korean adults reported a negative association between what its authors described as rigid I-position or self-assertion and marital satisfaction. The finding raises questions about measurement and cultural context; it does not test whether using an I-position improves a particular conversation. [Kim and Woo, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1111/jmft.70115)

**A requirement to be unaffected.** Someone can feel guilt, anger, or sadness while working toward a considered response. Visible emotion alone does not tell us how differentiated that response is.

**A substitute for realistic choices.** Financial dependence, caregiving duties, and threats can constrain action. Naming one’s position does not erase those conditions or oblige someone to disclose it when doing so is unsafe.

**A way to end contact without examining the relationship.** Bowen’s concept of emotional cutoff describes managing unresolved intensity through reduced emotional contact. That makes cutoff a relevant distinction, but it does not establish that every separation is unhealthy or that renewed contact is always appropriate. [Bowen Center](https://www.thebowencenter.org/emotional-cutoff)

## Related Bowen concepts

**Differentiation of self** supplies the broader theoretical context. The I-position is one expression of that effort, rather than an additional member of Bowen’s eight core concepts.

**Triangles** become relevant if Ellen recruits Mara’s sister to persuade her, or Mara asks the sister to intervene. An I-position then concerns Mara’s own participation within a wider process. **Detriangling** addresses how she responds to those invitations without automatically carrying the conflict. [Bowen Center: Triangles](https://www.thebowencenter.org/triangles)

**Person-to-person relationships** concern direct contact about each person’s life and experience. Mara’s continued interest in Ellen matters alongside her departure time; both belong in understanding how she relates. [Papero](https://doi.org/10.1002/anzf.1079)
