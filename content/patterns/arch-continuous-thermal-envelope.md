## What it is for

A wall has thick insulation, yet a corner stays cold and condensation appears at the recessed sides of a window. The specification looks impressive layer by layer. At the junction, however, insulation stops, the air seal is unclear and a structural element crosses the boundary. The problem is where parts meet, so adding insulation elsewhere may leave it unresolved.

## The core idea

A **Continuous Thermal Envelope** gives the heated or cooled rooms a connected protective boundary. The envelope includes walls, roof, windows and floors at the ground; the rooms within temperature control are called conditioned space. Insulation and airtightness each need a traceable route through their junctions. They can follow different layers, but each must connect where parts meet. This coordination is central to the Passive House approach cited here.

![Section traces a continuous insulation line around roof, wall and floor.](../assets/architecture/continuous-thermal-envelope-1.svg "Follow the thermal boundary through every junction rather than judging each material in isolation. Original schematic.")

## How it works

Draw the insulation line around the heated or cooled space, then trace the airtight layer separately. Check roofs, floors, windows and structural elements crossing the boundary. With the responsible designers and installers, establish how each connection can be reached and built in sequence. Design the intended ventilation alongside this controlled enclosure.

## Why it works

Insulation slows heat transfer through materials. Airtight construction limits air passing through gaps. Water-control layers manage rain and other moisture sources, while water vapor can move through materials differently according to the assembly and climate. Each function needs a suitable route through the construction. A thick insulation board may still need separate air-sealing connections, and an airtight membrane must suit the wall's moisture conditions.

A thermal bridge is a route through which heat passes more readily than through the surrounding insulated construction. A metal support or continuous concrete slab can create one; corners and other changes in geometry can also affect heat flow. The result may include colder internal surfaces and greater heat loss. Analyze the actual junction, including its moisture conditions, rather than trying to cancel a local weakness with more insulation on another wall.

## Architectural examples

### Reform Cottage — connecting an old house’s envelope

Reform Cottage in Herefordshire combined an 1838 stone cottage with later additions built in different ways. Owner and retrofit consultant Gervase Mangwana led its refurbishment with a local team and specialist advisers. The problem was to connect those constructions into one protective boundary. The participating supplier's account describes how structural and moisture questions had to be resolved before a complete external-insulation approach became feasible. [Read the project account](https://www.21degrees.com/case-studies/reform-cottage-enerphit-herefordshire/).

The roof edge was altered so the outside wall insulation could meet the roof insulation. Window frames were positioned within the insulation zone, reducing the interruption at each opening, while tapes and membranes connected the air seals. The Passivhaus Trust's guide includes construction photographs showing these junctions. The sequence matters: moving a frame or changing a roof edge affects where the layers can actually join, not simply how thick the insulation is.

The Trust records EnerPHit certification in 2018 and a reported airtightness result of **0.31 air changes per hour at 50 pascals**. This measures leakage under a test pressure; the occupied house uses designed mechanical ventilation with heat recovery. The result supports the account of a carefully connected air barrier. It does not isolate the effect of one junction or establish metered heating savings. For another retrofit, follow the wall, roof, floor and window connections as a coordinated whole, then verify the completed work. [See the guide's Reform Cottage case, printed page 53](https://www.passivhaustrust.org.uk/UserFiles/File/Technical%20Papers/How%20to%20Build%202023/How%20to%20Build%20v2.1.pdf#page=55).

### A roof-edge connection — illustrative scenario

In a hypothetical small building, wall insulation rises to the top of the wall but roof insulation begins farther inward, leaving a gap at the top of the wall. A coordinated section connects the two, while the airtight layer has an explicit supported junction and a buildable installation sequence. The revised drawing is only the beginning. Site inspection must confirm that the intended materials can actually meet without later services cutting through them.

### A balcony junction — illustrative scenario

An illustrative example is an apartment balcony formed by extending an internal concrete slab outdoors. That structural convenience can create a significant conductive path. Alternatives may involve a designed thermal-break component, separate support or a different balcony arrangement. Each needs structural, fire, durability and moisture assessment. The conceptual diagram illustrates the question; it is not a recommendation to alter an existing structural connection.

![Window junction separates insulation continuity from the airtight layer.](../assets/architecture/continuous-thermal-envelope-2.svg "Thermal resistance and airtightness are different functions that must both connect. Original schematic.")

The roof and balcony scenarios show why several trades need to resolve a junction together. A structural fixing, drain or ventilation duct may cross the line drawn by the architect. Decide how that crossing will be supported and sealed, and which part is installed first. A coordinated construction sequence makes continuity achievable on site instead of leaving installers to join incompatible layers after access has closed.

## Design considerations

Reducing leakage helps make indoor conditions more controllable. It does not remove the need to supply and extract appropriate air. Ventilation should be designed, commissioned and maintainable for the occupancy, climate and pollutants involved. An airtight building with an inadequate ventilation strategy can have poor indoor conditions. A leaky building is not a dependable substitute for intentional ventilation either.

![Two balcony junctions compare a penetrating slab and a deliberately separated support.](../assets/architecture/continuous-thermal-envelope-3.svg "Structural connections can interrupt the envelope; actual solutions require engineering and moisture assessment. Original schematic.")

Identify who installs each connection and whether they can access it at the right time. A membrane hidden behind a fixed frame may need to be connected before another trade arrives. Service penetrations should have a deliberate route and sealing method. Photographs, inspection points and appropriate testing can make hidden work reviewable. A final performance test is useful, but early checks leave more opportunity for repair.

Window interfaces deserve a coordinated review of their own. The frame, support, sill drainage, insulation return and air seal have to work together while allowing installation tolerances and maintenance. Moving the window within the wall changes those relationships. A detail selected only for a flush visual effect may make the connection harder to build and inspect. Compare a few plausible junctions with installers before repeating one around the entire facade. Preserve the reasoning in the drawings so a late product substitution does not silently remove a necessary connection or shift water into a vulnerable layer.

Use qualified envelope, structural and building-services advice for actual details and current local requirements. No numerical certification threshold is prescribed here.

## Useful when

This pattern helps in new construction, extensions and deep renovation where comfort and energy goals depend on the enclosure. It is especially valuable before materials and systems are ordered, while incompatible connections can still be resolved. In existing buildings, investigate moisture and construction before changing insulation or air-sealing strategy. Altering the thermal behavior can change where moisture accumulates and how assemblies dry.

- Our well-insulated building still has cold corners.
- How do we keep insulation continuous around a balcony or roof edge?
- Can airtight construction work with healthy ventilation?
- The wall specification is good but the junctions look unresolved.
- Why does a small gap matter in the enclosure?
- We need to connect a new extension to an old building without a cold bridge.
- How do we coordinate windows with insulation and airtightness?
- Is adding more insulation enough to make a comfortable building?

## Common mistakes and limits

Common mistakes include relying on product values rather than junction performance, confusing vapor resistance with airtightness, and drawing a perfect line through components that cannot physically connect. Another is using an untested renovation detail from a different climate. The direction and intensity of moisture loads vary, so a universally named “breather” product cannot establish the suitability of the whole assembly.
