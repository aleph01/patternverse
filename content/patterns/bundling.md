## What it is for

Use this pattern when customers would benefit from buying several offerings together—and when selling them as a package can improve the economics of the business.

A customer buying a camera may also need a lens, memory card, and introductory lesson. A small business may need software, setup, and support. Someone ordering lunch may prefer choosing a complete meal to assembling each part separately.

Bundling combines distinct products or services into one commercial offering.

The package can simplify a purchase, provide a more complete solution, introduce customers to additional offerings, or bring together benefits that different customers value differently.

## The core idea

Sell several components together under a combined price or purchasing arrangement.

The components should have recognizable value individually. The bundle gives customers a reason to acquire them together.

That reason might be convenience, compatibility, savings, or access to a broader collection. A discount is common, but a package can also earn its value through coordination: the customer pays for a combination that works.

There are two main arrangements:

**Pure bundling:** the components are available only as the package within that offer.

**Mixed bundling:** customers can buy the components separately or choose the package.

Mixed bundling preserves a route for customers who need only one part. Pure bundling makes the combined proposition the purchase decision.

## Why it works

**It combines different sources of value.** One customer may mainly want the music service; another may mainly want storage. Both may value the combined package enough to purchase it.

Economic research on information goods explains how bundling can make aggregate customer valuations more predictable under particular conditions, especially when additional distribution costs are low. The result depends on demand and cost assumptions. [Bakos and Brynjolfsson: Bundling Information Goods](https://ccs.mit.edu/papers/CCSWP199/)

**It completes a job.** Products that work together can become a useful solution: equipment with installation, a meal with a drink, or software with implementation support.

**It reduces purchasing effort.** Customers may face fewer decisions, transactions, invoices, or compatibility checks.

**It encourages discovery.** An included service gives customers an opportunity to try something they might not have purchased separately.

These mechanisms can coexist. A strong bundle makes clear which ones matter to its intended customer.

## Famous example: Apple One

Apple One combines services including Apple Music, Apple TV, Apple Arcade, and iCloud+, with different plans offering different storage, sharing arrangements, and additional services where available. Customers can also subscribe to the services individually. [Apple: Apple One](https://www.apple.com/apple-one/)

This is mixed bundling: the customer can compare separate subscriptions with a package.

Different customers can enter for different reasons. One may already value music and storage; another may want entertainment for several family members. The bundle brings those needs into one subscription.

The useful comparison is the cost of services the customer would actually choose. Adding up every component’s standalone price can overstate the customer’s savings if several components would otherwise remain unpurchased.

For Apple, the arrangement creates an opportunity to broaden service adoption through an existing customer relationship.

## Famous example: Amazon Prime

Amazon Prime combines delivery benefits with entertainment, shopping benefits, and other services. The exact inclusions and conditions vary by market. [Amazon: Prime Membership Benefits](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/retail/amazon-prime-benefits)

Its components serve different occasions. Delivery can matter when buying household supplies; video can matter on an evening at home. Members do not need to value each component equally for the overall membership to remain useful.

This illustrates bundling around a continuing customer relationship rather than a single task.

The economic challenge is that the components have different cost structures. Delivering another parcel involves physical fulfillment costs. Entertainment involves content, licensing, and distribution costs. A single membership price must support the resulting mix of use.

More benefits can strengthen the proposition while making it more expensive and complicated to deliver.

## Famous example: McDonald’s meals

McDonald’s offers familiar combinations such as the Big Mac Meal, which brings together a burger, fries, and a drink. Its menu also lists individual products. [McDonald’s: Big Mac Meal](https://www.mcdonalds.com/us/en-us/meal/big-mac-meal.html), [Full Menu](https://www.mcdonalds.com/us/en-us/full-menu.html)

The components are complementary: together they form a recognizable meal.

The customer can make one package decision while retaining some choices within it. For the restaurant, the bundle creates an opportunity to sell several items in one order.

The important commercial comparison depends on what the customer would otherwise buy. A meal can generate additional spending from someone who initially wanted only a burger. It can also discount an order from someone who already intended to buy all three items.

The same package can therefore have different economic effects across customers.

## The economics

Bundling can increase sales without increasing profit.

The business needs to account for the cost of every included component, additional usage, support, fulfillment, returns, and any revenue lost when customers move from separate purchases to the package.

For digital products, supplying another copy may be inexpensive, although development, licensing, infrastructure, and support still matter. Physical goods and labor-intensive services can add substantial cost with every bundle sold.

Unused components create another distinction. A digital entitlement that goes unused may create little additional delivery cost. A physical item still has to be manufactured, stored, and shipped.

The relevant comparison is contribution after costs, including changes in what customers buy. A higher average order value is only part of that picture.

## Useful when

Bundling is especially useful when offerings serve a shared customer, solve connected problems, or have different appeal across the same audience.

It fits situations where coordination is valuable, separate purchasing is inconvenient, or additional components can be supplied economically.

It can also work when customers want a predictable package rather than a series of individual charges.

The fit weakens when most customers strongly prefer one component, the extras are expensive to provide, or the package makes the underlying offer harder to understand.

## Common mistakes and limits

**Using weak extras to inflate apparent value.** A long list of inclusions contributes little when customers do not want them.

**Comparing against unrealistic standalone spending.** Savings should be meaningful relative to plausible customer choices.

**Discounting purchases that would happen anyway.** Some customers would already buy every component. Their migration can reduce revenue unless other gains compensate.

**Ignoring heavy usage.** A fixed package price can attract customers whose usage is unusually costly.

**Combining operationally incompatible promises.** Different delivery schedules, support responsibilities, or renewal terms can make a simple-looking package difficult to run.

**Making the package too broad.** Larger bundles can lose relevance. Different customer groups may need different combinations.
