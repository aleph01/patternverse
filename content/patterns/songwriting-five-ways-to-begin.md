**Pattern 2 of 49 · Part 1: Start Making Songs**

**Use when:** You have a fragment but no dependable entry into writing.

A songwriter opens a blank recording session and waits for a chord progression. Nothing arrives. Later, while walking home, a phrase appears: “The last train knows my name.” There is already something to work with, but the writer dismisses it because the music has not come first. The assumed starting order has become an unnecessary obstacle.

Begin with the material that suggests a next step. A song can start with words, melody, harmony, rhythm, or sound. Each entrance highlights some decisions and leaves others open. Choosing an entrance is not choosing an identity for the rest of your musical life.

## Working choice

Begin with the material that already suggests a next event, then let another musical layer answer it. An entry point is not a permanent order of work; switch when the current material stops generating useful choices.

## Start from a line

A title or lyric fragment can contain a situation, a voice, and a pattern of speech stress. “The last train knows my name” suggests repetition and a relationship between a person and a routine. It could become a weary confession, a comic observation, or a welcoming refrain. Those possibilities depend on the surrounding writing.

An original continuation might be “It waits beneath the same old clock.” Now there is a place and a repeated action. A simple spoken rhythm can guide a melodic phrase. The writer does not need to decide the final key before discovering whether these lines belong together.

The risk is writing a complete poem whose line lengths and word stresses resist singing. A lyric-first approach benefits from hearing the language aloud early. If an attractive sentence consistently fights the musical phrase, either the sentence or the phrase must remain open to revision.

## Start from a melody

Porch Light begins with E4–G4–A4 in a one–one–two beat rhythm. The fragment rises, then rests on its longest note. The next question can be small: repeat it, answer it with a descent, or place a different harmony beneath it.

The three notes alone do not determine the song's key or meaning. Against C major they suggest one relationship; against A minor they suggest another. A melodic entrance lets the writer discover harmony around a memorable gesture rather than waiting for a full progression.

The risk is a pleasing fragment that never becomes a phrase. A useful next decision concerns its ending: what would make the listener expect another line, and what would make a reply feel complete? Patterns on motifs and phrasing will make that development explicit.

## Start from chords

The original sequence C–Am–F–G is a workable harmonic setting. Each chord can last two bars while a voice explores different melodic rhythms. One melody might use short repeated notes; another might stretch a single line across the changes. The progression is a shared setting, not the finished song.

Chord-first writing becomes restrictive when every melody simply follows the highest note in a repeated keyboard shape. Changing the rhythm of the melody, singing before playing the next chord, or temporarily simplifying the accompaniment can reveal other possibilities. A familiar progression does not require an unfamiliar replacement every time it feels ordinary.

## Start from rhythm

Platform Three begins with a D bass note on beat one and a shorter A on the second half of beat three. The empty space is part of its character. A second instrument can respond in that space, creating conversation without a long chord sequence.

A rhythm-first approach is useful when the movement itself is compelling. It also supports words: a repeated speech rhythm can become a hook before exact pitches settle. The risk is confusing an enjoyable loop with an entire song. Later decisions must give the repeated material a changing context.

## Start from sound

A dry plucked note, a distant vocal sound, or a sustained chord can suggest a pace and setting. An original sketch might begin with a muted keyboard note that seems to disappear before it settles. That sound encourages space; filling every gap with new notes could remove the feature that made the entrance interesting.

Sound-first work can also become endless auditioning of presets. The useful boundary is whether the chosen sound provokes a musical action. If it only prompts another search for a better sound, a simpler temporary setting may let writing proceed.

## An instrumental entrance: Blue Monday

New Order's **Blue Monday** is a documented example of a song entering through instruments and programming before its final vocal identity. In the band's [Song Exploder conversation](https://songexploder.net/new-order), Peter Hook describes an initial instrumental intended to play after the musicians left the stage. The episode separates programmed parts, bass, and the later decision to add vocals; its [transcript](https://songexploder.net/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/Song-Exploder-New-Order-Transcript.pdf) makes the account easy to follow. This history comes from the musicians, rather than a guess based on which sound begins the finished record.

The writing lesson is the handoff between layers. A rhythmic and sonic framework could already provoke useful decisions before there was a finished lyric. Later contributions changed what that framework became. Platform Three uses the same freedom on a smaller scale: its bass gap can invite an upper answer before any vocal question is settled. A rhythm-first beginning is therefore an available route, not an instruction to postpone words in every song.

## Keep the entrances connected

These routes overlap quickly. A lyric suggests a rhythm; the rhythm suggests a melody; a melody suggests a bass note; the bass suggests a chord. The point is to follow those relationships rather than enforce an order.

A practical draft can preserve several entrances at once: a voice memo of the melody, a written title, a chord shorthand, and a note about the intended sound. That record keeps options available without demanding completion in one sitting.

This approach helps when a blank page feels larger than the available idea, or when a habitual instrument keeps producing the same opening. It does not guarantee that every fragment should become a song. Some fragments are worth saving; some are useful experiments; some reveal their limits once another part is added. [[songwriting-capture-musical-seeds]] preserves the promising ones, and [[songwriting-finish-a-tiny-song]] gives one of them a manageable destination.
