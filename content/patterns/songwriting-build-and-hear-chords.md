**Pattern 16 of 49 · Part 3: Notes, Keys, and Chords**

**Use when:** A chord substitution sounds unexpectedly rough, or a chord symbol feels like a shape with no explainable relationship to the tune.

A keyboard player changes E to E-flat inside a C chord. Only one note moves, but the chord's quality changes. The difference is easier to understand when the chord is treated as a set of relationships rather than a hand shape to memorize.

## The move

Inspect the chord tones beneath a fixed melody before replacing a familiar chord shape.

Start with C–D–E–F–G–A–B. Take C, skip D, take E, skip F, and take G: C–E–G is a C-major chord. In the player comparison **Chord notes · C major and C minor**, first hear those notes separately and then together. The second half lowers only E to E-flat. The chord changes because a note relationship changes. Hear the chord on its own before checking it against a melody.

Changing a chord's spacing is different from changing its quality, and an exposed clash may be a useful deliberate choice.

## Build one chord from three scale notes

A triad contains three different pitch classes that can be arranged as stacked thirds. The notes are called root, third, and fifth. The root names the chord; the third and fifth help determine its quality. Notes may be repeated in other octaves without creating a different triad.

C major contains C–E–G. Its names connect directly to the scale: C is the starting note, E is the third note counting from C, and G is the fifth. The root is the note from which this chord is built. It is C here. Later, D–F–A will have D as its root even if the larger song still feels at home on C. A chord's root and a song's tonic describe different roles.

Measured from C, E is four semitones above and G is seven. C minor contains C–E-flat–G: the third is lowered by one semitone while the root and fifth remain. The comparison keeps the rhythm, register, and number of notes fixed so the changed third is easy to follow. It does not promise a particular emotion from either quality.

In ordinary chord shorthand, C means C major and Cm means C minor. The player first presents the three members one beat at a time, leaves one beat of space, then sounds the complete chord for four beats. It repeats that eight-beat plan for C minor. A chord played note by note is called an arpeggio; sounding the members together reveals the same collection as a block.

A major triad is not simply three white keys. D major contains D–F-sharp–A. Nor does a chord with black keys necessarily sound more complex. The quality concerns the distances between its members, not their keyboard color.

## Connect the chord back to the scale

Using alternate notes from the C-major scale, D–F–A is D minor rather than D major: D to F spans three semitones. The scale's pattern creates different chord qualities at different starting points. [[songwriting-chord-families-and-numbers|Think in Chord Numbers]] builds the complete family. The first discovery here is simply that a chord can be explained by the notes it contains.

A fourth and fifth note can later add color, and two other triad qualities alter the fifth. Those comparisons belong in [[songwriting-sevenths-added-notes-suspensions|Add Color to a Working Chord]]. Major and minor are enough to make this first relationship audible.

## Distinguish a chord from its layout

C–E–G, E–G–C, and G–C–E can all express a C-major triad. The lowest sounding member determines the inversion when these are the chord tones in the bass. The spacing and octave placement form its voicing. Those topics receive their own pattern because changing a layout can substantially change a sound without changing the chord's name.

Likewise, a guitarist may play several C and G notes alongside one E. The chord still contains the same three pitch classes. Omitting the third creates a more open root-and-fifth sound that does not by itself declare major or minor quality.

A melody can also add a note beyond the underlying triad. If the singer holds A over C–E–G, the combined sound contains an additional color. Whether a chart needs to name that color depends on what the accompaniment is being asked to play and how the event functions.

## Put the triads into Porch Light

After hearing the chord alone, place C–E–G beneath Porch Light's E–G–A seed. Replacing it with C–E-flat–G leaves E natural in the melody against E-flat in the chord. That disagreement may be deliberate, but it is a second question beyond constructing the chord. The root, the chord quality, and the melody's notes each need to be heard rather than inferred from a hand shape.

The verse's chord tones are straightforward:

| Chord | Notes | Quality |
| ----- | ----- | ------- |
| C     | C–E–G | Major   |
| Am    | A–C–E | Minor   |
| F     | F–A–C | Major   |
| G     | G–B–D | Major   |

The seed E4–G4–A4 begins over C in the miniature. E and G belong to the triad; A extends beyond it. The phrase need not avoid A. That note's longer duration is a deliberate relationship with the harmony, explored later under melody and tension.

For a controlled comparison, replacing the opening C with Cm introduces E-flat in the accompaniment. A melody that still uses E natural now creates a close clash. That could be intentional, but it is not the same as simply making the whole passage “more minor.” Harmony and melody need to be considered together.

## Hear an arpeggio and a block chord as related

A chord's notes can sound together or be played one after another as an arpeggio. C–E–G–E repeated through a bar can imply C major even though the notes do not all attack simultaneously. Sustained sounds and musical context can make that harmony especially clear.

An arpeggiated accompaniment also has a rhythm and contour. Reordering its notes changes the instrumental gesture even if the chord stays C. This gives a writer another way to revise an accompaniment without replacing the progression.

Triad construction is particularly useful when a chord symbol feels like an opaque instruction, when a melody clashes unexpectedly, or when a familiar instrument shape needs to be transferred elsewhere. Chelsey Hamm's [Triads](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/triads/) supplies the technical reference. Our chord comparisons are original. [[songwriting-chord-families-and-numbers]] connects triads to a key; [[songwriting-sevenths-added-notes-suspensions]] adds further possibilities.

## Three Little Birds: separate chord quality from groove

Bob Marley and the Wailers' [Three Little Birds, official video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HNBCVM4KbUM), provides a clear setting for hearing familiar major-chord colors without a dense harmonic vocabulary. The basic A, D, and E major chords used in [Musical Futures' teaching arrangement](https://www.musicalfuturesonline.org/resources/learntoplay-keyboard-section-9c/) contain A–C-sharp–E, D–F-sharp–A, and E–G-sharp–B. The accompaniment's clipped attacks and the bass's movement make those colors part of a groove. They are not simply sustained keyboard blocks, and an audible bass note need not always be a chord's root.

A useful distinction emerges when the same chord is played in a different order or rhythm. C–E–G and E–G–C contain the same major-triad pitch classes; C–E-flat–G changes the quality. Shortening their attacks changes articulation instead. The recording makes that separation worth hearing because a simple harmonic palette can still have a highly specific rhythmic identity. In Porch Light, identify the notes of C, Am, F, and G before deciding how to distribute them. Those are the course's own chords; the Marley reference supplies a separate A-major context, without prescribing every recorded voicing. Simplicity of chord material leaves considerable room for specificity of performance.
