## A familiar moment

You finish a call and say, “I feel awful.” Someone interrupted you several times, but what stays with you is harder to name. Your chest feels tight. You want to avoid the next meeting. You also keep thinking of a reply you wish you had made.

“Awful” communicates that something matters, but it leaves several experiences bundled together. Looking more closely might reveal hurt, anger, embarrassment, or uncertainty. You do not need to choose a perfect label before beginning. You can start with what happened and what you notice.

## Understand the skill

Dialectical Behavior Therapy, or DBT, uses a model of emotions to help people identify and describe their experience. An episode can include a prompting event, interpretations, body changes, action urges, expressions or actions, and aftereffects. Background conditions, such as poor sleep or ongoing pressure, may also matter.

These components influence one another; they do not always appear in a tidy line. You might feel a sensation before noticing a thought. A memory can prompt a new feeling. An emotion about another emotion—such as shame about being angry—is sometimes called a secondary emotion. “Secondary” describes its relationship to another response, not its importance.

[DBT Self Help's guide](https://dbtselfhelp.com/identifying-describing-emotions/) explains the components. The purpose here is an understandable account of one episode. Naming an emotion does not establish another person's motive or require you to act on the urge that accompanies it.

## When to use it

Try the skill when you know you are affected but cannot tell what you feel, or when several feelings seem to blur together. It may help after a conversation, an unexpected change, or a disappointment. Useful cues include broad descriptions such as “bad,” “stressed,” or “all over the place.”

Begin with a manageable recent episode rather than the most painful event you can remember. You can use only external observations if attending to body sensations is uncomfortable. New or concerning physical symptoms deserve appropriate medical attention; an emotional description cannot determine their cause. If reflection becomes overwhelming, stop, orient to your surroundings, and consider support.

## How to practice

The following sequence organizes the established emotion-model components into a short reflection. It is a flexible adaptation rather than a claim about the exact order in your nervous system.

1. **Describe the context and prompting event.** What happened, or what memory or thought arose? Add relevant background conditions without assuming they caused the response.
2. **Separate interpretation from observation.** What did the event seem to mean to you? “They interrupted twice” and “My views don't matter” belong in different places.
3. **Notice sensations and possible emotion names.** Describe sensations if comfortable. Try one or two tentative labels; “unsure” is sufficient.
4. **Identify action urges.** What did you want to do: withdraw, argue, explain, seek reassurance, or something else?
5. **Describe expression and action.** What did you actually say or do? An urge and an action can differ.
6. **Notice aftereffects.** What happened next in your thoughts, feelings, behavior, or circumstances? Note additional emotions and gaps in recall.

## See it in action

Looking back at the call, you write: “While I was describing an idea, my colleague began speaking before I finished on two occasions.” You had also slept poorly. That is context; you cannot determine exactly how much it affected the call.

Your interpretation was, “They think my idea isn't worth hearing.” Your chest felt tight, and your face became warm. “Hurt and angry” seems closer than “awful,” though embarrassment may also fit.

The urge was to interrupt back and then stop contributing. What you actually did was say, “Okay,” and remain quiet for the rest of the discussion. Later you replayed the exchange and felt frustrated with yourself for withdrawing. That frustration may be another part of the episode rather than evidence that the first feeling was wrong.

The account does not settle whether your colleague intended disrespect. It does distinguish the event, its meaning to you, your experience, and your response. You can decide later whether to clarify the exchange or prepare a sentence such as, “I'd like to finish this point.” Understanding the episode is already a separate task from solving it.

## Try it yourself

**Describe One Emotional Episode** takes about 7–10 minutes. Choose your own situation, keep it in private notes, or use the meeting example. You will make a small record of the emotion-model components, with room for uncertainty and mixed feelings.

You will leave with an account in your own words and one cue you might notice again. The practice does not infer an emotion from a symptom, assign a diagnosis, or decide which label is correct. You can revise your understanding later.

## When it feels difficult

**“I don't know emotion words.”** Start with a broad family: anger, fear, sadness, shame, guilt, joy, or affection. A word such as “unsettled” is also useful. Your smaller first step is describing one urge or observable response without choosing a label.

**“Everything happened at once.”** Put the parts alongside one another and mark the order as uncertain. You do not need to invent a sequence. Begin with the one part you remember most clearly, then add another only if available.

**“Describing it feels like justifying my behavior.”** An explanation can include both understandable feelings and an action with unwanted effects. Write “I was angry, and I raised my voice” as two observations. A smaller first step is separating what you wanted to do from what you actually did. Repair can be considered afterward.

## Bring it into daily life

After one ordinary moment this week, note the event, one possible feeling, and one urge. Add other components only if useful. The aim is growing familiarity with your experience, without making every feeling into a project.

Which distinction—event and interpretation, urge and action, or first feeling and later feeling—makes this episode easier to understand?

“When I notice myself saying ‘I feel awful,’ I can try naming one event, one possible feeling, and one urge.”
