A story needs a small traveller to sit across from a towering wizard. Or a human to look enormous among elves. The actors need to talk, react and inhabit the same scene, while appearing to belong to different scales.

**Forced perspective creates that size difference by arranging people and scenery at carefully chosen distances from the camera.** The audience sees a convincing relationship between objects that, on set, may be metres apart.

The attraction is immediate: real faces, costumes, light and performances can become part of an impossible image.

## The puzzle: how can Bilbo serve tea to someone so much bigger?

![Bilbo and Gandalf share tea in Bag End.](https://theasc.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/LOTR-Fellowship-Gandalf-and-Bilbo.jpg)

*The Lord of the Rings: The Fellowship of the Ring (2001) — Film frame.* Bilbo and Gandalf share tea in Bag End. Their apparently shared table disguises the different distances from the camera that make their sizes diverge. [Image source](https://theasc.com/article/lord-of-the-rings-fellowship-of-the-rings-vfx/). Credit: Film image published by American Cinematographer.


In the Bag End frame, the apparently shared table makes Bilbo and Gandalf seem close enough to have tea together. It hides the depth separation that makes Gandalf look large and Bilbo small.

The hidden arrangement was much stranger. Visual-effects supervisor Jim Rygiel explains that, for the tea-serving shot, Ian Holm was actually about **30 feet behind Ian McKellen**. From the camera’s chosen position, that separation became a difference in apparent size. [Production account](https://theasc.com/article/lord-of-the-rings-fellowship-of-the-rings-vfx/).

The table and room help conceal the distance. The performers supply the conversation that makes the arrangement feel ordinary.

## The mechanism: turn distance into size

An object farther from the camera occupies less of the picture. For equally tall performers, doubling the distance along the camera’s viewing direction approximately halves their projected height.

But distance alone does not produce a convincing giant. Usually, we recognise the smaller figure as someone standing farther away.

The trick therefore has two parts:

1. **Create the apparent size difference** by separating the subjects in depth.
2. **Disguise that separation** so the audience interprets them as sharing the same space.

A tabletop can appear continuous across separate pieces. A foreground doorway can frame a distant performer. Furniture can be built at different scales. Platforms can bring feet, heads or surfaces into the positions the composition requires.

From beside the camera, the set may look peculiar. Through the lens, its parts fit together.

## A third puzzle: why does Buddy look enormous among the elves?
In *Elf*, Buddy’s size becomes funny because he appears to occupy the elves’ everyday world: their workspaces, furniture and rooms.

Jon Favreau describes aligning a smaller set near the camera with a larger set farther away. The nearer performer consequently appears larger, while the two environments read as one.

Even the paint mattered. When the distant floor looked darker than the foreground floor, an on-set painter lightened it to help the surfaces match. A difference in tone could reveal the separation that the construction was designed to hide. [Favreau’s explanation to ABC News](https://abcnews.com/Entertainment/things-knew-christmas-movie-elf/story?id=51197336).

## What makes the illusion convincing?
The strongest setups give the audience several reasons to accept the invented space.

**Scenery joins the subjects.** A shared-looking floor, counter or table establishes a relationship between people who are physically separated. A visible gap or mismatched edge can undo that relationship.

**Eyelines connect the performances.** Actors must appear to look at the character’s intended screen position. Looking directly at the other performer’s real position may produce the wrong angle.

**Focus conceals the distance.** If the nearer actor is sharp and the farther one is conspicuously blurred, the photograph supplies a strong clue about depth. Keeping both readable helps preserve the shared-space impression.

**Lighting agrees across the frame.** Different shadow directions, incompatible brightness or an obvious change in colour can make joined scenery feel disconnected.

These details explain why a simple optical principle can require painstaking preparation. The size difference is easy to create. Making everything else agree takes work.

## Where the trick becomes difficult
A forced-perspective setup belongs to a particular viewpoint. Move the camera sideways and nearby objects shift against distant ones. That relative movement—parallax—can expose the hidden separation.

A stationary camera makes the arrangement easier to maintain. Moving shots require more elaborate planning, potentially including coordinated movement of scenery or performers.

Physical contact creates another difficulty. Two actors who appear adjacent may be too far apart to shake hands. Passing an object between them, embracing or walking around one another can reveal the real geography. Such moments often call for another technique: a different shot, a scaled prop, a double or compositing.

The practical question is therefore: **what does this particular shot need the characters to do?** A conversation across a table offers different possibilities from a wrestling match.

## Recognising the pattern elsewhere

The same principle appears when a tourist seems to hold a distant tower between two fingers. It can also make a small model near the camera appear to belong to a full-size landscape farther away.

In each case, the camera records a carefully arranged projection. The audience supplies the imagined space.

When watching an impossible difference in scale, look for the details that make the subjects seem connected: a tabletop, a doorway, an overlapping edge, a glance. Those are often the most revealing parts of the puzzle.
