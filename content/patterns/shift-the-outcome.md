### What makes an attempt count as success

A designer tests an early prototype with five people, and four struggle to find the main action. The designer leaves the session discouraged: the product has failed before it has even been built. A colleague looking at the same result sees a valuable test. The confusion appeared while the design was still cheap to change. Their disagreement is partly about what the session was supposed to accomplish. Was it meant to demonstrate that the product worked, or to discover where it did not?

Robert Dilts calls the shift toward a different goal Another Outcome in Sleight of Mouth. An evaluation such as successful, too slow, or disappointing depends on an outcome, even when that outcome is never stated. The same event can meet one purpose and miss another. Making the purpose visible allows the evaluation to become more exact and sometimes reveals that the activity has been judged against a goal it was poorly suited to achieve.

This pattern is particularly relevant to exploratory work. An experiment can fail to confirm a hypothesis while successfully reducing uncertainty. A rehearsal can contain mistakes while revealing what requires attention. A conversation can leave a disagreement unresolved while making each person's concern clearer. Those outcomes do not erase the original disappointment. They help distinguish the different kinds of value the activity could reasonably produce.

### When a secondary goal takes over

Imagine a meeting arranged to understand why a project is delayed. As the discussion develops, each participant starts defending their own performance. Information is selected to avoid blame, and the meeting becomes a contest over who can offer the most convincing account. The stated goal remains understanding, but the working goal has become self-protection. A useful outcome shift reconnects the conversation with the decision it needs to support: what must change for the next stage to proceed?

The change becomes concrete when participants can share incomplete information without treating every uncertainty as an admission of fault. The meeting may then identify a missing dependency or a decision nobody owned. Accountability can still be addressed, but the inquiry no longer has to wait until one person wins the explanation. The pattern helps because different outcomes reward different contributions. A contest rewards defensible claims; a diagnostic discussion rewards information that makes correction possible.

Creative work provides another example. A musician enters a writing session hoping to find an unfamiliar melody, then evaluates every fragment as though it were a finished song. The evaluation suppresses the exploration that the session required. Restoring the original outcome changes how fragments are handled: some become promising directions, some reveal habits, and some can be discarded without making the whole session worthless. A later session may properly pursue polish. The relevant outcome can vary across stages of the same project.

### A legitimate shift versus retrospective rescue

There is an easy abuse of the pattern: after missing a commitment, invent a new goal and declare success. A team promises sales, achieves none, and reports that awareness was the real purpose. Awareness may have value, but it does not retroactively satisfy the sales commitment. The original outcome must remain available for evaluation, especially when other people relied on it. An additional outcome can enrich the account without replacing the obligation.

A helpful test is whether the alternative purpose would have been reasonable before the result was known. Learning from an exploratory prototype usually passes that test. Learning from an expensive implementation that skipped necessary checks may be a consequence worth recording, but it offers a weaker defense of the decision. The timing, cost, and original agreement all affect which outcomes legitimately belong in the evaluation.

Some outcomes also operate as constraints. A design may teach the team a great deal while failing a safety requirement. The educational value cannot make the requirement optional. Outcome shifts are most useful when they correct a misplaced emphasis among valid purposes; they become misleading when they obscure conditions that must still be met. A fuller account can therefore conclude that an attempt taught something important and was still unacceptable in a particular respect.

### Aligning judgment with the stage of work

Shift the Outcome helps when perfection crowds out exploration, an argument loses its purpose, or disappointment rests on a narrow definition of success. Move Up the Value Hierarchy examines which criteria should take precedence when several goals conflict. Change the Frame Size considers the wider setting in which those goals matter. Find the Positive Intention can reveal the concern that made a particular outcome important in the first place.

The prototype's confusing interface still needs revision. The meeting still needs decisions, and the musician still needs to finish songs eventually. What changes is the relationship between each activity and the outcome used to assess it. A first test can be judged as a test, a difficult conversation as an attempt at clarification, and an exploratory session as preparation for later selection. That alignment permits a more honest evaluation of both what was gained and what remains to be done.

## Pattern details

### Use when

- Exploratory work is judged as finished performance.
- A conversation’s working goal has drifted from its purpose.

### Goals

- Align judgment with the activity’s legitimate purpose.
- Recognize different outcomes without erasing commitments.

### Mechanisms

- goal reframing
- evaluation criteria

### Not ideal when

- A new goal would obscure a failed commitment or a necessary constraint.

### Difficulty

easy
