## A familiar moment

Your colleague asks whether you can cover their Saturday shift. You have already made plans and need the time off. “Maybe,” you say, hoping they will notice your hesitation. Later they thank you for agreeing, and you feel trapped between disappointing them and giving up the weekend.

You may know what you want but struggle to put it into a clear sentence. DEAR MAN offers a structure for making a request or saying no. It helps you prepare what to say and how to remain with the point when the conversation becomes uncomfortable.

## Understand the skill

DEAR MAN is an interpersonal effectiveness skill from Dialectical Behavior Therapy, or DBT. Interpersonal effectiveness concerns how you communicate needs, handle limits, and pursue goals with other people. This particular skill focuses on an **objective**: the specific request, refusal, or change you want to communicate.

The letters stand for **Describe, Express, Assert, Reinforce, stay Mindful, Appear confident, and Negotiate**. DEAR organizes the message. MAN supports how you deliver it and respond during the exchange. [DBT Self Help's DEAR MAN guide](https://dbtselfhelp.com/objectives-effectiveness-dear-man/) explains these components.

A clear objective is something you can state, not something you can force another person to grant. You might ask for a deadline change, refuse an extra shift, or request that someone return an item. Even a well-prepared message may receive a no. Your relationship, self-respect, circumstances, and safety still matter.

## When to use it

Try DEAR MAN when you keep hinting, overexplaining, apologizing for a reasonable limit, or agreeing before considering your capacity. It can help with household responsibilities, scheduling, requests for assistance, and ordinary work conversations.

First consider the setting and power differences. If saying no may bring retaliation or violence, a communication script alone is inadequate; practical safety and qualified support may be needed. You do not have to keep repeating a boundary to someone who is threatening you. Nor does every request require all seven parts aloud. For a straightforward question, a simple direct sentence may be enough.

## How to practice

Begin by identifying one objective, then prepare the established components.

1. **Describe.** State the relevant situation without adding an accusation: “You asked me to cover Saturday.”
2. **Express.** Explain your experience or position: “I need to keep that day free.” Choose how much personal detail to share.
3. **Assert.** Make the request or refusal explicit: “I can't cover the shift.” Avoid relying on a hint.
4. **Reinforce.** Explain an honest benefit or acknowledge cooperation: “Knowing now gives you time to ask someone else.” This is not an invitation to threaten or make promises you cannot keep.
5. **Stay mindful.** Keep the objective in view. You can acknowledge a new point and return to your request or limit rather than defending every side issue.
6. **Appear confident.** Use a clear, steady delivery that fits your abilities and culture. You can read from notes, communicate in writing, or speak while feeling nervous; eye contact is not a requirement.
7. **Negotiate.** Consider options you are genuinely willing to offer. Some limits remain firm. You do not have to create an alternative or bargain away safety to earn the right to say no.

[DBT.tools' DEAR MAN page](https://dbt.tools/interpersonal_effectiveness/dear-man.php) also describes this sequence. The accessibility and safety adjustments here are editorial adaptations.

## See it in action

You return to your colleague: “You asked about Saturday. I checked my plans, and I need to keep the day off. I can't cover the shift. I wanted to tell you now so you can make another arrangement.”

They reply, “But you said maybe. I'm really stuck.” You recognize the difficulty without withdrawing the limit: “I understand you were hoping I could. I wasn't clear earlier. I can't cover Saturday.” That is staying mindful of the objective while acknowledging your earlier ambiguity.

You have decided that swapping a different weekday is possible, so you add, “I could discuss a Tuesday swap if that helps.” If you were not willing to do that, you would leave it out. Negotiation needs a real option.

Your voice shakes, and the colleague looks disappointed. You have still made the position understandable. The outcome may involve a further practical conversation, and the colleague may need to find someone else. Clear communication can leave both people with difficult feelings.

## Try it yourself

**Build and Rehearse Your Request** takes about 10–15 minutes. Choose a request or refusal, keep personal details private, or select an example. You will draft the DEAR message, prepare MAN delivery notes, and rehearse one reply to possible pushback.

You will leave with a script and a smaller fallback sentence. The practice is an original adaptation, not an automated conversation with the other person. It will not send your script, infer their response, or evaluate your confidence.

## When it feels difficult

**“I have to justify every part of my no.”** Decide what information is relevant and what you wish to keep private. A smaller first step is one clear refusal sentence. Add context only if it helps communicate the situation.

**“If I'm nervous, I can't appear confident.”** Prepare a delivery you can use while nervous. Read the sentence aloud, slow down, or use a written message when appropriate. Start by saying the assertion once without adding an apology you do not mean.

**“Negotiating means giving in.”** Write down what is flexible and what is firm before speaking. Your smallest step can be naming one limit you intend to keep. If no alternative fits, “I can't offer another arrangement” is an available response.

## Bring it into daily life

Practise a small request where the stakes are manageable, such as asking for a meeting time to be clarified. Notice whether the other person can tell what you are asking. Review what you communicated separately from whether they agreed.

What is the one sentence you want the other person to understand, even if the conversation becomes uncomfortable?

“When I notice myself hinting instead of asking, I can try stating one clear request or refusal.”
