### The work a label does

A project misses its deadline after six months of work. At the review meeting, someone calls it a failure, someone else calls it a prototype, and a third person calls it a delay. Each description brings a different feature into view. The deadline was missed, something was built, and further work may still lead to a release. Trouble begins when one label becomes so dominant that the other facts disappear. If failure comes to mean that nothing valuable happened, the team may overlook useful discoveries. If prototype becomes an excuse, it may overlook the broken commitment.

In Sleight of Mouth, Robert Dilts calls the replacement of a term with a nearby description Redefining. The substitute refers to a similar situation while changing its implications. This is possible because ordinary words do more than identify events. They also suggest causes, responsibilities, expectations, and possible responses. Describing a person as difficult invites a different reaction from describing them as concerned about an unresolved issue, even if both descriptions arose from the same conversation.

The strongest version of the pattern keeps asking whether the new wording is accurate. A change in description earns its usefulness by revealing something the earlier wording compressed or obscured. It does not earn it merely by sounding kinder. A project can contain a failed commitment, a functioning prototype, and a delay at the same time. More precise language makes those distinctions available without requiring a single verdict on the entire undertaking.

### From identity to a workable description

Consider a person who says they are terrible at networking. The phrase combines several possible experiences: discomfort approaching strangers, difficulty remembering names, dislike of crowded events, or uncertainty about following up. It also makes these experiences sound like a stable personal property. A more specific description, such as finding unstructured professional gatherings tiring, places some of the difficulty in the format of the event. The person can then consider smaller meetings, introductions through existing contacts, or conversations around a shared activity.

Another description might be that they have no reliable way to begin a conversation. That points toward a skill rather than an environmental preference. The person could prepare a simple opening question and discover whether the difficulty changes. These reformulations should not be selected at random. Their value depends on which one fits the experience. The pattern creates a set of candidate descriptions that can be checked, rather than exchanging an unpleasant label for an attractive one and assuming the problem has been solved.

Commercial conversations show the same mechanism. When a client says a proposal is too expensive, the phrase may indicate a limited budget, weak expected value, a cheaper competitor, or an inconvenient payment schedule. A supplier who immediately discounts the price may solve the wrong problem. If the concern is cash flow, staged payments could help. If the expected benefit is unclear, a smaller demonstration may be more relevant. Redefining the objection exposes the decision that the original adjective was concealing.

### When softer language obscures the facts

There is an obvious temptation to use this pattern as cosmetic language. An organization can describe avoidable layoffs as renewal, or a person can rename dishonesty as creativity. The revised label may alter the emotional response while reducing understanding. A useful test is whether the people affected would still recognize the event in the description. If an important loss, breach, or responsibility becomes difficult to name, the new term is doing concealment rather than clarification.

Even a broadly accurate redefinition can arrive too early. Someone who has just lost a job may need the loss to be acknowledged before they can consider it a transition. The word transition might eventually fit, but insisting on it immediately can sound like a refusal to hear the present experience. Timing and ownership matter. A person choosing a new description for their own circumstances is in a different position from someone having a reassuring label imposed on them.

The pattern also cannot determine the facts by itself. Calling a missed deadline a learning experience does not establish how much was learned, and calling caution prudence does not show that the caution was proportionate. The descriptions still need to be supported by what happened. Their role is to organize attention so that relevant evidence becomes easier to inspect, not to give language authority over the evidence.

### Finding language that leaves room for action

Redefine the Terms is useful when a single loaded word keeps organizing an entire conversation or self-assessment. Chunk Down can help unpack the observations behind that word, while Inspect How You Know examines the route from those observations to the conclusion. Find the Positive Intention may reveal the concern behind a label, although the concern and the accurate description of the behavior still need separate consideration.

The result is often less dramatic than a positive reframe. A person may move from being lazy to repeatedly postponing tasks with unclear starting conditions. A team may move from calling a release disastrous to identifying two serious defects and several successful components. These descriptions are less absolute and more informative. They preserve what went wrong while making it possible to see where a response could begin.

## Pattern details

### Use when

- One word dominates a self-assessment or disagreement.
- An ambiguous label obscures different practical concerns.

### Goals

- Make descriptions more accurate.
- Reveal where an effective response could begin.

### Mechanisms

- semantic framing
- descriptive precision

### Not ideal when

- Softer wording would conceal loss, responsibility, or an important fact.

### Difficulty

easy

!ask label {
    Notice the loaded word

    Write a description or label that is shaping your interpretation, such as 'lazy', 'failure', or 'controlling'. Include the everyday situation.
}
!ask facts {
    Keep the observations

    What concrete behavior or events led to the label? Separate observations from judgments about a whole person.
}
!ask alternatives {
    Try more precise descriptions

    Write two alternative descriptions that fit the same observations. They do not have to be positive, only more accurate or specific.
}
!ask limits {
    Check what the new words hide

    Could either description minimize harm, hide responsibility, or contradict the facts? What must remain explicit?
}
!ask wording {
    Choose believable language

    Write a revised description you can honestly stand behind. Keep the original concern if it is still supported.
}
!ask next {
    Return to action

    What response becomes clearer with this wording? Choose one small action, question, or boundary.
