### The weight carried by always

A person says they always ruin interviews. They remember stumbling over a question, becoming tense, and receiving several rejections. The statement gathers those experiences into a rule that appears to predict the next interview as well. Asked about an earlier conversation, however, they recall an interviewer who responded warmly and invited them to another round. The memory does not make every rejection less disappointing, but it complicates the word always. The history contains variation that the rule has removed.

In Sleight of Mouth, Robert Dilts calls this movement Counter Example: finding a case that challenges or enriches a generalization. A universal claim says that a relationship holds across every case within its scope. A relevant exception shows that this scope or relationship needs reconsideration. The revised belief may remain serious and useful, but it can become conditional rather than inevitable.

The exception must fit the claim being examined. Another person's successful interview does little to test a statement about one's own performance. A successful written interview may be relevant to a broad belief about all interviews while offering less information about live panel discussions. Specificity matters because a good counterexample changes the account of the actual difficulty instead of offering generic reassurance.

### Discovering the missing conditions

Consider an employee who believes that disagreement always makes their manager angry. Looking at several interactions reveals that private disagreements were sometimes welcomed, while unexpected challenges during client meetings produced tension. The exception suggests a narrower relationship involving timing, audience, and preparation. The employee has not established that the manager behaves reasonably, but they have learned that disagreement alone does not explain the pattern. There are additional conditions worth understanding.

The logical structure deserves care. If a claim says A always implies B, a case of A without B challenges it. A case of B without A addresses a different question: whether A is necessary for B. Both inquiries can be useful, but they are not interchangeable. A manager becoming angry without disagreement does not refute the claim that every disagreement produces anger. It shows that anger can also have another route. Keeping those relations distinct prevents a persuasive conversation from becoming a muddled argument.

An internal example might concern creative work. A writer believes that nothing worthwhile is produced unless they feel inspired. A passage written during an ordinary afternoon provides a relevant exception if the writer still considers it worthwhile on rereading. The next question concerns what that afternoon supplied: a clear task, a recent conversation, or enough uninterrupted time. The exception becomes useful when it reveals conditions that can be recognized again, rather than remaining an isolated lucky event.

### A single case and a statistical tendency

One exception is enough to challenge a universal rule, but it does not erase a tendency. A service that fails once is not perfectly reliable; a service that works once is not therefore reliable enough for every use. To evaluate how frequently a relationship occurs, the number and representativeness of cases matter. The pattern should preserve that distinction instead of treating one favorable story as a decisive answer to every kind of claim.

This also protects the conversation from dismissing repeated difficulties. Someone who has often encountered hostility does not need to be told that one kind interaction proves the problem imaginary. The exception may show that the situation is not completely uniform while leaving a substantial pattern intact. A more accurate belief might be that hostility is common in one setting and less common in another. That information can support a choice without requiring the person to deny what they experienced.

Exceptions should not be manufactured to make an inquiry reassuring. If no relevant exception can be found, the claim may require further observation, a smaller scope, or direct acceptance of a real problem. A hypothetical possibility can suggest an experiment, but it is weaker than an observed case. The distinction between an actual exception and an imagined one should stay explicit, particularly when the conclusion will guide a consequential decision.

### Making a belief less total

Look for the Exception helps when words such as always, never, everyone, or impossible close down inquiry. Chunk Down can specify what the rule actually covers. Inspect How You Know examines which experiences have been counted as evidence, while Follow the Consequences considers how treating the rule as inevitable shapes behavior. Apply the Rule to Itself tests another aspect of a claim by examining whether its own standard applies consistently.

The interview history may still contain a real difficulty with unexpected questions. The employee may still need a better way to raise disagreements, and the writer may still find inspiration helpful. The exception does not promise an easy resolution. It allows the next step to be guided by a belief that contains some variation, which is often where the relevant conditions and available choices can be found.

## Pattern details

### Use when

- Absolute language closes down inquiry.
- Repeated experiences have become an inevitable rule.

### Goals

- Replace unsupported universality with a more conditional account.
- Discover the conditions behind variation.

### Mechanisms

- counterexample
- scope testing

### Not ideal when

- An isolated case is being used to deny a strong statistical tendency or repeated harm.

### Difficulty

easy
