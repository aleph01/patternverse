A person rehearses an argument with a colleague and repeatedly reaches the same conclusion: the colleague refuses to understand. Invited to occupy the colleague's role in the enacted scene, they notice something different. From that position, their own rapid explanation sounds like there is no room to answer.

## Core idea

Role Reversal, in psychodrama, temporarily changes the position from which an encounter is experienced. A person may take the role of another individual, an aspect of themselves, or a symbolic figure within a scene. Returning to their own position allows the effect of that shift to be explored.

The person is encountering their representation of the other role. What emerges can be informative without being factually accurate about the real individual. The colleague may actually feel pressured, confused, indifferent, or something else entirely.

The defining move is temporary participation from another position in an enacted relationship. That position can make different features salient: what there is time to hear, what a question seems to demand, or how a familiar response lands. The resulting perspective is a constructed possibility to consider, not testimony from the person represented.

## What changes in the experience

The distinction protects both perspective taking and epistemic humility. A role reversal can reveal assumptions precisely because the person must express them, and those assumptions may then become available for correction.

Ordinary perspective taking can remain abstract: someone lists reasons the other person might have acted differently. Enactment adds voice, timing, posture, and a direct address within the scene. These features may reveal aspects of a relational position that a conceptual account misses.

Changing position may affect voice, pace, orientation, and what feels relevant. The person can discover assumptions they supplied to the role, or recognize that the role is too difficult to imagine usefully. Returning to their own position permits comparison. The point is expanded perspective with preserved difference, rather than complete identification.

## A worked example

In the example, the discovery gives the person a hypothesis about their contribution to the interaction. It can motivate a real-world question or a different conversational pace. It does not authorize announcing what the colleague secretly feels.

The person notices, from the colleague's represented position, how quickly their own explanation supplies both sides of the conversation. They had experienced this as thoroughness. Watching the timing from the other role suggests that questions have little room to receive an answer. The facilitator helps keep that observation separate from a claim that the colleague feels exactly this way.

Back in their own position, the person rehearses a shorter question and an actual pause. They still do not know whether the colleague will cooperate. They have identified one contribution they can test and one question they can ask directly. The reversal has generated a useful hypothesis without making them responsible for the colleague's entire response.

## Why the move may help

Perspective taking can remain a list of reasons another person might act as they do. Enactment adds timing and relationship: the person encounters the sequence from a different location. That may reveal a constraint or possibility that was invisible while defending their own position.

Psychodrama frames role reversal within a larger theory and method of role relationships. An imagined shift does not verify empathy, settle a disagreement, or reveal another mind. Its value can lie in exposing the person's own representation of the other, including stereotypes and assumptions that need real-world checking.

## When it helps—and what can get in the way

The pattern can be useful when a scene feels locked into one viewpoint, when an internal or relational role is difficult to understand, or when someone wants to explore an unfamiliar capacity. It should be linked to a clear purpose rather than used whenever a conversation becomes difficult.

Role reversal need not involve an antagonist. Someone exploring a difficult decision might temporarily occupy the role of their own steadiness or a trusted supportive figure. From that position, a different tone or possibility may become available.

For example, a person who can readily defend a friend's right to rest struggles to recognize their own limit. Speaking from the role of the caring friend, they discover language that does not feel available from their usual position. On returning, they can notice whether any part of it feels credible.

This does not mean the supportive role possesses the correct answer. It offers an experiential perspective that can be accepted, modified, or left aside.

This can fit a scene that repeatedly stalls around one fixed account. It may be less useful when the immediate need is to have the person's own account heard without qualification. An early invitation to inhabit the other role can feel like a demand to excuse harm, even when the facilitator intends curiosity.

## Boundaries and common misunderstandings

Some roles are too evocative or unwanted to inhabit. Asking a person to play someone who harmed them may feel coercive, confusing, or invalidating. Understanding an aggressor is not a required step toward protection or recovery. A person may prefer a greater distance, an auxiliary player, or no enactment.

Practitioners also need to avoid stereotyping another person's culture, disability, or identity through a role. The scene is one person's representation, with limits that should remain visible.

Understanding possible motives does not erase impact or assign equal responsibility. A harmful role need not be enacted for the person to benefit from therapy. The facilitator should also provide a clear transition out of any assigned role, particularly when it carries strong emotion or an identity the participant does not wish to retain.
