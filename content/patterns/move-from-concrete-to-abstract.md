Abstract ideas are useful because they apply across many situations, but this generality can also make them difficult to grasp. A definition may be perfectly accurate while still giving the beginner very little to think with.

Move From Concrete to Abstract begins with specific situations and gradually reveals what they have in common. Opportunity cost might begin with choosing between a concert and visiting a friend, then appear in government budgets, business decisions, and everyday time management before being stated as a general economic principle.

The movement matters because examples provide cognitive grip. A learner can reason about a specific choice more easily than about an abstract relation between alternatives. Once several examples are available, the common structure becomes easier to see.

Multiple examples are important because a single one can become fused with the concept. Fractions can accidentally become “pieces of pizza,” conditioning can become “the thing with Pavlov's dog,” and opportunity cost can become “the concert example.” Variation reveals what is essential and what belonged only to the original story.

The abstraction becomes useful when it travels. If the learner can recognize the same structure in an unfamiliar setting, the concept has become more than a remembered example.

Experts often move in both directions. When an abstract idea becomes difficult, they construct a concrete case. When many examples begin to accumulate, they compress them back into a more general model. Learning often oscillates between these two levels rather than progressing only upward.

The danger of abstraction too early is empty language: the learner can repeat the definition without anything behind it. The danger of remaining concrete too long is the opposite: the learner understands each example but cannot transfer the principle.

Concrete cases provide something to stand on. Abstraction makes it possible to move.
