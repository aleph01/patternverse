*Bring separate scenes together by controlling what the viewer sees through—and reflected in—glass.*

A ghost must appear beside a living actor. We should see the furniture through its body, watch it move, then see it dissolve into an empty room.

Elsewhere, a filmmaker faces a different problem: actors must cross a monumental hall, but the production can afford only a small section of floor and a few doorways.

Both problems can be approached by combining a direct view with a reflection. The things that appear together needn’t occupy the same space.

**Pepper’s Ghost** uses this relationship to superimpose an apparition. **Mirror compositing**, particularly the Schüfftan process, uses it to assemble actors and scenery into an apparently continuous environment.

## The first puzzle: where is the ghost’s body?


Imagine looking through a window at night. You can see the garden outside, but also the reflection of a lamp inside your room. The reflected lamp seems to occupy space beyond the window.

Pepper’s Ghost turns that everyday occurrence into a carefully staged illusion.

The audience looks through a large, angled pane of glass toward a visible set. A second, concealed space contains a performer or object. Lighting that hidden subject produces a reflection in the glass, positioned so it appears to inhabit the visible scene.

The audience receives light from two places at once: the room seen through the glass and the figure reflected by it. MoMA describes the familiar arrangement as an angled glass plate reflecting a brightly lit subject from a hidden area into the apparent stage space. [MoMA: Pepper’s Ghost](https://mobile-api.moma.org/collection/terms/peppers-ghost)

The body is real. Its apparent location is the trick.

The concealed performer needn’t leave. Lowering the light on them weakens the reflection until the visible room dominates again.

This is why the hidden space matters as much as the performer. Anything illuminated there might become an unwanted reflection: a wall, a stagehand, the edge of a platform.

In the classic theatrical arrangement, the performer occupied a concealed area below the stage. The Corning Museum of Glass describes how brightening that space made the apparition appear during the 1862 presentation of Dickens’s *The Haunted Man*. [Corning Museum of Glass: the apparatus and its history](https://blog.cmog.org/2012/ghosts-and-magicand-glass-peppers-ghost-and-glass-optics)

### Example: the Haunted Mansion ballroom—solid machinery, transparent dancers

*How can moving figures dance inside a furnished ballroom while remaining visibly see-through?*

The ballroom sequence in Disney’s Haunted Mansion applies Pepper’s Ghost to animated figures. The figures supplying the reflections are solid; their reflected images appear ghostly within the room.

Imagineer Rolly Crump describes how he and Yale Gracey developed small models and then a full-scale demonstration before the attraction opened. The ballroom eventually gave the illusion a setting suited to a ride: guests could watch an entire gathering of apparent ghosts.

The reusable idea is larger than a single apparition. **A whole concealed performance can be reflected into another environment.** [Rolly Crump’s firsthand account at the Walt Disney Family Museum](https://www.waltdisney.org/blog/imagineering-haunted-mansion-rolly-crump)

## The second puzzle: why isn’t the enormous building transparent?

A ghost benefits from overlapping the scenery. A palace wall usually needs to conceal what lies behind it.

For that, filmmakers can divide the picture into regions rather than superimpose both scenes everywhere.

In a classic **Schüfftan composite**, an angled mirror reflects an off-camera miniature or scenic image. Selected areas of its reflective coating are removed, leaving clear glass through which the camera sees actors and full-size scenery.

The remaining mirror supplies one part of the picture; the clear areas supply another. Their edges are aligned so they form one view.

A contemporary 1928 explanation describes precisely this arrangement: a reflected miniature completes the upper scenery while the camera photographs the full-size lower set through the cleared portion. [*Cinémagazine*: illustrated explanation of the Schüfftan process](https://www.la-belle-equipe.fr/2017/05/28/procede-schufftan-cinemagazine-1928/)

*How can full-size actors inhabit an architectural space far larger than the practical set?*

The Schüfftan process combined live action with additional scenic elements in *Metropolis*. The 1928 account specifically identifies the stadium scene as an application.

The important economy is that only the portions needed for performance have to exist at full size. Reflected scenery supplies the surrounding scale.

This explains particular composite shots; the film also used other techniques, including extensive miniature work. [Contemporary account and illustrations](https://www.la-belle-equipe.fr/2017/05/28/procede-schufftan-cinemagazine-1928/)

### Example: *Blackmail* (1929)—a museum assembled elsewhere

*How can someone run through the British Museum when the actors are working in a studio?*

For the museum chase, Hitchcock used photographs of the interiors as elements in Schüfftan composites.

In his account to Peter Bogdanovich, reproduced by the British Museum, Hitchcock explains that long-exposure photographs became backlit transparencies. These were reflected in a mirror whose coating was removed where the live action needed to appear. Only the necessary pieces of practical scenery were built.

This variation replaces the miniature with a photographic location. The camera joins a recorded place to a live performance during filming. [Hitchcock’s explanation, reproduced in an archived British Museum article](https://museum1346.rssing.com/chan-25349938/all_p1.html)

### Example: *Ghost* (2002)—a moving image inside a museum object

*How can a tiny ship appear inside a bottle displayed in an ordinary cabinet?*

Artists Susan Gamble and Michael Wenyon adapted Pepper’s Ghost for an installation at Norwich Castle Museum. Their cabinet placed moving images within apparently solid display objects, including a ship in a bottle.

A hidden LCD monitor supplied the imagery, with reflections bringing it into the display.

The source can therefore be a performer, an animated figure or a screen. What matters is the optical relationship between that source, the reflective surface and the viewer. [The artists’ description and installation image](https://wengam.com/projects/ghost/ghost.html)

## What makes the combination convincing
Three relationships deserve particular care.

**Position:** the reflected image must land where the scene requires it. A ghost whose feet miss the floor, or a doorway that fails to meet its reflected upper section, exposes the construction.

**Brightness:** Pepper’s Ghost depends on balancing the reflected subject against the transmitted scene. Because reflection adds light, it does not automatically erase the background behind a figure. That transparency is useful for apparitions and troublesome for solid objects.

**Perspective:** mirror composites must agree on viewpoint, apparent scale and lighting. A miniature can look enormous, but its architectural lines and shadows still have to belong to the same apparent space as the actors.

These are useful ways to evaluate the illusion: inspect the feet, the joins and the light.

## Where the pattern becomes restrictive
The effect is designed for a viewing position or a controlled range of positions. Moving too far sideways can reveal the apparatus or break the alignment. A camera makes this easier to manage because the image needs to work from its chosen viewpoint.

Interactions also require planning. A reflected performer cannot physically touch a prop in the visible set. Apparent contact must be choreographed between separate spaces.

Mirror composites impose boundaries on movement: actors must remain within the areas where the camera can see them through the glass. Camera movement can also disturb the relationship between the joined elements.

## How it connects to neighboring tricks
**[Miniatures](/pattern/film-miniatures)** provide small physical scenery; mirror compositing supplies one way to place actors within its apparent space.

**[Forced perspective](/pattern/film-forced-perspective)** aligns differently sized objects directly within a camera view. Mirrors let the contributing elements occupy separate directions or spaces.

**Matte paintings and digital compositing** also assemble pictures from parts, with different production freedoms. The distinctive feature here is that the combined view can exist optically before it reaches the camera.

**Duplicate-set mirror gags** solve another problem: creating an apparent reflection without revealing the camera. Pepper’s Ghost instead uses an actual reflection to make something appear where it physically isn’t.
