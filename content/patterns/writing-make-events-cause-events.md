A narrative can contain several individually engaging incidents and still feel assembled from separate pieces. A person takes a job, quarrels with a friend, moves premises, and loses an opportunity. The order is clear, but it is difficult to say why this particular sequence belongs together.

This pattern helps when a synopsis repeatedly says “and then,” scenes could exchange places without much consequence, or a new problem arrives whenever the previous one ends. **Let an event change the conditions from which a later event develops.**

That connection may involve a practical consequence, a person's response, new knowledge, or a constraint left behind. It need not make the outcome inevitable. A choice can create a situation to which several different responses would remain possible.

## Show what carries forward

Chronology tells readers when something happened. Causal development also makes available what the earlier event changed.

Imagine a fictional workshop shared by two bookbinders, Leah and Emre. Leah needs money for a journey to her sister's wedding. Emre is preparing presentation boxes for a fair. An outline reads:

> Leah accepted an urgent atlas repair. Emre completed his boxes in another workshop. He moved out of their shared premises. Leah cancelled her journey.

These incidents might be related, but the outline leaves their relationship for the reader to invent. Emre could have moved for reasons that had nothing to do with Leah, and her cancelled journey could be an independent event.

A developed version supplies the links:

> To earn the fare, Leah accepted an atlas repair that needed their entire shared bench. She promised the client three days without checking Emre's schedule. Emre took his unfinished boxes to a friend's workshop so that he could meet his own deadline. Working there reopened a conversation about renting a permanent space. When he decided to leave, Leah used her travel savings to cover the first month of rent alone.

The repair changes access to the bench. Emre responds to that change. His temporary arrangement renews an existing possibility, which he chooses to pursue. His departure changes Leah's expenses, and she responds by using the money she had intended for travel.

The wedding journey and the workshop now affect each other. The sequence has consequences that survive the end of each individual incident.

## Distinguish a condition from a decision

An event can make a response possible or pressing without determining it. Emre might work at home, negotiate a schedule, or refuse to move his materials. The temporary workshop might suit him badly. Leah might find someone else to share the rent.

The story needs to make its chosen developments intelligible through the people and circumstances involved. Emre's move will carry more weight if the passage establishes his concern about access to the bench, his earlier interest in the other premises, or his wish to work beside his friend.

That does not require a biography before every action. A prior conversation, a brief remembered disagreement, or a concrete feature of the new space may supply enough. The connection should be available where readers need it.

Without that basis, the sequence can resemble a mechanism in which every action automatically triggers the next. Human responses are part of the causal design, not interchangeable joints connecting predetermined scenes.

## Repair the missing relationship rather than its wording

Adding a connective cannot establish a causal link the material does not contain. _Leah repaired the atlas; therefore Emre moved out_ still needs an account of what the repair did to their arrangement.

Sometimes the missing piece is information the writer already knows: the bench was shared, the booking was unannounced, and Emre had a deadline. Making it available may be enough.

Sometimes the incidents genuinely are independent. The writer can preserve that independence, find a different structural relationship, or redesign the fictional events. Merely changing _then_ to _because_ would conceal the gap.

The same principle applies within a scene. A reply can prompt a revised request; a discovery can change where someone looks next. Across chapters, a broken agreement can influence a later decision long after its immediate practical problem has been resolved.

## Keep sequence and presentation distinct

Causal order need not be the order in which the reader learns the story. A narrative could open with Leah alone at the emptied bench and reveal the urgent repair later.

The earlier event still belongs to the sequence that produced the empty space. Delaying its presentation changes the reader's experience of discovery, not what happened between Leah and Emre.

Such an arrangement may be useful when the reader should first encounter the result and then revise an interpretation of it. It becomes confusing when the withheld information prevents basic understanding of the present scene without giving that uncertainty a purpose.

A later explanation can also complicate an apparently simple cause. Emre might have been considering departure for months. Leah's repair could become the occasion for leaving rather than its sole reason. The story gains precision by allowing both the immediate trigger and the longer preparation to exist.

## Let chance create a situation without doing all the work

Coincidence can start a sequence or interrupt one. Leah might encounter the atlas client by chance. That meeting need not be explained as the consequence of an earlier strategic decision.

What follows can still develop through her acceptance of the job, use of the bench, and response to Emre's departure. A chance beginning does not require unrelated chance solutions at every later turn.

Nor must a fortunate event be forbidden. A new workshop partner could appear unexpectedly. The question is what the story asks readers to experience: Leah dealing with the consequences of her arrangement, a comic run of luck, or a world in which plans repeatedly fail to control events.

Making every incident an expression of a single person's mistake can become just as artificial as leaving everything disconnected. Emre has preferences of his own; the workshop has costs; opportunity depends on circumstances beyond either person. Causal development need not become a scheme of punishment or reward.

## Preserve other kinds of connection

A memoir chapter may bring together several unrelated departures because they illuminate one another. An observational narrative may move by place, season, or recurring image. These forms need a readable principle of connection, but not necessarily a continuous chain of causes.

This pattern is most useful when consequences are the development the work promises to follow. It should not turn every recollection or juxtaposition into an implied explanation.

For nonfiction, the distinction is especially consequential. Events occurring in succession do not by themselves establish that one produced the other. Records, reported reasons, and independent circumstances may support different accounts. A writer should preserve uncertainty or attribute an explanation where the evidence warrants it, rather than invent a smoother chain.

## Source note

Aristotle's [Poetics, Parts IX–X](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/poetics.1.1.html), in S. H. Butcher's translation at the Internet Classics Archive, distinguishes mere succession from events arising through probable or necessary connection. Its subject is principally dramatic poetry, not a universal specification for modern prose. Leah, Emre, and the workshop are original fiction.
