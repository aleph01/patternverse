*An editorial pattern drawn from Nonviolent Communication. The examples and dialogue are fictional teaching illustrations.*

A friend describes a difficult week with their new manager. You suggest documenting incidents. They explain why that would be awkward. You recommend a private conversation. They tell you the manager never listens. You suggest looking for another job. They go quiet.

You have offered three possible solutions. Your friend may still be waiting for you to understand what the week has been like.

## Discover what kind of help is needed

NVC’s approach to empathy emphasizes attending to what someone is experiencing and what matters to them. Reflection, tentative guesses, silence, and clarifying questions can all support that understanding; empathy is not limited to repeating a prescribed sentence. [Ike Lasater, “Collaborating in the Workplace,” excerpt on listening](https://www.nonviolentcommunication.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/part2CIW.pdf)

The practical distinction is between preparing an answer and learning enough to know what an answer should address. A proposal may solve the wrong problem if you have not understood the speaker’s concern.

“Understand before solving” suggests an order of attention. It does not impose a mandatory emotional conversation before every practical answer. If someone asks where the spare key is, they may simply need the key.

## Advice that misses the central difficulty

Return to the friend describing their manager. Instead of proposing another tactic, you say:

> “It sounds exhausting to have your work questioned in front of the team. Is the hardest part the criticism itself, or not knowing what your manager expects?”

Your friend answers:

> “Neither, really. I can handle criticism. What gets me is being told privately that the work is good and then hearing something different in the meeting.”

The clarification changes the problem. The friend is struggling with inconsistency and trust, not simply a lack of confidence in handling feedback.

You reflect that difference:

> “So you can’t tell which version to plan around, and you feel exposed when the message changes publicly.”

“Exactly,” the friend says. They may want to say more. They may now ask for help preparing a conversation. You can also ask, “Would it help to think through what to do, or would you rather keep talking about it?”

The earlier suggestions might eventually be useful. Understanding makes it easier to assess which one, if any, fits.

## Listening when you are part of the problem

It is harder to listen when the complaint concerns you. A partner says, “You weren’t there for me yesterday.” You remember several things you did to help, and want to correct the record immediately.

A first response might be:

> “Was there a point yesterday when you particularly wanted me with you?”

They explain that they wanted company after receiving upsetting news, while you thought collecting groceries would be helpful. You can understand the unmet wish for company without agreeing that everything you did was uncaring.

Later, you can explain your intention and discuss how to recognize that kind of moment next time. Listening first does not surrender your account. It gives you a more accurate understanding of the account you are responding to.

You may also discover that the complaint is based on something you did not do. “I understand why that would have hurt. I want to clarify one detail…” preserves both empathy and the need for accuracy.

## Understanding can be quiet

A person returning from a difficult family visit may not want to identify an emotion or formulate a need. They may appreciate a cup of tea and a listener who does not interrupt.

Silence becomes useful when it is attentive rather than absent. A brief “That sounds like a lot to carry” may be enough. Another person may prefer direct questions. The speaker’s responses provide better guidance than a rule about how empathetic listening ought to sound.

If a guess misses, accept the correction. “No, I’m not angry; I’m embarrassed” is new information. Defending your guess would turn curiosity into a claim of authority over the person’s inner life.

## Common mistakes

**Turning empathy into an interview.** A sequence of increasingly intimate questions can feel intrusive. Follow what the person offers and leave room for them to decline or change direction.

**Treating reflection as a technique for securing agreement.** Someone may feel accurately understood and still reject your proposal. Listening is weakened if it is merely a preliminary step toward getting your way.

**Agreeing with every explanation.** “You felt humiliated” acknowledges an experience. “Everyone there wanted to humiliate you” adds an unverified account of other people’s intentions.

**Withholding requested expertise.** If someone clearly asks for practical help, offer it. Checking whether they want to be heard can be useful; repeatedly refusing to answer until they discuss feelings can become frustrating.

## Listening has conditions and limits

This pattern helps when advice repeatedly fails to land, when a person keeps returning to the same point, or when conflict has become a contest over whose account gets airtime.

It should also be reciprocal over time. Being a caring listener does not require unlimited availability. You can say, “I want to hear this properly, and I have fifteen minutes now,” or arrange another time.

Where an immediate action is needed, act. Understanding the story behind a spill can wait until the water is off and the floor is safe.
