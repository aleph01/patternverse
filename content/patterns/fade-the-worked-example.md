A completed example provides clarity but little independence. A blank page provides independence but can overwhelm a beginner with too many decisions at once.

Fade the Worked Example creates a bridge between the two. Start with a complete model, then gradually remove parts of the support until the learner can perform the task independently.

Someone learning portrait drawing might first study a fully constructed head with visible guidelines for proportion. The next exercise may provide only a few of those guides. Later there are only reference points, and eventually the learner begins from an empty page.

The same pattern works in writing. A learner might first examine an annotated essay showing what each paragraph is doing, then receive an outline with some missing sections, then only a broad question, and finally write independently.

This gradual removal of support reduces unnecessary difficulty. When a task is new, the learner may not yet have enough mental structure to handle every decision at once. A worked example allows attention to focus on the organization of the task without simultaneously inventing every step.

The danger is leaving the support in place too long. A singer who always rehearses while reading the lyrics may become good at reading and singing together rather than remembering the piece. A student who always writes from a rigid template may struggle when the template disappears.

The support should therefore become less complete as competence grows.

Fading is useful because independence does not need to arrive as a cliff. There can be many steps between “watch someone do it” and “do everything yourself.” Designing those intermediate steps often makes the transition smoother and more reliable.
