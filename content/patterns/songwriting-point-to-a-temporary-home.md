**Pattern 36 of 49 · Part 6: Bass Movement and Harmonic Color**

**Use when:** A chord arrival needs a brief directional push without changing the whole song’s home.

An A-minor chord has appeared several times in a C-major song. On its next appearance, an E7 arrives just before it. The Am now receives a stronger local approach, even though the song may soon return to C. A chord can become a temporary destination without taking over the entire song.

Point to a Temporary Home means preceding a destination chord with its own dominant. This is commonly called tonicization through a secondary or applied dominant. The technique adds local direction; it does not necessarily establish a full new key.

## Working choice

Introduce the destination’s dominant and hear the temporary emphasis within the surrounding key. A temporary emphasis is not automatically a modulation; a persistent new center needs further evidence.

## Build the relationship from the destination

To approach Am with its dominant, start from A and find E, a fifth above it. E major contains E–G-sharp–B. Add D to form E7. The G-sharp lies outside C major's ordinary scale and acts as a leading tone toward A in this local relationship.

In C-major analysis, E7 can be labeled V7/vi: dominant seventh of the sixth-degree minor chord. The slash here means “of,” not “put vi in the bass.” A slash chord such as C/E belongs to a different notation convention.

To approach G locally, use D or D7, containing F-sharp. In C, that is V/V or V7/V. The new accidental is evidence of a particular relationship, not a reason to assume the entire passage has already changed key.

## Insert the approach without losing time

Porch Light's base verse gives C two bars and Am the following two. One original variant keeps the eight-bar span but changes the second C bar to E7. The sequence becomes C | E7 | Am | Am | F | F | G | G. Am arrives at the same bar as before, now with a directed approach.

A subtler timing option keeps C for six beats and gives E7 the final two beats before Am. This changes harmonic rhythm as well as color. A performer needs the duration written clearly; a chord list alone cannot communicate the shorter approach.

![C moves through E7 toward Am, with G-sharp resolving to A while the wider C-major context remains visible.](/diagrams/songwriting/temporary-home.svg 'A secondary dominant points toward a local destination. Its presence alone does not establish a full modulation.')

The change is useful if Am deserves emphasis at that moment. If the lyric's important event arrives later over F, pointing strongly toward Am may redirect attention away from it. Harmonic direction should cooperate with the phrase's priorities.

## Check the melody through the approach

The original E7 contains G-sharp. A held G natural in the melody can create a prominent semitone friction with it. The writer can place a compatible melody note such as E, B, or D, change the timing of G, or retain the tension intentionally.

A local voice-leading example keeps E in an upper voice while G-sharp rises to A and D falls to C as E7 becomes Am. These movements make the destination audible. The bass can move E to A while upper lines connect economically.

Not every dominant-quality chord must resolve as expected. A writer can defer or redirect its arrival. That choice becomes meaningful when the expected destination has been made clear enough to hear the deviation.

## Bach: a dominant can point toward the dominant

Bach's **Prelude in C major, BWV 846**, gives a precise example in bars 10–11 of the [Mutopia score and MIDI edition](https://www.mutopiaproject.org/cgibin/piece-info.cgi?id=5). Bar 10 arpeggiates **D–F-sharp–A–C**, a D7 chord; bar 11 moves to **G–B–D**. Against the larger C-major setting, the reduction is **V7/V–V**: a dominant seventh directed toward G, which is itself the dominant of C. F-sharp supplies the sharpened pull toward G; C can move down to B. These pitch-class sets summarize the bars rather than reproducing every attack or doubling.

The [Netherlands Bach Society performance by Siebe Henstra](https://www.bachvereniging.nl/en/bwv/bwv-846) supplies a named performance context. Use the score's bar numbers rather than assuming a fixed timestamp across recordings. Porch Light's D7 before G uses the same destination-first principle on a smaller scale. The temporary emphasis on G does not, by itself, prove a lasting modulation: subsequent phrases determine whether G becomes home or resumes its role within C. This is a local harmonic direction, not a claim that the prelude is a chain of new keys.

## Distinguish a visit from a move

If E7–Am occurs briefly and the song resumes its C-centered progression, Am may be heard as a local destination. If a longer passage establishes A as home through its phrases, cadences, and continuing material, an A-minor key area becomes a stronger interpretation.

There is no universal bar count that settles the boundary. Duration, emphasis, and context all contribute. An analysis should explain the evidence rather than declare every accidental a modulation.

Platform Three offers a contrasting case. Its D-centered groove can remain modal without using an A7 approach. Adding C-sharp to create A7 would change its characteristic palette at that moment. That may be a useful alteration, but it is not needed to validate D as a center.

## Use direction sparingly when it serves the song

Secondary dominants help when a destination feels underprepared, when a phrase needs more local pull, or when a repeated progression deserves one specific change. They can become intrusive if every chord receives its own approach and the song loses the broader line.

Some styles draw much of their character from this kind of local direction; others rely on repeating harmony, modal centers, or texture. A technique's usefulness depends on the desired experience, not its position in a theory curriculum.
