## Purpose and problem

Lee declines an extra Sunday visit to his father. His sister Mara, who handles most of their father's daily care, hears a familiar message: Lee does not care enough. She stops asking what he could contribute and begins listing what he has failed to do.

Mara may have good reasons to be angry. The visit has still been declined, and she is still carrying the work. Yet the interpretation has begun deciding the conversation before Lee has explained this particular refusal.

These are fictional people. Their difficulty introduces a distinction that matters in systemic work: an event can be real while the meaning assigned to it remains open to investigation. Another perspective may reveal a missing account, a different priority, or a mistaken professional assumption. It need not make the situation pleasant or remove the disagreement.

## Core idea

**Open Another Perspective means making room for a different account while allowing the people concerned to question, correct, or reject it.**

The aim is greater freedom to investigate and respond. A therapist might explore how another participant understands an exchange, introduce a tentative interpretation, or reconsider their own explanation. The alternative remains a possibility until there is reason to give it more weight.

UCL's systemic competence framework describes holding more than one hypothesis, making the therapist's assumptions explicit, and revising hypotheses through feedback. It also includes circular questions that explore how participants understand relationships and one another's experiences. These are related practices, not interchangeable question scripts. [UCL: Specific systemic techniques](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/brain-sciences/sites/brain_sciences/files/migrated-files/Systemic_specific_systemic_techniques.pdf)

An alternative can be useful without being true, and plausible without being useful. Neither usefulness nor plausibility establishes another person's motives. Factual claims, consent, and responsibility still require attention.

## How it works

### Find the point where an account becomes a verdict

Descriptions often move quickly from what happened to what it proves. “Lee declined Sunday” becomes “Lee never cares,” while a professional's “she asks her mother for help” becomes “she is resisting independence.” The broader account may organize many observations, but it also determines what the listener notices next.

Separating an event from its interpretation creates room for inquiry. It does not require denying the event or declaring the original interpretation wrong. Mara's account of an unequal workload may remain well supported even if one explanation for Lee's refusal changes.

### Explore differences rather than supply a nicer story

Different systemic traditions offer different ways into this work. Circular inquiry explores relationships among participants' accounts. Reframing offers another context or meaning for an experience. Hypothesizing organizes provisional possibilities that can guide further questions.

Cecchin's account of curiosity, developed within the Milan tradition, revisits neutrality as an active openness to alternative views rather than a cold refusal to take responsibility. It is a theoretical stance, not evidence that curiosity alone improves outcomes. [Cecchin: An Invitation to Curiosity](https://shinuischool.co.il/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/Cecchin-G.-1987.-Hypothesizing-Circularity-and-Neutrality-Revisited.pdf)

A few alternatives can be enough. The important difference is whether they lead to a more informative conversation. Replacing “he is selfish” with “he is secretly protecting everyone” merely substitutes one unverified motive for another.

### Return authority to the person whose experience is being discussed

Imagining someone else's account can identify a question. It cannot answer that question on their behalf. Where appropriate, the person should have an opportunity to explain what fits, what is missing, and what has been misunderstood.

The same applies to professional explanations. “Could I be reading this as reluctance when you mean something else?” makes the therapist's account available for correction. An elegant question that only permits the preferred answer offers little real choice.

### Notice what the invitation does

The response matters as much as the wording. More detail, disagreement, hesitation, or distress may change what is useful to explore. A person who rejects a reframe may be correcting it, protecting a boundary, or saying the timing is wrong. Their disagreement should not automatically be renamed resistance.

This is the proposed practical value of the pattern: loosening a settled interpretation can make a different question or response available. Whether it helps in a particular encounter must remain open to feedback.

## Worked examples and case studies

### A refused visit and three possible meanings

*Fictional teaching scenario. All people, dialogue, and outcomes are invented.*

Mara wants dependable relief from caring for their father. Lee wants to remain involved without committing to visits he cannot manage. Initially, their discussion treats his refusal as evidence that he is indifferent and her anger as evidence that nothing he does will count.

The therapist asks Mara what the refusal means to her, then checks whether Lee recognizes that account. He acknowledges leaving her with too much work but disputes indifference. Before deciding what explains this Sunday, they consider three possibilities: low commitment, a practical obstacle, or disagreement about the care arrangement. These possibilities may overlap.

![The same fictional observation, Lee declining an extra Sunday visit, appears above three possible accounts: he does not care, he cannot make Sunday, or he wants a different care plan. Each is paired with a question that could clarify it. Green highlights the questions, not a preferred explanation.](../assets/systemic-therapy/perspective-care-accounts.svg 'The event is held constant while its possible meanings are explored. None of these accounts has been established by the drawing. Green marks opportunities for inquiry and correction; it does not identify the most sympathetic account as true.')

Lee explains that this visit conflicts with an extra shift he feels unable to refuse because of rent arrears. He also describes handling their father's prescription orders remotely, work Mara had not counted. Mara recognizes that contribution but says it does not cover evenings when she cannot leave the house.

The therapist asks, “What changes in your understanding, and what remains a problem even with that explanation?” Mara can distinguish this Sunday's constraint from the continuing lack of relief. Lee can acknowledge that doing something useful does not settle whether the division of care is fair.

In this invented continuation, they discuss a more specific commitment and whether their father would accept additional support. They have not agreed on the whole arrangement. Their anger has not disappeared, and Lee's financial pressure remains. The useful difference is that disagreement now concerns particular contributions and constraints, rather than requiring a final verdict about whether either sibling cares.

### When independence is the professional's interpretation

*Fictional teaching scenario. All people, circumstances, dialogue, and outcomes are invented.*

Rae is an adult with an intellectual disability who receives support in her own flat. Her mother brings cooked meals each week. Some staff see this as overprotection and propose reducing family help as a goal. Her mother understands the meals as an ordinary expression of family care.

The therapist initially shares the staff's assumption that less parental involvement would indicate progress. Rae, using accessible written prompts and time to answer, says she wants to keep the meals. She also wants her mother to arrange visits beforehand rather than arriving whenever she chooses.

The therapist acknowledges having treated independence as doing more alone. The revised inquiry asks which decisions Rae wants to control and which assistance she chooses. The weekly meals stay the same; their meaning changes from automatic evidence of dependence to a form of support whose fit needs to be considered with Rae.

Staff recognize that reducing assistance was their proposed measure of progress, not Rae's stated priority. Her mother is relieved that care is valued but finds the request for advance notice uncomfortable. Different views of family involvement become discussable without making culture or disability an explanation for everyone's preferences.

In the fictional continuation, the team revises the goal around Rae's control over visits and support choices. Her mother agrees to discuss visiting times, although following through remains uncertain. Service resources are still limited. Opening the perspective has corrected a professional assumption; it has not proved that every existing arrangement is wanted or that every requested support can be supplied.

### A caring interpretation offered too quickly

*Published conversation-analysis case, summarized from Muntigl and Horvath (2023). The paper calls the couple Colin and Anna.*

The researchers examined selected excerpts of couples therapy using narrative and solution-focused techniques. In the exchange discussed here, Colin was distressed after recently learning about an affair Anna had had years earlier. The therapist first acknowledged his hurt, which Colin confirmed.

The conversation then shifted toward how Anna might now be showing care and trying to repair trust. The questions invited Colin to consider a relationship-building interpretation of her current conduct. However, he hesitated, spoke quietly, and became increasingly tearful. The therapist continued pursuing the positive thread and suggested possible evidence of Anna's wish to repair the relationship, including her presence in therapy.

The authors identify both the relational work these questions attempted and a possible timing problem: the alternative perspective may have been introduced too quickly to attend adequately to Colin's distress. An invitation to understand another person can carry a strong preference about what the listener should conclude.

The paper also places both featured treatments overall in an improved outcome category. That broader finding must be retained alongside the difficult exchange. It neither makes the distress irrelevant nor shows that this particular questioning caused the improvement. The study analyzes how conversation unfolds; it does not experimentally establish the benefit or harm of a question format. [Muntigl and Horvath: Strategic use of observer-perspective questions in couples therapy](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1229991/full)

For this field guide, the distinction is between opening an account for consideration and moving someone toward it before their existing experience has been sufficiently heard. The paper does not document the specific collaborative repair one might propose in hindsight, so none is added here as an outcome.

## Conditions for useful application

A participant needs a credible way to say that an interpretation does not fit. This requires more than polite permission: the therapist must be willing to revise or withdraw the idea. Accessible communication, interpretation where needed, and time to respond affect whose account can influence the conversation.

Safety changes what can be discussed together. Understanding someone who has used coercion must not become an obligation imposed on the person harmed. NICE recommends private, one-to-one enquiry about domestic abuse in a safe setting. Perspective work should not override that need or imply that joint discussion is always appropriate. [NICE: Domestic violence and abuse, recommendation 6](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ph50/chapter/Recommendations#recommendation-6-ensure-trained-staff-ask-people-about-domestic-violence-and-abuse)

## When to use it

This pattern is useful when one interpretation determines every subsequent response, when participants disagree about what an action means, or when professional language has become more authoritative than the person's own account.

Two brief fictional examples show its range. A parent describes a teenager's request for privacy as rejection; the teenager describes wanting some conversations with friends to remain their own. Both can discuss privacy without deciding the family bond is unimportant. Elsewhere, a therapist hears a client's repeated agreement as engagement, but the client explains that disagreeing with a professional feels unsafe. The therapist's interpretation needs revision, not reinforcement.

When a concrete fact, urgent need, or clear violation requires action, generating more possible meanings may add little.

## Tradeoffs

Considering another account can take emotional effort, particularly when the person has already explained their experience repeatedly. More possibilities can also make a decision harder. The goal is an informative opening, not an endless catalogue of explanations.

An alternative may clarify disagreement without reconciling it. Someone can understand why a request matters to another person and still decline it. Practical shortages, incompatible commitments, and unequal workloads remain consequential even when communication becomes more accurate.

## Common mistakes and limitations

**Confusing perspective with factual equivalence.** Different experiences deserve consideration, but accounts can contain factual errors. Evidence does not become optional because meanings differ.

**Smuggling in the preferred answer.** “How does his controlling behavior show he cares?” presupposes a benevolent meaning. A more open inquiry would first establish what happened, its effects, and how the person understands it.

**Turning group membership into an explanation.** Culture, class, religion, gender, or disability may shape experience without determining what one individual thinks. Ask about their relevance rather than assigning a worldview.

**Using understanding to cancel accountability.** Another person's fear, history, or intentions do not erase the effects of harmful actions or the need for boundaries.

**Treating acceptance as the outcome.** Agreeing with the therapist can reflect compliance. Rejecting an interpretation can be valuable information. Neither response alone demonstrates clinical improvement.
