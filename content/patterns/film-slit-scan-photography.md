*Build an image over time, turning motion into shape and light into apparent space.*

A spacecraft must enter somewhere beyond ordinary experience. A model tunnel would look too concrete. A painted background would look static. The filmmaker wants the audience to feel movement through a space that has no recognizable walls.

Slit-scan offers an unusual solution: **the apparent environment can emerge from the way the image is exposed**.

A narrow opening admits a small portion of an illuminated design. Coordinated movement during the exposure stretches that light across the recording. Flat artwork can become a luminous corridor; a solid object can appear to elongate.

The broader family also includes strip photography and digital techniques that assemble images from narrow slices recorded at different times. These methods share an interest in building a picture progressively, although their mechanisms differ.

## The puzzle: where did they build the tunnel?

In the Star Gate sequence of *2001: A Space Odyssey*, colored patterns extend toward a distant vanishing point. The viewer seems to rush between enormous walls of light.

There was no corresponding full-size tunnel.

Douglas Trumbull’s slit-scan system combined illuminated artwork, a narrow opening and controlled camera movement. A long exposure accumulated the changing image onto a single frame. Repeating the process with incremental changes produced moving footage.

Trumbull describes the result as an image that did not exist as a physical scene: it gave the experience of going somewhere through patterns of light. [Douglas Trumbull’s firsthand explanation for AMC](https://www.amc.com/blogs/james-camerons-story-of-science-fiction-qa-douglas-trumbull-visual-effects-director--1005555)

Think of a camera exposure as something that can be constructed throughout its duration.

In the classic cinematic arrangement:

1. **Restrict the light.** An opaque mask exposes only a narrow part of backlit artwork.
2. **Move during the exposure.** The camera and artwork move in a coordinated relationship while one film frame remains exposed.
3. **Let the light trace a shape.** Changing geometry makes the narrow illuminated image sweep and stretch across the frame.
4. **Repeat for animation.** Change the starting conditions slightly and expose the next frame.

The final movement comes from a sequence of painstakingly constructed images. It is not simply ordinary footage of a camera traveling quickly.

Trumbull’s contemporary technical article calls the broader process **animated streak photography**—a useful description because the essential material is controlled movement recorded within an exposure. [Trumbull’s illustrated article in *American Cinematographer*, October 1969](https://cdn.theasc.com/AMERICAN_CINEMATOGRAPHER_VOL.50_1969_10.pdf)

### Example: *2001: A Space Odyssey* (1968)—movement without a destination you can recognize

*How do you convey an extraordinary journey without explaining what the traveler is passing through?*

The Star Gate’s slit-scan imagery supplies strong perspective and apparent forward motion while withholding an ordinary environment. You recognize the sensation of travel before you can identify the surrounding material.

That makes the technique useful dramatically: it offers a convincing visual experience without turning the unknown into a familiar piece of architecture.

The sequence contains other kinds of imagery too. Slit-scan explains its characteristic corridors of patterned light, rather than every effect in the journey. [Trumbull discussing the invention and its purpose](https://www.denofgeek.com/movies/douglas-trumbull-interview-2001-silent-running-the-future-of-cinema/)

### Example: *Doctor Who* (1973 titles)—the opening determines the tunnel’s shape

*How can a time tunnel take the shape of a circle, a diamond or even a person’s outline?*

For the 1973 titles, designer Bernard Lodge adapted the process after reading Trumbull’s explanation.

His account describes a rostrum camera exposing one frame while tracking along its column, photographing a backlit outline. Different openings produced different tunnel forms: a circular opening, an outline of Jon Pertwee and a diamond for the new logo.

In the linked title sequence, compare the circular tunnel with the diamond-shaped passage around the logo. The revealing detail is that the “slit” needn’t be a straight line. **Its shape becomes part of the apparent space.**

Lodge worked within about five feet of camera travel, illustrating how the principle could be adapted to a much smaller setup. [Bernard Lodge’s account and the original title sequence, Ravensbourne’s BBC Motion Graphics Archive](https://www.ravensbourne.ac.uk/bbc-motion-graphics-archive/doctor-who-1973)

### Example: *Star Trek: The Next Generation*—stretching a rigid ship

*How can the Enterprise elongate like a rubber band if the filming miniature is solid?*

Instead of photographing artwork through an opening, the effects team projected a narrow band of light onto the ship model.

The band scanned along the Enterprise while the camera pulled back during the exposure. Different portions of the ship were therefore recorded from different camera positions, producing an apparently stretched form.

Visual-effects supervisor Rob Legato describes this as adapting the slit-scan idea from two-dimensional artwork to a three-dimensional miniature. The model’s construction remained rigid; the exposure changed its recorded shape. [Legato’s explanation in *American Cinematographer*](https://theasc.com/article/star-trek-50-part-vii-next-generation-production/)

## A related puzzle: how can a photograph show a car that never had that shape?
Strip recording provides another way to understand how time can alter a picture.

Imagine repeatedly sampling the same narrow vertical view as a car passes it. Place those samples beside one another, with earlier moments on the left and later moments on the right.

The resulting width represents elapsed time. If part of the car passes slowly, it occupies more recorded columns and appears stretched. Faster passage produces fewer columns and compression.

Andrew Davidhazy’s account of digital strip cameras explains this central property: time becomes one dimension of the recorded image. Photo-finish cameras are a practical application of that principle. [Davidhazy: digital strip-camera theory and examples](https://www.davidhazy.org/andpph/text-digital-strip-camera.html)

Digital strip-camera examples illustrate this relationship between speed and recorded shape. They are a related method rather than a reconstruction of Trumbull’s optical apparatus.

## Why the different methods belong together
In ordinary picture-making, we tend to imagine a frame as a view of a scene at one moment.

These techniques make the timing of its construction visible. The image depends on which portion is admitted or sampled, when it is recorded, and how the subject or camera moves meanwhile.

Optical slit-scan accumulates controlled traces of light. Digital strip assembly places selected samples beside one another. Related time-displacement effects assign different moments to different parts of a video image.

Their shared creative opportunity is to **design the relationship between time and picture space**. Golan Levin’s illustrated catalogue documents many variations, from cinematic effects to interactive installations. [Catalogue of slit-scan artworks and research](https://www.flong.com/archive/texts/lists/slit_scan/index.html)

## When the pattern helps—and where it becomes difficult
Slit-scan is especially useful for experiences in which ordinary geometry should loosen: extraordinary travel, transformation, temporal disturbance or abstract titles.

Its difficulty is coordination. Exposure, movement, light and source imagery jointly determine the result. Changes that seem small in the apparatus can have substantial consequences in the finished image. The historical optical process also demanded considerable production time.

Digital versions offer easier experimentation, but require a clear sampling rule. Copying one fixed column over time produces a different result from taking successive columns from different moments.

## Neighboring tricks

**Motion blur** also records movement during an exposure, but slit-scan constrains which light contributes and how its trace develops.

**Time-lapse** changes the interval between recorded moments; strip assembly changes how those moments occupy a picture.

**Video feedback** creates evolving patterns by feeding an image back into itself. It produced earlier *Doctor Who* titles before Lodge adopted slit-scan.

**Digital warping** can imitate stretched shapes directly. Slit-scan provides a particular reason for those shapes: their geometry emerges from the timing of the recording.
