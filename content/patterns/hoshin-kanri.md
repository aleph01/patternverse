**Core question:** How can an organization turn strategic priorities into feasible, coordinated work and keep learning whether that work is producing results?

## What it is for

Imagine a hypothetical equipment maker announcing that reliable delivery is its priority. Sales launches a forecasting project, engineering proposes new planning software, and production adds improvement workshops. Each department can explain its initiative. Nobody has established which delivery problems matter most, whether the projects fit together, or where the necessary capacity will come from.

By midyear, routine work has displaced the improvement effort. The annual strategy still looks convincing; its execution is fragmented.

Hoshin Kanri connects direction, commitments, and review. It helps leadership choose a few important changes, work through their implications with the people who must deliver them, and revisit progress throughout the year. It applies across an organization or a substantial business unit, especially where priorities cross management levels and functional boundaries.

## The core idea

**Select a few strategic priorities, negotiate how the organization will pursue them, and use recurring reviews to learn and adjust.**

Also called policy deployment or strategy deployment, Hoshin Kanri links strategic direction with plans containing goals, actions, responsibilities, timing, and measures. The Lean Enterprise Institute emphasizes alignment both vertically, between leadership and frontline work, and horizontally, between functions. [LEI, _Strategy Deployment_](https://www.lean.org/lexicon-terms/strategy-deployment/)

Its distinctive interaction is **catchball**: proposals move back and forth as leaders and teams examine targets, methods, resources, and dependencies. The purpose is a credible commitment, not merely acknowledgment of a target handed down from above.

The process also applies plan–do–check–act thinking: make a plan, carry out the work, examine results, and adjust. A completed planning document starts the management cycle rather than finishes it. [LEI, _Hoshin Kanri_](https://www.lean.org/lexicon-terms/hoshin-kanri/)

Shared outcomes provide a common result. Hoshin Kanri adds a deployment and review process for concentrating strategic effort. It complements daily management, which must still sustain ordinary operations; every recurring task need not become a strategic initiative.

## How it works

![Leadership proposes a few priorities. Teams and functions exchange targets, proposed methods, capacity, and dependencies through catchball before agreeing owned commitments. Execution produces evidence for recurring reviews, which adjust the work and inform the next planning cycle.](../assets/organizational-design/hoshin-kanri.svg)

**Start from the actual situation.** Examine customer needs, performance gaps, recurring obstacles, and longer-term direction. Include evidence from people close to the work. In the equipment-maker illustration, late deliveries might arise from unstable specifications or missing components; declaring production efficiency the answer would bypass that diagnosis.

**Choose a few priorities.** Identify changes important enough to warrant concentrated leadership attention. Connect longer-term ambitions with a manageable planning period, commonly an annual plan. Say what will be deferred and protect essential operating work. A strategic label does not create extra capacity.

**Develop proposals through catchball.** Leaders explain the desired change; managers and teams propose methods, targets, resources, and support. They identify unrealistic assumptions and return alternatives. This exchange also runs between functions: procurement and production may need to agree replenishment changes together rather than optimize separate plans.

**Settle authority and commitments.** Leadership remains responsible for final priorities and unresolved resource tradeoffs. Catchball is meaningful consultation and negotiation, not an unlimited veto for every participant. Give each commitment an owner and confirm contributions from other units before treating them as available.

**Make the connections visible.** Record the priority, baseline, intended result, proposed actions, measures, owners, and review dates. An A3 report or an X-matrix can help show relationships; neither substitutes for agreement. A simple plan that people can explain is more useful than an elaborate diagram containing unconfirmed promises. [Mark Reich, June 2025 interview](https://www.leanblog.org/2025/06/hoshin-kanri-managing-on-purpose-mark-reich/)

**Execute through real problem-solving.** Teams test and improve processes while maintaining operations. Track both whether planned actions happen and whether they improve the intended result. Completing a training session or installing software does not establish more reliable delivery.

**Review and adjust.** Meet at a useful cadence, often monthly, to examine gaps, learning, and support needs. Distinguish weak execution from a mistaken assumption about what would help. Authorized leaders change actions, capacity, or priorities when evidence warrants it. Reflect across the planning period so the next cycle starts from what was learned.

## Why it works

Concentrating priorities makes competition for attention explicit. Catchball then brings local knowledge into the plan before execution exposes its impossibilities. Horizontal discussion can reveal that one department’s improvement requires another’s time or creates work elsewhere.

Recurring review makes strategy part of management rather than an annual presentation. Comparing expected and actual results tests the reasoning behind the plan, while named commitments make follow-through visible.

These benefits depend on leadership behavior. Teams must be able to challenge assumptions without being treated as resistant, and leaders must be willing to stop or defer work. If every initiative survives and every target is fixed before discussion, the process can produce more documentation without better choices.

## Examples

### Maersk North Europe Operations, beginning in 2017

In a March 2021 firsthand account, Agne Nainyte describes Maersk’s North Europe operations leadership responding to persistent firefighting in October 2017. They developed a three-year breakthrough plan and translated it into annual priorities. [Nainyte, _3 Lessons Maersk Learned from Adopting Hoshin Kanri_](https://www.lean.org/the-lean-post/articles/3-lessons-maersk-learned-from-adopting-hoshin-kanri/)

One customer-satisfaction priority led operations staff to observe customer service. They found fragmented, poorly updated information and developed a shared information website. Another priority changed network development so planners considered operators’ evidence about schedules that were impractical to deliver.

The arrangement connected strategic concerns with specific process problems and information flowing back from operations. Leaders also made time for strategic work by stepping away from some daily decisions. Nainyte reports performance improvements, but the account does not isolate Hoshin Kanri from other changes. This describes the historical regional initiative, not Maersk’s current company-wide structure.

### Turner Construction’s New York business unit, 2019–2022

Doug Cooper’s account in an April 2022 LEI interview describes Turner New York building a management system around Hoshin Kanri and A3 problem-solving from 2019. Senior leaders, general managers, and department heads developed connected plans through discussions with the levels above and below them. [LEI, _Meeting Strategic Objectives_, interview with Cooper](https://www.lean.org/the-lean-post/articles/meeting-strategic-objectives/)

Broad priorities became more specific objectives and worksite problems. Monthly meetings reviewed progress and the problems addressed through A3s; planning for the next year began in October. Frontline experience also informed priorities, while lean managers and coaches supported the process.

This shows a continuing management rhythm linking direction, operational problem-solving, and review. It did not require replacing the conventional hierarchy. LEI had participated in coaching the unit, so the source is an involved practitioner account. It documents the arrangement and reported learning rather than independently proving a competitive advantage or describing present practice.

## Useful when

- **Strategy becomes a long project list.** Initiatives compete for the same people without an explicit order of importance.
- **Targets travel downward but constraints do not travel upward.** Plans rely on capacity or assumptions that teams cannot challenge.
- **Departments plan separately.** Strategic progress depends on commitments across functions that remain unconfirmed.
- **Review happens too late.** Leaders discover near year-end that completed activities did not produce the intended change.

## Common mistakes and limits

**Cascading targets and calling it catchball.** Allow proposals to change in response to evidence, including resource limits and dependencies.

**Making everything strategic.** Protect a few priorities while sustaining routine operations. Otherwise deployment merely renames an overloaded portfolio.

**Treating a matrix as the method.** Templates can reveal relationships; they cannot create ownership, resources, or a productive disagreement.

**Reviewing colors instead of problems.** A red status should prompt investigation and decisions, rather than pressure to report green. Keep quality, cost, and customer effects visible alongside headline targets.

**Following the annual plan despite new reality.** Preserve direction where appropriate, but revise assumptions and commitments transparently. Frequent changes also have a cost: teams need enough stability to test an approach.

Small organizations may need only a lightweight version. Where survival requires immediate action, resolve urgent constraints first; a planning cycle cannot substitute for operational control or leadership decisions.
