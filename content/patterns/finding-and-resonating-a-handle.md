A person describes a difficult meeting as stressful. The word is accurate but does not help them understand why the meeting continues to occupy them. After a pause, another phrase appears: "I was there as furniture." It captures something about being present, useful, and unconsulted that the original label missed.

## Core idea

Finding and resonating a handle, in Focusing, means letting an expression emerge from a felt sense and checking whether it fits. A handle can be a word, phrase, image, gesture, or other symbol. Its usefulness depends on the person's recognition, not on how clever or persuasive it sounds.

Resonating is this movement between the expression and the experience. The person notices whether something is captured, omitted, or altered. A small shift in breath or posture may accompany recognition, but no particular bodily response is required. Tears, relief, or a nod do not by themselves establish accuracy.

The defining move is a two-way exchange between expression and experience. A phrase does not merely label something already known: hearing it can reveal what was missing. Resonating preserves that feedback. A useful handle is specific enough to bring the experience back into view, while remaining open to correction or further development.

## What changes in the experience

The first expression is provisional. In the example, furniture may initially fit, then feel too passive. The person changes it to "a stagehand waiting for permission to speak." That revision adds both effort and withheld participation. No therapist needs to select which metaphor is objectively correct.

The process can also work through ordinary language. Someone may move from "I'm angry" to "I'm angry that I was expected to know without being told." The added precision matters even if no striking image appears.

### Drawing the felt sense

Focusing-Oriented Art Therapy gives symbolization a visual form. Color, shape, spacing, line, and composition can carry aspects of experience that are hard to express verbally. The person alternates between the drawing and the felt sense, checking whether the image fits and whether the experience has changed.

Imagine someone drawing a tight gray circle to represent an uneasy family visit. As they look back at it, the circle feels too closed. They add a narrow opening and recognize that they do want contact, provided there is a way to leave. The drawing has helped specify a condition, not revealed a diagnosis.

The therapist does not interpret the gray as depression or the opening as avoidance. The image belongs to its maker. A person might use the same shape to convey excitement, concentration, pressure, or comfort. Visual symbolism has no reliable universal dictionary for these meanings.

A drawing can be changed while the person checks the experience again. The change itself may be informative: more space, a different edge, or the absence of a figure can matter. The task is not to produce an aesthetically successful picture, and verbal interpretation need not follow every mark. Its maker decides whether the image remains connected to the concern.

## A worked example

Returning to the meeting, the therapist likes the word 'stagehand' and begins exploring invisibility. The client corrects this: being unseen is sometimes welcome; waiting for permission is the painful part. They revise the phrase to 'ready with the scenery, but someone else decides whether the play starts.' That wording brings a more specific frustration into view.

The client notices an urge to say one sentence at the next meeting before being invited. Imagining that sentence, however, changes the metaphor again: they do not want to seize the stage, only to be recognized as a contributor. The image has supported discovery because it can be altered. A fixed interpretation about invisibility would have missed the issue of shared authority.

## Why the move may help

This is a rationale for articulation and attention, not proof that the expression reveals a hidden truth. A metaphor may resonate because it is evocative, familiar, or suggested by the therapist. It still needs to remain revisable and answerable to external facts.

A stable expression can reduce the effort of repeatedly reconstructing an elusive concern. It can also make distinctions portable: the person later recognizes 'waiting for permission' in a new setting and can ask whether the same meaning is present. That recognition is a hypothesis to examine, not a reason to interpret every hesitation through the same metaphor.

Focusing treats symbolization as capable of carrying experience forward. An observable shift might be a clearer sentence, a change in felt quality, or a newly imaginable response. None establishes that the symbol is uniquely correct or that bodily resonance verifies an external fact. The expression earns its place by remaining useful and revisable.

## When it helps—and what can get in the way

The pattern becomes less useful when symbolization turns into performance. Someone may produce elaborate metaphors while becoming less connected to experience. Another may feel pressured to create art or find a profound sentence. An ordinary description, silence, or an unresolved sense can be adequate.

The pattern fits a description that is accurate but too general to work with: stressful, bad, stuck, or complicated. It may be unnecessary when an ordinary sentence already conveys the issue well. Some people prefer plain language to images, and some recognize a gesture more readily than a word. Elaborateness is not a measure of depth.

## Boundaries and common misunderstandings

A therapist may offer a tentative phrase, but should make correction easy. A client who says that a phrase is almost right is not obstructing progress. The difference may be precisely where new meaning lies.

A practitioner's appealing metaphor can easily dominate the encounter. Tentative wording helps, but the stronger test is whether correction changes the conversation. A client should not have to defend an ordinary phrase against the therapist's more dramatic one. The same principle applies to art: shared associations may be explored, but a symbol dictionary cannot determine personal meaning.
