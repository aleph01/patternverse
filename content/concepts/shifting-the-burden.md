## A recognizable situation

Lin leads a local technical team. When an unfamiliar equipment fault holds up a customer, she calls Tomas, a central specialist. He solves it correctly and quickly. The customer gets working equipment; the local technicians return to their other duties.

This works so well that joint troubleshooting sessions are repeatedly cancelled. Months later, documentation is outdated, a knowledgeable technician has left, and more faults require Tomas. When he is unavailable, work stops.

In this hypothetical situation, nobody is producing bad repairs. Successful assistance is gradually replacing the learning that would make assistance less necessary.

## The underlying pattern

**Shifting the Burden occurs when a response relieves a recurring symptom while displacing or weakening a more durable way of addressing it, increasing dependence on the relief.** The burden shifts toward the symptomatic response and away from developing or maintaining the underlying capability.

It is a system dynamics **archetype**: a recurring feedback structure. The pattern appears in _The Systems Thinker_ in 1990; Daniel H. Kim's later treatment distinguishes recurring rescue from maintaining a deliberately chosen capability. [Early account](https://thesystemsthinker.com/wp-content/uploads/pdfs/010402.pdf), [Kim's explanation](https://thesystemsthinker.com/shifting-the-burden-moving-beyond-a-reactive-orientation/)

When an outside helper increasingly takes over, the narrower term is **Shifting the Burden to the Intervener**. Outside help alone does not establish the trap. The critical question is: **What needed capacity does this arrangement prevent us from developing or maintaining?**

## What keeps it going

Two routes can reduce Lin's unresolved faults. Tomas can take over now, or technicians can develop the ability to resolve a defined class of faults locally. Each is a **balancing feedback loop**: more unresolved work prompts a response that reduces it.

The routes operate on different timescales. Expert intervention can work immediately. Supervised practice must accumulate, understanding must be tested, and suitable cases must recur before local capability reliably improves.

Relief also changes decisions. With the immediate crisis over, managers redeploy training time. Takeovers remove opportunities to diagnose faults, and neglected documentation becomes less useful. Lower local capability then generates more specialist calls. This **reinforcing loop** strengthens reliance on rescue.

Capability is an accumulated resource: skills, usable procedures, and experienced people. Less practice can slow capability growth without immediately reducing existing skill. In this example, turnover, disuse, and outdated procedures also remove usable capability; it declines when those losses exceed what practice and maintenance replace. A training session is an input, not proof that this stock has increased. These links are hypotheses to investigate in Lin's team, not established by naming the archetype.

## Why the obvious responses fail

**Provide faster rescue.** This may improve customer service while leaving the dependence mechanism intact. It can also make the local capability gap less visible.

**Tell people to become independent.** If every difficult case is removed from them and learning time remains interruptible, the instruction changes little.

**Send a manual afterward.** Documentation can help, but receiving an explanation does not demonstrate that someone can diagnose the next unfamiliar fault.

**Withdraw support to force learning.** Removing the functioning route before another exists can leave customers without either solution. Meadows recommends rebuilding the system's capability before removing an intervention on which it depends. [_Thinking in Systems_, chapter 5](https://www.sustainable.soltechdesigns.com/meadows.pdf)

## Possible ways out

**Specify the capability worth retaining.** Lin and the service owner can identify recurring fault classes that local technicians should handle, with clear quality and escalation criteria. Rare, highly specialized problems may sensibly remain centralized. Kim explicitly distinguishes prudent outsourcing from unintentionally losing a capability the organization needs. [Capability and outsourcing](https://thesystemsthinker.com/shifting-the-burden-moving-beyond-a-reactive-orientation/)

**Make relief support learning.** For suitable cases, Tomas can guide diagnosis while a technician operates the tools and explains the reasoning. Record reusable findings and test transfer on another case. Joint work costs time and requires teaching skill; passive observation may reproduce the old arrangement.

**Protect the resources through the delay.** A manager must reserve local participation and specialist coaching time even when the queue looks healthy. This may require less routine work, additional staffing, or longer delivery commitments. Calling training a priority does not supply those hours.

**Reduce assistance against demonstrated capacity.** Assess independent work, error rates, and escalation judgment before changing coverage. Keep an exception route for unfamiliar or high-consequence cases. Some continuing support may remain worthwhile; complete self-sufficiency is not the objective in every setting.

These are practical design proposals. They change how support and learning are connected, rather than assuming goodwill or a training announcement will overcome the resource conflict.

## A worked example

### Keep the local team in the repair

Lin asks Tomas to send fuller explanations after each takeover. Repairs still leave local ownership before technicians attempt a diagnosis. Documentation grows, but opportunities to practice remain scarce.

They review which faults could reasonably be handled locally and what knowledge is missing. In the hypothetical diagnosis, repeated takeovers have displaced supervised learning, while turnover and outdated procedures have further weakened capability. Those relationships would need evidence from actual cases and staffing history.

They redesign suitable interventions as guided joint work. A local technician proposes a diagnosis, carries out the safe steps, and records the reasoning; Tomas coaches and checks. High-risk or unusually complex cases remain specialist-led. Lin protects time for the local work by deferring other duties rather than expecting learning to occur in spare moments.

At first, joint cases take more specialist attention than direct rescue. The team may complete fewer other tasks, and urgent customers cannot always wait for instruction. In the stipulated useful outcome, technicians gradually demonstrate independent handling of the defined case types. Tomas’s reduced involvement follows verified competence, not an arbitrary withdrawal date.

The intervention changes what help produces: a completed repair and a learning opportunity, rather than a completed repair alone. It does not prove that every case should become training or that expertise can be transferred quickly.

If Tomas has no coaching capacity, a smaller programme, another instructor, fewer commitments, or a consciously centralized service may be necessary. Immediate removal of specialist support can leave customers without repairs before local capability exists. Dependence can be a deliberate division of labor; the trap requires the relief process to weaken a capability the organization still intends to maintain.

## Limits and lookalikes

Dependence is not inherently harmful. A local office may rationally buy expert help for rare problems. The label fits when relief crowds out a capability needed for the chosen service model. Whose capability matters, and who pays to develop it, are substantive decisions.

Repenning and Sterman's 2001 research combined organizational case studies, interviews, records, and quantitative measures with feedback models. They describe a **capability trap** in which urgent production displaces improvement and declining capability increases urgency. This supports investigating crowding out; it does not validate the hypothetical coaching arrangement or establish universal training effects. Their simulated improvement paths are distinct from observed case histories. [Original research](https://web.mit.edu/nelsonr/www/Repenning%3DSterman_CMR_su01_.pdf)

An archetype is also not a diagnosis of addiction, disability, or individual motivation. This article concerns organizational capability and resources; it cannot justify withholding support merely because someone relies on it.
