## A recognizable situation

Iris and Noah run competing shops in a town whose demand has shrunk. While both stay open, each loses money. Either shop could be viable with enough of the other's customers, so each hopes the rival will close first.

They recognize that the losses cannot continue indefinitely. Yet another difficult month can look worthwhile if the other shop seems close to leaving. Closing stops future operating losses but gives up the chance to be the survivor.

This hypothetical setting assumes that one shop could serve the remaining market profitably, and that staying open has no separate investment benefit large enough to explain the losses. Not every struggling business is in an attrition contest.

## The underlying pattern

**A war of attrition is a contest in which participants incur costs while waiting for others to concede, and concession gives the remaining participant a more valuable outcome.** In the basic two-player version, the contest ends when the first player withdraws.

The strategic question is: **How much more will I spend waiting for you to give up?**

Maynard Smith's 1974 work on animal conflicts introduced a foundational model of this structure into evolutionary game theory. Broom's specialist account describes the classic contest: the longer-persisting player wins, but both pay for the time until the first withdrawal. Biological models explain selection among strategies; human negotiation additionally involves intentions, institutions, and agreements. [Model and historical account](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/988/1/Comtb.pdf)

## What keeps it going

Continuing costs Iris money but preserves a chance of the profitable survivor position. Exiting ends those future losses but hands that opportunity to Noah. He faces the same comparison. His visible difficulties can encourage Iris to expect that one more period will bring his exit; her difficulties can encourage him in turn.

The participants choose when to exit and observe whether the rival remains. They cannot see the rival's intended stopping time. In the simple benchmark, the prospective survivor benefit and waiting costs are known, neither runs out of funds, there is no deadline, and waiting creates no independent productive value. This is one continuing contest, without later reputation rewards.

Actual shops have finite reserves and changing demand. The benchmark isolates the incentive to outlast; it does not establish that shops can wait forever or that their future profits are certain. Declining-industry exit is an established economic application. [Georgiadis, Kim, and Kwon](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/georgiadis/WarOfAttrition.pdf)

Formal models can have randomized stopping equilibria and asymmetric equilibria where one side leaves immediately. A strategy is an equilibrium when neither improves by changing alone against the other's strategy. That does not make the ongoing losses worthwhile for the pair.

Elapsed waiting is not a universal countdown to the rival's exit. What survival reveals depends on their resources, information, and stopping rule.

## Why the obvious responses fail

**“Show how much we have already wasted.”** Earlier expenditure is unrecoverable. The decision concerns future costs and prospects. In this benchmark, another costly interval still carries a chance that the opponent will withdraw first; persistence need not result from a sunk-cost mistake.

**“Wait until we recover our investment.”** Waiting does not reimburse expenditure. It can consume the entire benefit being contested, and individual contests can cost more than the prize is worth.

**“Promise never to concede.”** An unenforceable statement may change nothing. A credible commitment changes the opponent's problem, but two parties unable to withdraw can create a worse predicament. It is not a generally safe prescription.

**“A deadline will force agreement.”** It might instead force a costly last-minute contest or destroy the prize. Its effect depends on what happens at the deadline.

## Possible ways out

**Withdraw using a forward-looking comparison.** A participant can stop paying, sacrificing the contested benefit. Compare remaining prospects with remaining costs and outside options. In the idealized symmetric version, both face the same prize value and cost of waiting. Against its particular randomized stopping rule, the additional chance of winning by waiting balances the additional expected waiting cost. That model result does not establish that quitting and continuing are equally sensible against an actual opponent. Stopping does not recover earlier losses.

**Replace endurance with a binding allocation rule.** Where a genuine organizer controls a contest prize, adjudication, an agreed draw, or another accepted procedure can replace endurance. Independent shops have no such organizer who can simply allocate customers. This must actually terminate the contest: losing cannot simply reopen it. Administration, legitimacy, and unequal claims remain consequential costs or constraints.

**Make concession less costly.** Parties can negotiate compensation, an alternative opportunity, or a divisible settlement where feasible. Someone must fund transfers or provide the alternative. Such changes alter the payoffs or available outcomes; they do not make waiting efficient under unchanged rules.

**Remove the reward for prolonging the contest.** Institutions can stop awarding benefits according to who remains longest, or set an enforceable fallback that extra endurance cannot improve. This requires control over the allocation mechanism. Merely making waiting cheaper can prolong willingness to wait rather than resolve the dispute.

These intervention families are practical editorial synthesis, not universal results about settlement.

## A worked example

### Improve the exit option instead of hoping to recover old losses

Iris and Noah each review the losses already incurred, then remain open because the other might leave soon. Those past losses are real, but unrecoverable. Their size does not determine which future course is better.

Iris separates the forward-looking alternatives: more net losses while both operate, uncertain survivor earnings, and what she could do after exiting. In this hypothetical diagnosis, ordinary operating work does not build a valuable new business, demand is not expected to rebound independently, and the shop has no other benefit sufficient to justify the losses.

An independent employer offers Iris a suitable job starting soon. She also confirms a feasible way to dispose of equipment and meet closure obligations. This improves her own exit option; it does not require an agreement with Noah about prices, customers, or market division.

For the stipulated continuation, Iris prefers the verified exit package to further waiting and closes. Her operating-loss stream stops after closure costs. Noah no longer pays the cost of competing against her, and under the example's survivor assumption his shop becomes viable. These are hypothetical choices and consequences, not a measured outcome or the prediction of every attrition model.

Iris loses the chance to capture the remaining market. Earlier losses remain lost; employees, customers, and the community may bear transition costs. The outside job and asset disposal may not exist for the other participant—or for Iris in a different case.

The intervention changes the value of exit. Without an improved option, leaving may still be the best forward-looking choice, but the article cannot decide that without credible costs and prospects. Nor can an external mediator simply award a town's customers by drawing a winner.

### When persistence has another explanation

If losses finance a credible product development effort, demand is expected to recover, or the survivor would still be unviable, waiting has a different structure. Examine those alternatives before calling expenditure avoidable attrition.

## Limits and lookalikes

The symmetric randomized equilibrium is not the only possibility. Complete-information attrition games can also have asymmetric equilibria with immediate concession. A modern model by Georgiadis, Kim, and Kwon shows how stochastic operating payoffs and different exit payoffs can eliminate mixed equilibria within its specified class of state-based strategies. That is a qualified result about model structure, not a proof that real contests cannot drag on. [Research paper](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/georgiadis/WarOfAttrition.pdf)

Experiments also caution against reading equilibrium expenditure as observed waste. Hörisch and Kirchkamp found lower persistence bids than their risk-neutral equilibrium benchmark in laboratory attrition contests, with privately known costs and differing bidding procedures. Their setup differs from the equal-cost, complete-information example here. [Author-hosted study](https://www.kirchkamp.de/pdf/ad12.pdf)

Budgets, changing prize values, information revealed by survival, unequal costs, and multiple participants require different calculations. The label also fits poorly when waiting creates substantial productive value or nobody can concede.

## Related patterns

[[chicken|Chicken]] likewise rewards making the other side yield. Attrition makes the ongoing cost and timing of concession central, rather than requiring a single catastrophic collision.

[[bargaining-problems|Bargaining Problems]] concerns selecting terms when agreement offers gains. Costly delay can turn bargaining into attrition, but not every negotiation rewards outlasting an opponent.

[[zero-sum-conflict|Zero-Sum Conflict]] isolates offsetting payoffs. An attrition contest dissipates resources while allocating its prize, so ending the waste can create shared gains even when only one side wins.
