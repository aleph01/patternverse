## A recognizable situation

Mira wants a secondhand bicycle. Dev wants to sell one. They agree which bicycle is offered and know its condition, but Mira wants to pay less and Dev wants to receive more.

They exchange opening prices, then repeat them. Each has another acceptable option: Mira can buy a different bicycle, and Dev can keep his or sell elsewhere. A possible gain from trading does not decide how they should share it.

In this hypothetical sale, quality is known so the example can focus on agreeing terms rather than hidden defects.

## The underlying pattern

**A bargaining problem arises when participants can benefit from agreement but disagree over which agreement to choose.** It combines a shared interest in creating or preserving gains with a conflict over distributing them.

Two objects matter: the **feasible agreements**, meaning the outcomes actually available, and the **disagreement outcome**, meaning what happens without agreement. A demand is not necessarily a genuine walk-away threshold.

Nash's 1950 foundational treatment represents agreements as utility pairs and compares them with disagreement. Utility is a numerical representation of preferences, not necessarily money. His solution selects the agreement maximizing the product of the participants' gains over disagreement, under specified assumptions and axioms. It does not model an offer-by-offer negotiation. [Original paper](https://www.haverford.edu/sites/default/files/Nash1950.pdf)

The central question is: **Can we agree on who receives the gain from agreeing?**

## What keeps it going

Mira compares a proposed deal with another available bicycle. Dev compares it with keeping the bicycle or another sale. Their relevant limits depend on what agreement would leave each with after costs, rather than on their opening demands alone.

A **zone of possible agreement** exists when some terms make both at least as well off as walking away. Within that zone, a higher price favors Dev and a lower price favors Mira. The existence of shared gains does not uniquely divide them.

A bargaining analysis must specify more than preferences. Who offers first? Can an offer be withdrawn? Does silence end the opportunity? Are alternatives known or private? Can accepted terms be enforced? In this one-sale example, both can reject and take a known alternative, earlier discussion is nonbinding, and future relationship rewards are excluded.

An agreement can be efficient in avoiding wasted opportunity while still favoring one side. A particular solution concept supplies additional assumptions about how to select terms; it does not make all reasonable people accept the same division.

## Why the obvious responses fail

**“There is enough benefit, so agreement will happen.”** A mutually beneficial deal can remain unselected. Each participant may wait for a larger share; a shared gain does not remove competing claims.

**“Split the opening offers.”** Their midpoint reflects chosen demands, which may be strategic. It need not divide gains equally, respect alternatives, or satisfy either party.

**“Set a tougher deadline.”** Deadlines can close discussions or destroy the opportunity. Roth, Murnighan, and Schoumaker found agreements concentrated near deadlines, alongside substantial disagreement, in four laboratory bargaining experiments. Much of their timing analysis reused experiments designed for other questions. This documents a phenomenon, not a general causal guarantee that tighter deadlines improve outcomes. [Original study](https://web.stanford.edu/~alroth/papers/1988_AER_Deadline_Effect.pdf)

## Possible ways out

**Establish the decision-relevant facts.** Participants can assess collection costs, alternative opportunities, included equipment, and who has authority to agree. Evidence or a trusted intermediary may narrow uncertainty. Revealing a walk-away limit—Mira’s highest acceptable payment or Dev’s lowest acceptable receipt—can weaken that person’s negotiating position. Economists call this a reservation value; verification does not require disclosing those limits to each other.

**Agree on a selection rule.** Parties can use an accepted benchmark, mediator's proposal, allocation formula, or arbitration procedure. This requires consent or legitimate authority. A rule can settle a distribution without establishing that it is just. The example below selects one acceptable price without declaring a uniquely fair division.

**Trade across different priorities.** Price, timing, specifications, and risk allocation can create better packages when participants value them differently. Test the whole package against both alternatives. A concession on payment timing may create financing costs elsewhere. Adding issues changes the feasible agreements; it does not remove every distributive conflict.

**Change alternatives or the process.** A genuine alternative seller or buyer, affordable financing, flexible collection, or a different offer procedure can change leverage or reduce the cost of waiting. These changes may benefit one party while worsening the other's position.

Rubinstein's 1982 model makes procedure explicit: two informed players can alternate offers over a fixed divisible pie without a final deadline. In its constant-discount version, each values later payoffs less by a known fixed factor. The subgame-perfect equilibrium—strategies optimal after every possible negotiation history—depends on patience and who moves first. That conditional result is not a universal bargaining rule. [Original paper](https://arielrubinstein.org/papers/11.pdf)

## A worked example

### Find an acceptable price without calling it uniquely fair

Mira and Dev initially defend their opening prices. Mira then compares the bicycle with the alternative she could actually buy; Dev compares selling with keeping it or accepting another real offer. A strategically chosen opening demand is different from either person's true acceptance limit.

For this hypothetical example, their limits are known and leave a range of mutually acceptable prices. They check condition, included accessories, and collection costs so they are comparing the same package. That reveals room for agreement, not an obligation to split the difference between opening demands.

They accept a proposed price within the range and arrange an enforceable exchange of bicycle and payment. In the stipulated outcome, both prefer the completed sale to their alternatives. A higher acceptable price would have favored Dev; a lower one would have favored Mira.

The agreement selects a division within the original possibilities. Nash's solution supplies one formal selection principle under specified axioms; it does not generally mean equal money, and does not describe how these two people must negotiate. [Original paper](https://www.haverford.edu/sites/default/files/Nash1950.pdf)

Neither the existence of overlap nor the accepted price establishes fairness. Revealing a private walk-away limit could weaken bargaining power; known limits are an assumption here, not a reliable truth-extraction technique. In an actual sale, verifying condition and completing a protected exchange are additional problems.

### When the acceptance ranges do not overlap

Suppose Mira's best alternative improves until she will pay less than Dev would accept. The original package has no mutually acceptable price. A midpoint cannot create one. Changing accessories, collection arrangements, costs, or alternatives may change the feasible package; otherwise they should not trade. Failed agreement need not mean poor negotiating skill.

## Limits and lookalikes

The Nash bargaining solution assumes a fixed disagreement point, appropriately scaled expected utilities, and a compact, convex feasible set: bounded possibilities including their limits and mixtures. Its axioms require efficiency, equal treatment in symmetric cases, and invariance to utility units. Another, **independence of irrelevant alternatives**, says removing other options leaves the selected agreement unchanged if it remains available. These are substantive modeling choices, not moral facts.

An alternative available only by leaving is not always equivalent to the payoff received while negotiations continue. Delays, uncertain alternatives, private information, enforcement failures, and unequal decision rights require a different model. The known acceptance limits above do not establish a general method for extracting truthful private values.

## Related patterns

[[zero-sum-conflict|Zero-Sum Conflict]] concerns necessarily offsetting payoffs. Here price transfers gains within a sale, but trade versus no trade changes the combined benefit.

[[battle-of-the-sexes|Battle of the Sexes]] isolates competing preferences over coordinated outcomes. Bargaining additionally examines alternatives, feasible divisions, and procedures for selecting terms.

[[chicken|Chicken]] concerns incentives to make the other side yield while avoiding mutual disaster. Some bargaining tactics create that structure, but it is not inherent in every negotiation.

## Sources and further reading

- [John F. Nash Jr., _The Bargaining Problem_](https://www.haverford.edu/sites/default/files/Nash1950.pdf) (1950). Foundational axiomatic treatment, disagreement baseline, and product-of-gains solution.
- [Ariel Rubinstein, _Perfect Equilibrium in a Bargaining Model_](https://arielrubinstein.org/papers/11.pdf) (1982). Strategic analysis showing why timing, preferences, and the offer procedure matter.
- [Alvin E. Roth, J. Keith Murnighan, and Françoise Schoumaker, _The Deadline Effect in Bargaining: Some Experimental Evidence_](https://web.stanford.edu/~alroth/papers/1988_AER_Deadline_Effect.pdf) (1988). Laboratory agreement timing and disagreement evidence, with clear limits on interpretation.
