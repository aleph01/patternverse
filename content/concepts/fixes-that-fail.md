## A recognizable situation

Rae's support team has a backlog. Staff stay late to clear it. The extra hours initially help: more work is completed and the visible queue falls.

The late shifts become routine. Staff have less recovery time, fatigue grows, and more mistakes escape ordinary checks. Some mistakes return later as correction work. The backlog rises again, prompting another request to stay late.

This hypothetical case assumes the delayed error-and-rework burden outweighs the initial relief. A short, recoverable overtime period need not have that result. The defining question is whether the remedy helps create the renewed problem.

## The underlying pattern

**Fixes That Fail is a feedback structure in which a remedy reduces a problem now but creates a delayed consequence that aggravates that same problem later.** Renewed pressure can prompt more of the remedy.

The name comes from the system dynamics tradition of **archetypes**: recurring patterns of feedback. Daniel H. Kim's September 1992 treatment in _The Systems Thinker_ refers back to a 1990 account. These are diagnostic structures, not universal laws predicting that every quick intervention must fail. [Kim's original article](https://thesystemsthinker.com/wp-content/uploads/pdfs/030703E.pdf)

The memorable formulation is: **the relief helps cause the relapse.** Merely leaving a problem's original cause untouched is insufficient to establish this particular structure. The fix must contribute a harmful return path. [Archetype introduction](https://thesystemsthinker.com/introducing-the-systems-archetypes-fixes-that-fail/)

## What keeps it going

A larger backlog increases pressure to work additional hours. Initially those hours raise completions and reduce the queue. That immediate **balancing loop** opposes the original increase.

A delayed route can act in the other direction. Repeated extended work with inadequate recovery contributes to fatigue; fatigue can increase errors or reduce effective performance. Errors that are discovered later return as rework, adding to the backlog and encouraging further overtime. This **reinforcing loop** amplifies the pressure. “Reinforcing” describes direction, not benefit.

Known unfinished work and undiscovered errors are different accumulations. New work and discovered corrections add to the active backlog; genuine completions remove it. Errors enter a latent pool before discovery transfers them into correction work. Counting a handoff as complete does not establish that no later obligation remains.

Fatigue can also build and subside over time. Resting can improve present working conditions without removing errors already released. Recovery and defect discovery have different delays, so the visible queue may worsen for a period after the harmful practice stops.

These are causal hypotheses about Rae's team. Long-hours research gives reason to examine fatigue and performance, but does not establish that every extra hour creates errors or that this team's rework exceeds its early gain. [NIOSH review and limitations](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/11308)

## Why the obvious responses fail

**Repeat the action that worked last time.** Early relief is real but incomplete evidence. Another extended shift can add errors before earlier ones have returned.

**Judge output only at handoff.** A completed ticket can reappear as correction work. Count useful resolution over an appropriate follow-up period, not only the first departure from the queue.

**Stop overtime and expect the queue to vanish.** Recovery time and safer working conditions do not erase inherited defects or create spare capacity above continuing arrivals.

**Blame every recurrence on fatigue.** New demand, harder cases, missing skills, or equipment faults can also create errors and delay. Evidence must support the particular return path.

## Possible ways out

**Follow relief into its later consequences.** Rae can examine work schedules, recovery opportunities, job difficulty, errors, repeat contacts, and correction effort. Compare like work where possible. A before-and-after comparison can mislead if the hardest cases were assigned to late shifts.

**Break the fatigue-and-error path.** A manager can stop treating extended hours as the permanent staffing plan, protect recovery, improve handovers, and review error-sensitive work. These changes require coverage and attention to how work actually occurs. A nominal hours limit with the same impossible workload can shift pressure into shortcuts elsewhere.

**Fund the transition.** Temporary qualified help, fewer incoming commitments, later delivery dates, or reduced scope can make room for recovery and inherited corrections. Someone bears extra cost, waiting, or less service. If ordinary capacity merely matches new arrivals, a one-time backlog still needs a surplus of completions to clear.

**Use emergency relief with explicit boundaries.** A bounded extra effort can be justified when immediate harm from delay is greater than the foreseeable later burden. Specify duration, recovery, review, and follow-up resources. The lesson is to account for consequences, not to assume all short-term relief is harmful.

Kim recommends mapping intended effects, unintended consequences, and the connections sustaining repeated fixes. These intervention families apply that approach; they are practical proposals to test, not guaranteed remedies. [Mapping the structure](https://thesystemsthinker.com/using-fixes-that-fail-to-get-off-the-problem-solving-treadmill/)

## A worked example

### Clear work without recreating the queue through errors

Rae first extends shifts and sees the queue fall. She repeats the arrangement rather than revising commitments or staffing. Later corrections consume the earlier saving and more. That net comparison is stipulated for this example, not calculated from a universal overtime effect.

She checks case difficulty, new demand, error types, schedules, and recovery time. In the hypothetical diagnosis, repeated insufficient recovery contributes to preventable errors and delayed returns; an unrelated surge alone does not explain the renewed backlog.

Management stops routine extended shifts, protects recovery and handover, adds qualified temporary coverage, and renegotiates some delivery dates. The plan includes correction work from earlier periods rather than reporting only newly accepted requests.

In the stipulated recovery, fewer avoidable errors enter use and the inherited return stream subsides after discovery and correction. Temporary usable capacity permits completions to exceed new work long enough to clear the old queue. Rest alone would not guarantee that throughput relationship.

Some users wait longer initially, temporary help costs money, and onboarding can temporarily consume experienced staff time. If qualified help is unavailable, the service must reduce accepted scope, change promises, or accept a longer recovery. Expecting the same people to meet every original deadline would preserve the pressure that prompted the fix.

A genuine emergency can still warrant a limited extra effort. The distinction is between accepting and funding its consequences and repeatedly calling it a solution while excluding the later burden.

## Limits and lookalikes

The example holds ordinary demand and job mix steady to isolate the proposed feedback. Real productivity, recovery, discovery, and harm vary; discoveries are usually spread over time. A causal-loop drawing cannot determine how large a rebound will be; that requires evidence and, where useful, a quantified model.

Oliva and Sterman's 2010 service-system model explicitly represents undiscovered errors returning as rework. It draws on earlier field research, including a UK bank study, but its simulated feedback and policy effects are not randomized intervention results. It supports examining this mechanism, not importing the hypothetical return-work assumptions into another workplace. [Model and research context](https://mitsloan.mit.edu/shared/ods/documents?PublicationDocumentID=4349)

**Shifting the Burden** is a neighboring archetype: symptomatic relief displaces or weakens a more fundamental response, creating dependence. Both can coexist, but Fixes That Fail requires a damaging consequence of the fix; weakened problem-solving capability is an additional mechanism, not a prerequisite.
