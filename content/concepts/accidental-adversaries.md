## A recognizable situation

Mira manages clients for a small website service; Owen organizes delivery. Their partnership makes sense: Mira brings clear, funded work, while Owen's reliable deliveries help clients return.

Under pressure to retain a customer, Mira promises a late change without checking the schedule. Owen interrupts work to accommodate it. After several disruptions, he protects his team by refusing to offer dates until requirements are final. Mira now has fewer confirmed options to offer anxious clients. To keep them, she makes more promises before consulting him.

Each sees the other's behaviour as the obstacle. In this hypothetical situation, their efforts to protect the service are damaging the collaboration that makes it possible.

## The underlying pattern

**Accidental Adversaries describes partners whose actions to improve or protect their own results unintentionally obstruct each other's success, provoking further protective actions and undermining their alliance.** Mutual benefit remains possible, but a defensive feedback loop begins to dominate it.

Jennifer Kemeny described the archetype in a February 1994 article, identifying an early supplier–retailer case involving Procter & Gamble and Walmart. The article also announced its forthcoming treatment in _The Fifth Discipline Fieldbook_. [Original article](https://thesystemsthinker.com/wp-content/uploads/pdfs/050102p.pdf)

The memorable formulation is: **my safeguard makes your job harder; your safeguard makes mine harder.**

## What keeps it going

The original partnership contains a beneficial feedback: useful client commitments support delivery, and successful delivery supports future client work. Both partners have reasons to sustain it.

Each also faces a local performance gap. Mira wants to avoid losing clients; Owen wants to avoid missing dates. Their corrective actions can be **balancing responses**, intended to reduce those respective gaps.

The trouble lies in the effects crossing between them. Mira's unverified promises increase Owen's disruption. Owen's withheld dates increase Mira's difficulty negotiating credible commitments. That pressure encourages more unverified promises, completing a **reinforcing loop**: the consequences of a response generate pressure for further responses.

Ramsey and Wells describe how safeguards and blame can obscure each partner's contribution to the other's difficulties. Interpreting a missed date as evidence of bad character can discourage inquiry into the instructions, constraints, or commitments that preceded it. [Practitioner explanation](https://thesystemsthinker.com/how-am-i-supposed-to-work-with-her-the-accidental-adversaries-storyline/)

Effects can arrive late. A promise helps a client conversation today; disruption emerges during implementation. Unfinished work accumulates as a backlog, while both partners may attribute today's problems to the other's latest action. The loop is a causal hypothesis to investigate, not proof of anyone's motives.

## Why the obvious responses fail

**Demand greater commitment to the partnership.** Both may already care about the service. A shared aspiration does not resolve conflicting operating rules.

**Strengthen each safeguard.** More rigid sign-off can protect delivery while making client negotiations harder. More generous promises can retain customers while making delivery less predictable.

**Find who started it.** Accountability matters, but identifying an initiating incident does not remove the continuing return path.

**Remove all protection in a gesture of trust.** Owen still needs feasible commitments; Mira still needs usable dates. Eliminating safeguards without replacing their function can expose real risks.

## Possible ways out

**Map both local successes and cross-effects.** Examine particular jobs: what was promised, what information was available, what changed, and whose workload increased. Distinguish the effect of an action from assumptions about its intent. Include the customer rather than optimizing only the two internal roles.

**Redesign the interface.** Give Mira provisional capacity information and a clear route to confirm a slot before promising it. Give Owen an agreed cutoff, complete change requests, and authority to identify what must move when urgent work arrives. Both need a usable alternative to their existing safeguard.

**Make trade-offs explicit at commitment time.** A shared queue can show who may accept a change, its estimated effort, and which earlier commitment it displaces. A manager or customer may have to choose later delivery, less scope, or additional resources. Better cooperation cannot make incompatible promises feasible.

**Test the joint outcome.** Track completed usable work, delivery reliability, accepted changes, restart effort, and customer losses. Lower interruption alone could conceal rejected useful work. These are practical intervention proposals, not results established by drawing the loop.

## A worked example

### Make a promise depend on a shared capacity check

Mira’s emphatic delivery assurances do not supply Owen with more time. Owen’s refusal to offer early dates protects his immediate schedule but leaves Mira less able to negotiate credible commitments. Each response makes the partner’s next difficulty more likely.

They examine interrupted jobs together. In this hypothetical diagnosis, repeated unverified promises create avoidable restarts, while withheld provisional dates encourage further promises made without consultation. They distinguish this overhead from legitimate change work that still needs doing.

They introduce a jointly visible delivery queue. Owen provides provisional options with clear assumptions; Mira checks the slot before confirming it to the client. Changes receive scheduled review, and an urgent override must identify which existing commitment it displaces. Neither partner simply transfers the uncertainty to the other.

In the stipulated successful outcome, fewer avoidable interruptions leave more usable time for the same underlying work. Mira can give clients credible choices earlier, and Owen can plan against commitments he has helped assess. The improvement depends on customers accepting some limits and both partners keeping the queue current.

The arrangement creates no new productive hours by itself. If demand exceeds capacity even without interruption losses, they must add capacity, reduce scope, or delay work. It also cannot absorb every emergency without cost.

The test is whether the cross-effects change: fewer unsupported promises, earlier workable dates, fewer restarts, and more reliable delivery. A joint meeting that ends with each person tightening their own safeguard can preserve the adversarial loop while improving its language.

## Limits and lookalikes

The name does not establish that harm is accidental. Deliberate deception, coercion, unequal bargaining power, or genuinely incompatible interests require attention to responsibility, protection, and possibly ending the relationship. A useful causal map does not excuse misconduct.

Kemeny reports that promotions encouraged retailer stockpiling, which then disrupted manufacturers' results and encouraged further promotions. It is a documented practitioner account of a mutually obstructive pattern, not a controlled test proving that every alliance fails or that a particular pricing policy always repairs it. [Historical account](https://thesystemsthinker.com/accidental-adversaries-when-friends-become-foes/)

Novak and Levine's software-acquisition report similarly examines partner hostility while warning that causal diagrams do not establish quantitative behaviour or reveal motives. The map helps formulate questions; evidence is needed for the links and the size of their effects. [Technical report, sections 3.3, 3.11, and 5.5](https://www.sei.cmu.edu/documents/835/2010_005_001_15242.pdf)
