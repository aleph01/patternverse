## A recognizable situation

A funder compares two research teams working on equally promising projects. Team A already has more grant support, so it can employ more researchers and complete more studies. Team B works with less support and produces fewer results.

The next award rewards the larger record without asking what resources produced it. A receives more of the limited funding pool; B receives less. The following review seems to confirm the original decision.

This hypothetical example assumes comparable potential under comparable conditions. Earlier opportunity is becoming evidence for further opportunity; it does not establish that every difference between research teams comes from funding.

## The underlying pattern

**Success to the Successful occurs when success earns resources or opportunities that help generate further success, while competing alternatives lose access to those same means.** The key question is: **Does winning this round improve the conditions for winning the next?**

This is a system dynamics **archetype**, a recurring feedback structure. Peter Senge included it in _The Fifth Discipline_ (1990); Donella Meadows later described the trap as rewards that equip winners to win again. These accounts identify a structure, not a universal law that every lead becomes a monopoly. [Senge, Appendix 2](https://www.bookdio.org/_files/ugd/ef8af1_5169a4238fd3412dba674fd8dbd81be6.pdf), [Meadows, pp. 127–130](https://www.sustainable.soltechdesigns.com/meadows.pdf)

The broader idea is **cumulative advantage**: an existing advantage helps produce additional advantages. Robert K. Merton's 1968 **Matthew effect** concerned disproportionate recognition of established scientists, drawing partly on Harriet Zuckerman's interviews. It is a related account of scientific credit and visibility, not the origin of every resource-allocation model. [Merton's paper](https://garfield.library.upenn.edu/merton/matthew1.pdf)

## What keeps it going

Each team sits inside a **reinforcing feedback loop**: funding supports research capability, capability supports results, and results influence future funding. The loops are coupled because money allocated to A from the fixed pool is unavailable to B. Reduced support can likewise produce fewer results and become the reason for withholding the next award.

The funder need not dislike B. Choosing the apparently proven team can seem prudent. Yet a project needs time to recruit people, prepare equipment, and produce credible work. Withdrawing support before that delay has passed can prevent an alternative from demonstrating its value. [Kim's explanation](https://thesystemsthinker.com/a-tale-of-two-loops-the-behavior-of-success-to-the-successful/)

Funding per period is an allocation or flow, not a stock of skill. Equipment, retained expertise, relationships, and reputation can accumulate and persist after an award ends. Training and research delays separate an award from its eventual results; turnover and obsolescence can erode capability. An equal grant today would not instantly equalize that history.

The proposed causal chain needs evidence. Repeated winners alone do not show that the allocation rule amplifies advantage: differences in skill, project difficulty, demand, and quality can also matter.

## Why the obvious responses fail

**Tell B to be more productive.** Effort alone cannot supply missing staff, essential equipment, or the time a study requires. Raw totals can compare different opportunities.

**Give B one encouraging award.** A brief boost may end before useful results appear. If the next review returns to unadjusted totals, the same selection mechanism can dominate again.

**Publish the counts more clearly.** Accurate reporting can reproduce the problem faithfully. The missing question is what support, time, difficulty, and quality sit behind the counts.

**Assume equal funding is always right.** Projects can have different costs, risks, and public value. Comparable support is one possible evaluation design, not a universal allocation rule.

## Possible ways out

**Evaluate results alongside the conditions producing them.** Funders can consider support received, project maturity, difficulty, quality, and plausible future value. Counting outputs per unit of funding is not enough when projects have fixed costs, delayed payoffs, or incomparable purposes.

**Protect a meaningful opportunity to demonstrate value.** A funder can reserve developmental support or a bounded trial for promising alternatives. It must last long enough to build the capability and generate evidence being requested. Clear review conditions distinguish a real opportunity from indefinite exemption from scrutiny.

**Separate recognition from automatic resource transfer.** Excellent work can be acknowledged without making raw prior totals the sole rule for the next grant. Funding may instead depend on an explicit portfolio purpose, needs, quality, or additional value. That changes the allocation relationship, not the past record.

**Expand or diversify access where worthwhile.** New facilities or other funding sources can reduce direct competition for the original pool. They require resources and may create new gatekeepers. Under a fixed budget, helping B generally means less money available elsewhere; that loss should be explicit.

## A worked example

### Give a promising alternative a usable trial

The funder first asks B for a stronger publication record without changing its support. B cannot hire the missing researcher or run the necessary study. The request repeats the evidence requirement while withholding a means of meeting it.

A review considers the teams' resources, project stage, work quality, and proposed value. For this hypothetical diagnosis, B has a credible project whose low output mainly reflects inadequate opportunity, rather than poor work. Comparable potential is assumed for the illustration, not established by a low output count.

The funder replaces automatic winner bonuses with a protected, adequately funded trial for B and a review timed to the research cycle. The award can hire the needed person and use the relevant equipment. The total funding pool stays fixed, so A receives less than it would under the old allocation rule.

In the stipulated continuation, B builds usable capability and produces assessable work. The next review uses that evidence alongside quality and costs. It does not assume that an award has already produced results or that B must become the better team.

The intervention changes how future opportunity is allocated. It sacrifices some immediate concentration on the established option to learn about an alternative. If B cannot recruit, the equipment is unavailable, or the trial ends before results can emerge, equal-looking award amounts may provide no meaningful test.

Past advantage also persists. Reputation and retained expertise do not reset with the grant decision. Future allocations can remain unequal for defensible reasons; the purpose is to stop inherited opportunity being mistaken for complete evidence of enduring merit.

## Limits and lookalikes

Repeated success alone does not demonstrate this feedback. Stable differences in skill, demand, or circumstances can also explain repeated winners. Nor must success be undeserved for its rewards to amplify advantage.

Allocation rules matter: with equal productivity and no other differences, assigning future support strictly in proportion to current output can preserve an existing unequal split rather than widen it. Concentration depends on how the allocator responds, not on the existence of a gap alone.

There is causal evidence in particular settings. Van de Rijt and colleagues randomly provided initial funding, ratings, awards, or signatures in four online systems. Their first crowdfunding experiment sampled initially unfunded US projects with low funding goals: 70% of treated projects attracted subsequent third-party funding, compared with 39% of controls. This measures additional funding, not completed projects. Larger initial boosts did not produce proportional further gains in their follow-up experiments. The findings support feedback within those samples and observation periods, not inevitable runaway dominance or the hypothetical research allocation rule. [Original experiments](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4024896/)

**Preferential attachment** is a particular rule in which new connections favor already connected nodes. **Positive network effects** mean a service becomes more valuable as others use it. Either can contribute to cumulative advantage, but neither is required when a funder reallocates grants. [Attachment model](https://barabasi.com/media/Emergence-Barabasi-1999_gN74Tws.pdf), [network-effects explanation](https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/shapiro/systems.pdf)
