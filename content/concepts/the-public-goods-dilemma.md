## A recognizable situation

A listener-supported radio station asks for help paying for local reporting. Ari enjoys the programmes but knows she can keep listening if somebody else contributes. Bea, Cam, and Dev face the same choice.

The next appeal explains how valuable the reporting is. Everyone may agree, while each still has private uses for the money. The audience can value a stronger service collectively and leave it short of funding individually.

This hypothetical station supplies the example. It does not reproduce a named broadcaster's finances. The question is how to finance an ongoing shared benefit available to noncontributors.

## The underlying pattern

**A public-goods dilemma arises when people can benefit without contributing, so individually attractive choices can leave a collectively worthwhile good underprovided.**

A pure **public good** is nonrival: one person's use does not reduce what others can use. It is also nonexcludable: preventing nonpayers from benefiting is difficult or impractical. A freely received radio broadcast approximates these properties within its coverage area; producing the reporting still uses scarce staff time and money. Technical access costs and limited coverage remain outside this simplified example.

Paul Samuelson's 1954 _The Pure Theory of Public Expenditure_ gave a foundational formal account of collective consumption, explicitly acknowledging earlier economists. Its central distinction concerns benefits enjoyed without subtracting from others' consumption. [Original paper](https://publics22.classes.ryansafner.com/readings/Samuelson-1954.pdf)

The dilemma concerns incentives, not an inevitable absence of generosity. Nor does noncontribution establish bad faith: beneficiaries can have different needs, resources, or valuations. The relevant question is whether the funding arrangement makes private incentives diverge from shared gains.

## What keeps it going

Ari can hear reporting funded by other listeners. Her own donation has a direct private cost, while the additional reporting benefits the wider audience. Bea, Cam, and Dev face the same tension.

For the standard one-period benchmark, assume each listener values the additional reporting attributable to their own contribution less than it costs them, whatever others give. The combined benefits nevertheless exceed that cost. They choose without observing current contributions, know the structure, and value only their own net benefit; future favors, recognition, and sanctions are excluded.

Under these assumptions, withholding is each listener's best choice regardless of the others' choices. All withholding is the **Nash equilibrium**: nobody improves by contributing alone. A shared contribution can still leave everyone better off. This conditional incentive result does not predict that actual audiences are never generous. [Standard model and assumptions](https://web.mit.edu/14.193/www/Fehr-MIT-Lecture7-8CooperationPublicGoods.pdf)

The example concerns incremental provision: funding supports additional reporting within an operating service. It does not assume one decisive target below which the station vanishes. In a threshold project, someone can instead find contributing worthwhile because that contribution makes provision happen. Shared access alone does not determine which game applies.

## Why the obvious responses fail

**Repeat the shared-benefit argument.** Understanding that everybody gains does not remove the private cost of contributing.

**Ask the most enthusiastic listener to cover everything.** A sufficiently interested donor might do so, but that changes who bears the cost and can create dependence on one donor's priorities. It is not guaranteed by the original model.

**Publish contribution totals.** Visibility may support recognition and reciprocity. Under the unchanged one-shot benchmark, however, knowing others contributed does not remove the private cost of giving more than one personally recovers.

**Assume one successful fundraiser solves maintenance.** An initial grant can pay for one period of reporting while leaving the next funding decision unchanged. Continuing costs need a continuing arrangement.

## Possible ways out

These intervention families are editorial synthesis. Identify the good, beneficiaries, costs, and decision rights before choosing among them.

**Create legitimate collective funding.** An association can allocate approved budgets or collect agreed dues; public authorities can finance public goods through their own authorized processes. This requires authority, collection, accountability, and a defensible distribution of costs. It replaces some voluntary contribution choices rather than proving that free riding disappears spontaneously.

**Support voluntary cooperation.** Identifiable contributors, communication, acknowledgment, and useful feedback may help people who value reciprocity or the shared project. Repeated relationships can add future consequences, but they are a different game from the one-shot model. Anonymous, replaceable participants and disputed contribution measures weaken this route.

**Change the private return.** Matching funds, contributor services, or valued recognition can make giving more attractive. Specify who funds them and what participants actually value. A subsidy may reduce the private disadvantage without removing it, or leave someone indifferent rather than willing to give. Its effect depends on the changed choice, not the label “match.”

**Use conditional commitments where failure risk matters.** Refundable pledges can prevent payment for an unfunded project. For a genuinely all-or-nothing project, a contributor can be pivotal in reaching the target. That changes the payoff structure. It is not the same as solving the incremental provision problem described here.

**Reconsider access or scope.** Charging for an excludable service can finance a public component. A paywall also excludes some beneficiaries and changes the good toward a club good. Alternatively, accept a smaller project and state what will remain unprovided. Ostrom's work emphasizes varied institutional arrangements rather than one universal public-or-private prescription. [Nobel lecture](https://faculty.sites.iastate.edu/tesfatsi/archive/tesfatsi/BeyondMarketsAndStates.NobelLecture.EOstrom2009.pdf)

## A worked example

### Finance a period of reporting while leaving listening open

The station repeats its donation appeal. Ari can still save her money and hear work funded by Bea, Cam, and Dev; they can reason similarly. Describing the benefit again does not remove that private choice.

The station proposes a listener association with a reporting budget and dues that members expressly accept for an agreed funding period. Joining remains voluntary. Adoption must therefore be addressed separately from collecting an obligation after it has been accepted.

In this hypothetical continuation, enough willing members approve affordable shares and collection is reliable. The collected budget pays for the agreed reporting during that period. Programmes remain freely available to members and nonmembers. The intervention changes accepted funding responsibilities rather than excluding listeners.

Those are stipulated conditions, not an equilibrium prediction that every listener will join. If members can withdraw before payment and keep listening, approval alone does not secure the budget. Nonmember free riding also remains; a bounded funding arrangement need not capture every beneficiary to support some useful provision.

Later periods require renewed commitments or a different continuing source. Administration consumes funds, members may disagree about editorial priorities, and a charge affordable to one listener may exclude another from membership. If adoption or collection fails, a smaller reporting commitment, an outside sponsor, or another authorized funding structure may be necessary.

### What an experiment adds

Fischbacher, Gächter, and Fehr's 2001 experiment elicited contribution schedules from 44 university students in a one-shot public-goods setting. Half displayed conditional cooperation: willingness to contribute tended to rise with others' contributions. Thirteen consistently specified zero contributions. These classifications concern a particular monetary experiment, not population-wide personality labels or evidence that the radio arrangement will work. The study shows why zero-contribution equilibrium reasoning alone is an incomplete behavioral forecast. [Original study](https://www.econ.uzh.ch/dam/jcr%3Affffffff-9758-127f-0000-00003449356a/ArePeopleCondCooperative.pdf)

## Limits and lookalikes

**Common-pool resources** are difficult to exclude users from but subtractable: taking fish leaves fewer for others. Their appropriation problem concerns excessive use, while this article concerns insufficient provision. A fishery may also require a public good, such as shared monitoring. Both problems can coexist. [Ostrom's classification](https://faculty.sites.iastate.edu/tesfatsi/archive/tesfatsi/BeyondMarketsAndStates.NobelLecture.EOstrom2009.pdf)

The benchmark simplifies differences in benefits and excludes diminishing returns, congestion, funding thresholds, and concern for others. Actual provision and behavior can differ sharply. More contributors do not automatically mean less cooperation: what each receives from contributing, and what others receive, matter.

## Related patterns

[[the-prisoners-dilemma|The Prisoner's Dilemma]] identifies an incentive structure: defection is attractive regardless of the other’s choice, despite mutually preferable cooperation. This incremental public-goods benchmark extends that conflict across several contributors; public goods with other provision rules need not have the same incentives.

[[the-volunteers-dilemma|The Volunteer's Dilemma]] needs one sufficient contributor who prefers acting to nobody acting. [[threshold-contribution-problems|Threshold Contribution Problems]] requires a sufficient combined commitment. Assignment and conditional pledges address these additional structures, rather than universally resolving free riding.

[[cold-starts-and-critical-mass|Cold Starts and Critical Mass]] concerns participation becoming worthwhile once enough relevant people join. A linear public-goods contributor still loses privately even when everyone else contributes.

[[make-trade-offs-explicit|Make Trade-offs Explicit]] helps examine the access, administrative cost, authority, and fairness consequences of a funding rule.
