## A recognizable situation

Maya wants to crowdfund a short documentary that everyone will be able to watch freely. Finishing it requires a minimum editing and production budget. Several people offer support, but their combined pledges still leave it short.

Ari worries about paying for a film that may never be finished. Dev hopes enough other people will fund it so he can watch without contributing. More enthusiastic messages do not close the funding gap.

This hypothetical campaign separates two questions familiar from crowdfunding: will enough money actually arrive, and who will supply it?

## The underlying pattern

**A threshold contribution problem arises when a shared outcome requires a minimum combined contribution, making each contribution's value depend on whether others supply enough.** The threshold may concern money, participants, hours, or complementary commitments.

Economists study **threshold public goods** and **provision-point mechanisms**: rules specifying when sufficient contributions trigger provision. This is a family of models, rather than one game with a universal solution. Van de Kragt, Orbell, and Dawes's 1983 experiments examined groups needing a specified number of fixed contributions and introduced the **minimal contributing set**: an identified group sufficient to provide the good, with every member necessary. [Original study's abstract](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/abs/minimal-contributing-set-as-a-solution-to-public-goods-problems/2A0FA64FDDB11B6BF4346E49E74E133F)

A worthwhile project can face two distinct obstacles: fear of paying when provision fails, and disagreement or uncertainty about who will supply enough. Removing one does not automatically remove the other.

## What keeps it going

A contribution has different consequences depending on what others commit. If the other backers supply too little, paying alone may waste money without producing a film. If they already supply enough, withholding saves money while preserving access. A contributor is **pivotal** when its action changes whether the project can proceed.

For a simple benchmark, backers choose contribute or withhold without observing current choices. Everyone knows that the agreed set can fund the project, nobody can fund it alone, and each member of that set prefers paying its share to losing the film. Initially an underfunded payment is lost, and no future rewards or sanctions are included.

Both nobody paying and an exactly sufficient contributing set can be **Nash equilibria** under those assumptions: an isolated contribution cannot help in the first case, while withdrawal would destroy provision in the second. Equilibrium does not itself choose the desirable outcome.

Protecting against failure and coordinating sufficient commitments address different barriers. Refundable pledges can reduce exposure to an unsuccessful attempt while leaving the project short of willing or capable contributors.

## Why the obvious responses fail

**Explain the benefit again.** Shared value does not identify a sufficient coalition or protect money committed to a failed attempt.

**Count expressions of interest.** Approving messages are not collectible commitments. Ability to pay, willingness, payment validity, and the date resources become available can differ.

**Offer refunds and declare the problem solved.** Refunds protect unsuccessful contributions. They need not make anybody pivotal when the remaining gap is too large, or resolve which backers should pay.

**Lower the displayed target.** A cheaper scope may be feasible. Advertising a lower target while the same producer still requires the original funding disguises the shortfall.

## Possible ways out

These practical recommendations are editorial synthesis informed by the research below.

**Define a credible provision point.** The organizer needs a verified price, deliverable, deadline, and treatment of fees and excess contributions. A target should correspond to an actual feasible project. Meeting a fundraising number is not proof that production will succeed.

**Make contributions conditional.** An assurance arrangement collects valid pledges and releases payment only when sufficient funds have actually been collected, or refunds payments if the target is missed. Someone trustworthy must administer collection, expiry, and repayment. This changes failure payoffs and requires resources; unrecovered preparation time can remain costly.

**Agree a sufficient contributing set.** Identify capable participants, proposed shares, and explicit acceptance. Communication can coordinate an existing successful equilibrium; the 1983 study reports groups organizing this way under its experimental conditions. That is evidence of possibility, not a guarantee for these backers. The allocation may also leave outsiders benefiting without paying. [Study](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/abs/minimal-contributing-set-as-a-solution-to-public-goods-problems/2A0FA64FDDB11B6BF4346E49E74E133F)

**Secure a real lead contribution or reduce scope.** Committed seed funding reduces the remaining gap. A smaller deliverable can reduce the actual threshold. A sponsor must bear the lead cost; a smaller project sacrifices some benefits. Neither announcement creates resources by itself.

## A worked example

### Protect the pledges and identify enough real backing

Maya's donation appeal stalls. Ari and Bea cannot cover the production bill alone. Cam wants protection against paying for an underfunded attempt. Dev prefers to watch the eventual free film without paying. Interest and collectible support are different.

Maya replaces unconditional donations with a hypothetical all-or-nothing arrangement. She verifies a budget covering completion, fees, and administration, then obtains accepted pledges from Ari, Bea, and Cam. At their agreed shares, the three together are sufficient and removing any one leaves a gap. The freely available film is assumed valuable enough to each of those backers to justify their share when pivotal.

Valid pledges permit collection at the deadline. Maya authorizes production only after sufficient money has actually been collected. If a pledge becomes invalid, collection does not begin under this arrangement; if collection leaves a shortfall, the agreed failure procedure returns money already collected. Preparation and failed collection still cost resources and need separate coverage.

In the stipulated successful outcome, the pledges remain valid, collection succeeds, and the producer completes the film. Dev watches without contributing. The result provides the shared benefit without equalizing its cost.

Conditional payment changes the consequences of an unsuccessful attempt. Identifying a sufficient group separately coordinates who pays. Neither step guarantees the other. In the simplified refund benchmark, an unsuccessful pledge costs nothing; when no single backer can meet the target, pledging alone and withholding are equally unrewarding if everyone else withholds. Nobody pledging can therefore remain a weak Nash equilibrium. Real preparation costs are excluded from that comparison, not magically reimbursed.

If all willing backers together cannot afford the real budget, Maya must reduce scope, secure other funding, or stop. Meeting a funding target still leaves production risk.

### A documented crowdfunding rule

Kickstarter documents an all-or-nothing model: if a campaign misses its goal, pledged funds are not collected. It advises creators to budget for delivering the promise and fulfilling rewards. This documents a real funding procedure; it does not make all Kickstarter rewards public goods, guarantee collection of every pledge, or guarantee delivery after funding. Maya's additional full-collection and shortfall-return conditions are stipulated for this example, not attributed to Kickstarter. [Creator handbook](https://www.kickstarter.com/help/handbook/funding)

### What field evidence adds

List and Lucking-Reiley's 2002 field experiment randomly assigned 3,000 prospective donors to six university fundraising solicitations varying seed funding and refunds. More seed funding increased giving substantially; refunds increased amounts raised more modestly. Two of the three refund campaigns still missed their targets and returned donations. This documented case shows both potential and limits; it does not estimate an effect for Maya's campaign or establish net fundraising profitability. [Published study](https://econweb.ucsd.edu/~jandreon/PhilanthropyAndFundraising/Volume%202/8%20List%20Lucking-Riley%202002.pdf)

## Limits and lookalikes

A threshold must be established, not inferred from a stalled campaign. Some projects provide useful partial outputs; some need different skills rather than interchangeable contributions. Uncertain costs, private valuations, and failed collections require richer models.

Bagnoli and Lipman's foundational 1989 refund analysis assumes complete information and flexible contribution amounts. Its strong implementation result uses **undominated perfect equilibrium**, a stricter selection criterion than ordinary Nash equilibrium. It does not show that every Nash equilibrium funds every project, or that the backers' binary-choice game inherits the theorem. [Original paper](https://sites.bu.edu/blipman/files/2021/10/public_goods.pdf)

Successful provision also need not distribute burdens fairly. Equal benefits are an assumption here; a backer valuing the film below its charge might reasonably reject the proposed share.
