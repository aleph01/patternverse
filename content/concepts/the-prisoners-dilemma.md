## A recognizable situation

Two suspects, Noor and Leo, are questioned separately. Each can stay silent or implicate the other. The offers have been arranged so that each receives a better personal outcome by implicating the other, whatever the other chooses.

If both stay silent, both do better than if both implicate. Yet Noor cannot improve her own outcome by remaining silent while holding Leo's choice fixed. Leo faces the same incentive. Before separation, each might have hoped that a promise of mutual silence would settle matters.

This is the classic stylized prisoner story, not a description of legal procedure or advice about an actual investigation. Its force lies in the stated ranking of outcomes, without needing invented sentence lengths.

## The underlying pattern

**In a prisoner's dilemma, each player benefits from defecting regardless of the other's action, but both prefer mutual cooperation to mutual defection.**

The game emerged from Merrill Flood and Melvin Dresher's work at RAND in 1950; Albert Tucker supplied the familiar prisoner story. Flood's 1952 report documents the early experiments. [Historical account](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/prisoner-dilemma/), [RAND report](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_memoranda/RM789-1.html)

For the simplest symmetric version, the preference ordering is **temptation > mutual cooperation > mutual defection > being the sole cooperator**. “Cooperate” and “defect” name actions within the model; they do not establish moral judgments. [Formal definition](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/prisoner-dilemma/)

In the prisoner benchmark, Noor and Leo choose **stay silent** or **implicate** without observing the other’s current choice. Both know the offers and care only about their own stipulated outcome. There is one decision, no binding agreement, and no future reward or punishment. “Cooperation” here means silence relative to the other suspect; it is not a moral endorsement of that action.

## What keeps it going

If Leo stays silent, Noor gets the best personal outcome by implicating him. If Leo implicates her, she still does better by implicating than by being the only silent suspect. Leo has the same ranking. Implicating is **strictly dominant**: preferred against either choice by the other person.

Both implicating is the unique **Nash equilibrium**: neither can improve by changing alone. Both silence would be better for each, but either would then have an individual reason to depart from it. An equilibrium is resistant to an individual change, not necessarily desirable for the participants.

Certainty about Leo's silence would not remove Noor's temptation. This is why the problem differs from cooperation blocked only by fear that the other will fail to join.

The prediction depends on the stated preferences, knowledge, timing, and absence of other consequences. Concern for another person, conscience, reputation, or a credible future response can change the game. The story does not demonstrate how actual suspects or people generally behave.

## Why the obvious responses fail

**Explain the shared benefit again.** Both may already understand it. The unilateral incentive remains.

**Promise without enforcement.** A promise can express intentions, but an unenforceable message alone does not change the stated payoffs or available actions.

**Trust the other to cooperate.** Even certainty that the other stays silent leaves implicating more rewarding in this model. This distinguishes the dilemma from problems driven solely by uncertainty.

**Repeat a known number of times.** With the same payoffs, a commonly known final round, complete information, and commonly known payoff-maximizing rationality, backward induction yields defection in every round. The last round has no future discipline; the same reasoning then applies to the preceding round. [Ben Polak's Yale lecture](https://oyc.yale.edu/economics/econ-159/lecture-21)

## Possible ways out

**Make cooperation part of an ongoing strategy.** Indefinite interaction can connect today's behavior to future treatment. This needs identifiable partners, sufficiently valuable future interaction, and usable information about past actions. It changes the overall game from a one-shot encounter to a repeated relationship; the stage-game incentives can remain unchanged.

In an ongoing relationship, participants might cooperate while cooperation is reciprocated and respond to a later omission. Such strategies can support cooperation when future consequences matter enough relative to the immediate gain from defection. That is a conditional repeated-game result, not an assurance that cooperation will emerge. Always skipping can remain an equilibrium too. Permanent retaliation is especially brittle when omissions are misread, so monitoring and a way to repair mistakes matter. [Repeated-game framework](https://oyc.yale.edu/economics/econ-159/lecture-21)

**Change the immediate incentives.** An authorized coordinator can reward verified cooperation or impose a collectible cost for skipping. Monitoring alone is insufficient unless consequences follow. Costs, mistaken judgments, evasion, and unequal burdens must enter the redesigned game.

**Change the arrangement.** In a workplace application, separate responsibilities or a funded shared service can remove a recurring temptation to leave work to others, while creating payment and supervision problems. These are redesigns of everyday dilemmas, not remedies presumed available to the suspects. No practical intervention applies automatically to every setting with this preference structure.

These practical intervention families are editorial synthesis. If the encounter must remain one-shot, the payoffs fixed, and agreements unenforceable, there is no strategy that makes mutual cooperation a Nash equilibrium of this strict game.

## A worked example

### A promise does not change a dominant action

Before their separate choices, Noor and Leo express a wish that neither implicate the other. Once the offers are considered, Noor still prefers implicating against silence and against implication. Leo reasons the same way.

In the model's equilibrium outcome, both implicate and receive the mutually worse result. Their shared preference for mutual silence has not made silence a best response. This follows from the hypothetical ranking, not an observation of a real case.

There is no intervention within the unchanged one-shot game that turns mutual silence into an equilibrium. Binding commitments, different consequences, or a future relationship would change assumptions. Recognizing that limit is part of the example; the story supplies no recommendation for arranging actual criminal proceedings.

### An everyday application where redesign is available

Suppose two workshop users each prefer skipping their assigned cleanup whether the other cleans or skips, while both prefer mutual cleanup to mutual neglect. A repeated appeal leaves those rankings unchanged.

For this separate hypothetical application, an authorized coordinator introduces an accepted booking condition: verified omissions cause a reliable loss of prepaid access. Assume the consequence makes cleanup preferable against either action, checks are accurate, and users prefer participating under the new terms to leaving. Mutual cleanup is then the equilibrium of the revised game.

The intervention changes incentives; it does not solve the original game under fixed payoffs. Verification takes staff time, mistaken penalties impose losses, and a routinely waived rule may restore the temptation. This example describes a conditional design, not measured improvement.

### What experiments establish

Dal Bó and Fréchette's 2011 laboratory study used randomly continuing prisoner's dilemmas and varied continuation probabilities and cooperation payoffs. Cooperation did not reliably emerge merely because repeated-game equilibrium conditions allowed it. The controlled monetary setting informs the gap between possibility and behavior; it does not validate either teaching story's preferences or an enforcement scheme. [Original article](https://gfrechette.com/print/Dal_Bo_2011a.pdf)

## Limits and lookalikes

Check the actual preference ordering before applying the label. Guilt, concern for others, reputation, or future access may already affect payoffs. If cooperation is preferred when the other cooperates, the situation may be an assurance game rather than this strict dilemma.

Real relationships can involve unequal power, several actions, disputed observations, or changing partners. The two-by-two model deliberately leaves those out. An equilibrium analysis conditional on preferences does not prove that people are selfish or that punishment is preferable to redesign.
