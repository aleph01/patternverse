## A recognizable situation

Nora reviews purchase requests. Large commitments require additional approval, but the automated check looks only at each individual request. A department divides one planned purchase into smaller requests, each below the trigger.

Every record passes that narrow check. The department still makes the larger commitment without the intended review. Lowering the per-request trigger may simply produce more fragments.

This hypothetical system illustrates passing an inadequate test, not necessarily complying with the complete rules. A policy can already prohibit deliberate splitting even when its software fails to detect it.

## The underlying pattern

**Rule Beating is an adaptive response that meets, or appears to meet, a rule's formal test while evading its intended constraint.** Its central question is: **What can someone do that passes this check but defeats the purpose?**

Donella Meadows treats Rule Beating as a named system trap in _Thinking in Systems_ (2008). Her emphasis is on people adapting around rules and on redesigning rules so that ingenuity serves their purpose. She did not originate the broader study of distorted incentives. Steven Kerr's 1975 article examined arrangements that reward one behavior while hoping for another. [Meadows, pp. 136–137](https://www.sustainable.soltechdesigns.com/meadows.pdf), [Kerr's original article](https://doi.org/10.2307/255378)

The essential ingredients are a purpose, an operational test or boundary, and room to adapt around that boundary. A loophole can matter even when every recorded fact is accurate. Some variants also involve concealment or false reporting; the label alone establishes neither legality nor dishonesty.

## What keeps it going

The department wants the whole purchase while avoiding the delay or restriction of additional approval. Dividing the same requirement changes the records presented to the checker, not the overall commitment.

The report can accurately say each request passed its test. That does not show the institution reviewed what it intended to govern. A department respecting the aggregate purpose may face scrutiny that a department exploiting the checked unit escapes.

Complaints can prompt a lower trigger, more elaborate splitting, and further checks. Administrative work and exceptions may accumulate while the resulting commitments create obligations beyond the original report. This adaptive cycle is a hypothesis to examine, not a necessary consequence of detailed rules.

The relevant relationship is between the checked unit and the behavior being governed. Tightening the same unit does not necessarily repair that mismatch.

## Why the obvious responses fail

**Lower the trigger.** If the check still treats each request independently, the same planned purchase can be fragmented further.

**Inspect every record more carefully.** Accurate amounts on separate requests can still conceal their connection. Reviewing the wrong unit perfectly leaves the aggregate commitment unchecked.

**Treat a passing flag as complete compliance.** A software test is evidence about the test it performed. It is not a conclusion about every applicable policy or legal obligation.

**Punish every small related request.** Separate needs, staged work, and legitimate urgency can resemble fragmentation. Blanket penalties can discourage disclosure and obstruct useful purchases.

## Possible ways out

**Confirm the purpose and authority.** The responsible owner should state which commitments require review and why. Cost control, competition, safety, and speed can conflict. An unjustified or unworkable approval requirement may need redesign rather than stricter detection.

**Check the aggregate requirement.** Nora can link requests serving the same planned acquisition, use a meaningful time boundary, and refer the combined commitment for review. Identity and aggregation criteria need proportionate evidence; a shared supplier alone need not make unrelated purchases one requirement.

**Use transparent review and an appeal route.** Explain which records were linked and allow correction of mistaken grouping. Review consumes time and can introduce inconsistent discretion. Consistent consequences for deliberate evasion can support a sound rule, but cannot make an unsuitable rule useful.

**Provide a legitimate route for urgency or staging.** A department can seek approval for the whole commitment with phased delivery or an expressly authorized exception. This can preserve necessary scrutiny while allowing feasible work. Approval is not guaranteed: the authority may defer, reduce, or reject the commitment.

These changes alter the checked boundary and decision rights. They do not merely ask the department to stop noticing the existing loophole.

## A worked example

### Review the purchase the fragments belong to

Nora first lowers the individual-request trigger. The department submits smaller requests, so the larger commitment still escapes review. Some legitimate purchases also generate extra paperwork.

She examines whether the records represent separate needs or one planned acquisition. In this hypothetical diagnosis, they are parts of the same known requirement; the issue is not an unexpected later purchase or a permitted staged authorization.

The policy owner clarifies aggregate review, and the system links related requests to the overall requirement. Nora notifies the department of the grouping, gives it an opportunity to correct mistakes, and routes the complete commitment through the appropriate approval process. An urgent or phased purchase has a visible authorized route.

In the stipulated continuation, the department submits the full plan. The responsible authority approves a smaller feasible scope after review. The purchase proceeds within that authorization; some desired items are deferred. The benefit is restored scrutiny of the intended commitment, not a guarantee that all requests are funded or all linked requests deserve rejection.

If affiliates or shifting dates defeat the new link, the boundary needs further examination. Excessively broad aggregation can also block unrelated work. Evidence, appeals, and the cost of review remain important.

An actual procurement counterpart makes the compliance boundary clear: the US Federal Acquisition Regulation page for section 13.003(c)(2) explicitly addresses prohibited splitting to avoid applicable purchasing requirements. That is a documented rule in a particular regime, not a description of every organization's policy or a claim that this fictional test represents it. [Official text](https://www.acquisition.gov/far/13.003)

## Limits and lookalikes

An unexpected way of achieving the intended outcome with fewer resources is legitimate improvement, not rule beating merely because it was unforeseen. Conversely, falsification may be both rule beating and a violation of an explicit rule. Intent, actual terms, and consequences require evidence.

Bevan and Hood's 2006 analysis of English healthcare targets describes reported practices such as keeping patients in ambulances outside emergency departments until their recorded wait inside could meet the target. This illustrates shifting activity across a measurement boundary. Their evidence combined inquiries, reports, surveys, and other studies; incomplete audit prevented them from determining how much reported improvement was genuine or offset by gaming. It does not establish that all improvements were false or that most providers behaved this way. [Original analysis](https://straty.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/Whats-Measured-is-What-Matters-Bevan-Hood-2006.pdf)

The purchase example assumes a legitimate review purpose. When people dispute that purpose or the authority behind it, better record linkage will not settle the disagreement. The purpose itself needs a legitimate decision process.
