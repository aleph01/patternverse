## A recognizable situation

Nora launches a local secondhand marketplace. Buyers look for useful listings and leave when they find little nearby. Sellers see few serious buyers and decide that photographing and listing their items is not worth the effort.

Nora advertises to both sides. Registrations grow, but buyers still struggle to find suitable goods and sellers still receive few inquiries. Each side is waiting for the other to make joining worthwhile.

This hypothetical launch has a familiar chicken-and-egg difficulty: buyers attract sellers, and sellers attract buyers. People are allowed to join now; the missing condition is enough useful activity in the same market.

## The underlying pattern

**A cold start occurs when an activity needs participation to become valuable, but its initial lack of value discourages that participation.** Members wait for an active community; contributors wait for an audience; sellers wait for buyers.

**Critical mass is the configuration of relevant participation that can support continued activity under particular conditions.** It concerns who participates, where, when, and how reliably. It is not automatically a percentage of everyone who could join, nor a promise of growth after a single launch.

Several traditions inform this account. Granovetter's 1978 threshold models connect individuals' willingness to act with others' participation, showing that distributions of thresholds can produce very different collective outcomes. [Granovetter](https://gwern.net/doc/sociology/1978-granovetter.pdf)

Network economics examines how compatible users and components affect value and adoption. [Katz and Shapiro, 1994](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/jep.8.2.93) Andrew Chen's _The Cold Start Problem_ develops a business framework around an “atomic network”: a small network capable of sustaining useful participation. It is a practitioner framework, not the origin of all threshold theory. [Chen, 2021](https://andrewchen.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/ColdStartProb_9780062969743_AS0928_cc20_Final.pdf)

## What keeps it going

A large directory does not guarantee a useful market. Nora's buyers need available goods of the right kind within collection distance. Sellers need people likely to buy what they offer. Registrations in distant places or unrelated categories do not necessarily help either side.

Thin supply discourages buyers from returning; low buyer activity discourages sellers from listing. Suitable listings and credible demand could instead encourage transactions, further listings, and recommendations. These are causal hypotheses to test through current inventory, serious inquiries, completed exchanges, and later activity. A signup total establishes none of them.

Concentrating a launch changes who can usefully encounter whom. Starting with one district and one category can make a viable exchange more likely without adding more registrants overall. It also excludes some people, and cannot create willingness to sell, reliable pickup, or demand for unsuitable goods.

There need not be one magic headcount. A smaller network with compatible needs can be more useful than a large one whose members have no reason to trade with one another.

## Why the obvious responses fail

**Advertise everywhere.** Awareness can grow while each local category remains too thin to use. More registrations do not establish a working buyer–seller match.

**Recruit only the easy side.** More buyers cannot buy goods that sellers have not listed. More sellers cannot create willing demand merely by waiting.

**Count launch-day traffic as critical mass.** Curiosity, subsidies, and hands-on support can generate a busy first occasion without supporting ordinary use later.

**Wait for everyone to join spontaneously.** Each side's reasonable reluctance can preserve the other side's reason to stay away.

## Possible ways out

**Concentrate the initial market.** An organizer can focus on a usable location, category, and collection window. Relevant supply and demand become easier to connect, at the cost of excluding other users during the launch.

**Recruit the missing roles.** Speak to both sides, establish what each needs, and secure real availability. Extra buyers do not replace absent sellers. Support may need to be asymmetric, and listing assistance or guarantees cost someone resources.

**Offer standalone value or temporary support.** A useful inventory tool, assisted listing, or subsidized first exchange can make joining worthwhile before the network is strong. This changes the return to participating. It may attract people interested only in the subsidy, so later activity must be tested separately.

**Make commitments visible and credible.** Current listings, confirmed availability, and clear pickup arrangements can reduce uncertainty. Expressions of interest are not purchases; a confirmed seller may still withdraw.

**Test continued activity as support changes.** Track useful matches and completed exchanges across repeated occasions. One functioning district is a starting point for another test, not proof of profitable expansion everywhere. These are practical design families rather than guaranteed launch methods.

## A worked example

### Start one useful local market before expanding

Nora's wider advertising produces signups but few exchanges. She interviews prospective buyers and sellers. For this hypothetical diagnosis, the problem is sparse relevant inventory and uncertainty about demand, rather than an unusable website or prices nobody can afford.

She narrows the launch to secondhand bicycles in one district. She recruits owners with available bikes, checks that listings are current, and tells interested local buyers what is actually offered. She provides temporary help with listing and pickup arrangements. That work costs time; other categories and districts wait.

In the stipulated successful outcome, suitable bikes and interested buyers meet, some trades complete, and further sellers have a reason to list. Nora then checks whether useful new listings and buyer activity continue as the temporary support is reduced. Selling the first batch is not evidence of a self-sustaining market. Buyers of durable goods need not return frequently; continuing activity may require new buyers as well as returning participants.

For a simplified strategic benchmark, each participant prefers joining a useful active market to staying away, but prefers staying away to a fruitless listing or search. Choices are made before observing everyone else's current participation. Under those stated preferences, widespread participation and widespread absence can both sustain themselves: one isolated participant cannot create the missing other side. Real users have different needs and entry costs, so this benchmark is not a launch forecast.

If suitable supply does not exist or the interested buyers cannot reach it, concentrating publicity cannot fix the problem. Nora needs another initial market, useful standalone services, more support, or a smaller promise.

### A documented business interpretation

Chen describes buyer–seller marketplaces as a cold-start setting and contrasts them with workplace chat, where a small team may already form a useful network. Credit-card networks need both cardholders and accepting merchants. These are practitioner case interpretations, not controlled demonstrations that concentrating any launch causes success. [Chen, introduction and chapter 6](https://andrewchen.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/ColdStartProb_9780062969743_AS0928_cc20_Final.pdf)

## Limits and lookalikes

**There is no universal critical number.** Centola and colleagues' 2018 experiment found tipping dynamics around a committed minority of 25% in an artificial naming-convention task. Participants received incentives for pairwise coordination. The authors explicitly reject treating 25% as universal. Changing an established convention also differs from filling an initially empty service. [Centola and colleagues](https://www.asc.upenn.edu/sites/default/files/2021-03/Experimental%20evidence%20for%20tipping%20points%20in%20social%20convention.pdf)

Individual thresholds, a market's minimum useful size, a platform's continued viability, and profitability are different quantities. Preferences may vary; congestion, poor matching, or unreliable participation can reduce value as numbers grow. A smooth increase in usefulness need not contain a sharp tipping point.

A circular prerequisite blocks a permitted first step. A cold start can allow joining immediately while making it unattractive. Ordinary startup expense lacks this participation dependence. “Cold start” also has other technical meanings, including recommending to a user with little history and initializing inactive software; those are outside this article's scope.
