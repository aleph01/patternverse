## A recognizable situation

Nila runs a customer-support service that promises a timely substantive response. The team repeatedly misses that promise. Management relaxes the response standard to match recent performance “until things settle down.”

The next report looks better, although customers wait just as long. With less recorded shortfall, work on restoring response capacity loses priority. Waiting then worsens, and the acceptable response time is relaxed again.

This hypothetical service distinguishes an honest forecast from a lowering standard: the report improves because more delay is being accepted, not because users are getting help sooner.

## The underlying pattern

**Drift to Low Performance occurs when disappointing results lower the standard used to judge them, weakening corrective action and making further deterioration easier.** The memorable formulation is: **the gap closes because the benchmark falls.**

This is a system dynamics **archetype**, a recurring feedback structure, also called **Eroding Goals** or **Drifting Goals**. Peter Senge described eroding goals in _The Fifth Discipline_ (1990); Donella Meadows used this article's title in _Thinking in Systems_ (2008). These are qualitative structural accounts, not a theorem that every adjusted target causes decline. [Senge, Appendix 2](https://www.bookdio.org/_files/ugd/ef8af1_5169a4238fd3412dba674fd8dbd81be6.pdf), [Meadows, pp. 121–123](https://www.sustainable.soltechdesigns.com/meadows.pdf)

The essential conditions are an adjustable standard, disappointing performance, and a link from the recognized shortfall to corrective effort. Lowering an inappropriate goal can be sensible. The trap arises when recent failure substitutes for a reasoned judgment about what the service must achieve.

## What keeps it going

An ordinary **balancing feedback loop** counteracts a shortfall: a larger gap prompts correction, which improves performance and reduces the gap. A second route also reduces the gap: management lowers the goal. This route can act before recruitment, training, or process repair produces results.

When a lower goal reduces attention or resources, actual performance can slip further, prompting another reduction. That return path is **reinforcing feedback**: each round helps create the conditions for another. Meadows emphasizes how remembering bad results more readily than good ones can strengthen it. That bias is an amplifier; Nila’s example depends on continuing service pressure and repeated accommodation of missed response promises.

Standards accumulate successive revisions. Skills and staffing also change over time, with delays. A monthly report can conceal both the accumulated lowering of expectations and the lag before corrective work pays off. This causal account needs investigation: target histories, resource decisions, unchanged quality measures, and users' experiences should support it before the label is applied.

## Why the obvious responses fail

**Celebrate the shrinking gap.** A smaller distance from a moving target does not establish improvement. Report actual results and the history of target changes together.

**Insist on the original number.** This removes one escape route but creates no usable response capacity. Under conflicting quality, cost, and schedule demands, rigidity can encourage corner-cutting elsewhere. Daniel H. Kim's account explicitly examines that danger. [Conflicting priorities](https://thesystemsthinker.com/drifting-goals-the-challenge-of-conflicting-priorities/)

**Reward the best week by permanently raising expectations.** Exceptional performance may depend on overtime, unusually easy work, or luck. Treating it as sustainable capacity can start another cycle of misses. Historical success is evidence to examine, not an automatic new requirement.

## Possible ways out

**Give the standard an independent anchor.** Service owners can connect it to user needs, commitments, or a tested operating requirement, rather than automatically copying recent results. Keep exceptions visible and give temporary concessions an expiry and a named reviewer. This changes how the goal is governed; it does not guarantee better work.

**Make correction feasible.** Managers controlling resources can protect substantive response time, remove duplicate work, improve handovers, or reduce incoming scope. Check the actual limiting activity before adding people. These changes cost money or displace other work; their effects need observation over the relevant delay.

**Revise openly when the requirement is wrong or unaffordable.** Those responsible for the promise, including affected users where possible, must choose among capacity, scope, timing, and service level. Virginia Anderson distinguishes legitimate adaptation from erosion: flexibility itself is not failure. Preserve the reason and consequences of the decision instead of silently relabeling the same service as satisfactory. [Anderson's account](https://thesystemsthinker.com/introducing-the-systems-archetypes-drifting-goals/)

## A worked example

### Keep the service promise distinct from the recovery forecast

Another missed response promise prompts Nila's managers to accept a longer wait. The reported shortfall narrows immediately. No customer receives help sooner, but restoring response coverage now appears less urgent.

Nila shows actual response times alongside the full history of standards. She distinguishes acknowledgments from substantive help and reviews user needs, demand, work complexity, and staffing. For this hypothetical diagnosis, the service promise remains worthwhile and achievable with a feasible change in coverage; repeated relaxation has displaced that correction.

Management restores an agreed reference grounded in user needs, funds usable response coverage, and gives the temporary recovery forecast a separate name and review date. Staff can report likely misses honestly without those forecasts automatically becoming the new definition of acceptable service.

In the stipulated improvement, the added coverage becomes operational and more requests receive substantive responses within the agreed standard. Recruitment, training, and inherited work can delay improvement. A restored target alone supplies no staff and clears no backlog.

The intervention changes both standard-setting and the capacity to act on a shortfall. It costs resources that could serve another purpose. If the original promise is no longer appropriate or affordable, responsible owners may openly change service scope, timing, or access after considering affected users.

A realistic forecast and a desired service standard answer different questions. The problem is not telling customers the truth about delays; it is letting each disappointing result quietly decide what users should be expected to accept.

## Limits and lookalikes

Poor performance alone is insufficient. Demand may have changed, the original target may lack justification, or measurement may be noisy. The example isolates the standard-to-correction feedback. Demand, inherited backlogs, turnover, and training can also affect actual response times; none establishes erosion on its own. The narrative illustrates a possible direction of change; the archetype alone predicts no rate of decline.

There is relevant field-informed work. Oliva and Sterman's service model draws on a UK bank study in which internal time-per-task standards adjusted downward more readily than upward. Their later chapter explains how those norms interact with work pressure and capacity decisions. This supports a plausible mechanism in a particular setting, not a universal causal estimate or validation of the hypothetical service’s behavior. [Service-quality research](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/226538976_Death_Spirals_and_Virtuous_Cycles_Human_Resource_Dynamics_in_Knowledge-Based_Services)

Metric gaming changes what is counted; eroding goals can occur with honest, consistent measurement. Conversely, retaining an obsolete goal can waste resources. An explicit change of purpose is different from quietly accepting deterioration in the same promised outcome.
