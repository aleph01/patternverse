## A recognizable situation

Mara leads a school. Jo teaches both the skills covered by a standardized test and valuable work the test barely captures, including extended discussion and independent writing. Mara introduces a stronger reward tied to test results.

Jo devotes more lesson time to tested exercises. Those skills improve in this hypothetical case, while discussion and extended writing receive less attention. The reported results are accurate; nobody needs to cheat for the balance of teaching to change.

The recognizable concern is teaching narrowly to the test: a reward for one part of learning can pull attention away from other parts that the school also values.

## The underlying pattern

**Multitask incentive distortion occurs when rewards disproportionately favor some activities or output dimensions, redirecting effort away from others that also matter.** The activities interact: spending more attention on one makes another harder or less attractive.

The central question is: **What becomes less worthwhile when this measure becomes more rewarding?**

Holmström and Milgrom's 1991 _Multitask Principal-Agent Analyses_ is a foundational formal treatment. Its models explain why strong incentives on easily measured duties can be undesirable when competing duties are poorly measured. Conclusions depend on assumptions about task interactions, measurement, preferences, and available contracts; they are not a blanket argument against performance pay. The paper itself discusses test-based incentives for teachers; this is a theoretical illustration, not evidence that every testing policy produces this result. [Original paper](https://gwern.net/doc/economics/1991-holmstrom.pdf)

## What keeps it going

The school values several kinds of learning. Jo faces a reward tied to a narrower set of results. Time on tested exercises can become privately more rewarding than time on less readily measured discussion or writing, even when the school would prefer a different balance.

Incentives therefore affect both **how much effort** someone supplies and **where it goes**. Increased total effort does not guarantee a better allocation. The mechanism need not involve fabricated reports, poor intentions, or ignorance of the broader goal.

Baker's 1992 performance-measurement model formalizes a related distinction: a measure can respond differently to a worker's actions than the employer's underlying objective does. Accurate recording alone does not remove that mismatch. His formal results use particular information and contracting assumptions. [Original paper](https://www.edegan.com/pdfs/Baker%20%281992%29%20-%20Incentive%20Contracts%20and%20Performance%20Measurement.pdf)

## Why the obvious responses fail

**Increase the bonus again.** That can reinforce the same preference for counted output. More motivation is not necessarily better direction.

**Add another indicator.** It helps only if it captures relevant value and changes incentives appropriately. Counting discussion sessions can reward documentation without establishing useful learning.

**Tell people to care about everything.** Professional standards can matter, but competing rewards and workloads still shape what is practical. An instruction does not create extra capacity.

**Remove all variable pay.** This can reduce the distortion while also weakening productive effort. The worked example shows why neither a larger bonus nor no bonus necessarily works best.

## Possible ways out

**Reduce excessive rewards on the measured activity.** The manager can lower the opportunity cost of neglected duties by moderating the competing incentive. This helps only if workers have reasons to undertake those duties under the revised arrangement. Flat pay alone does not specify a desirable allocation.

**Improve relevant evaluation.** Credible quality checks, follow-up outcomes, and informed review can support broader rewards. Reliability, delay, attribution, and review costs matter. Subjective assessment also requires safeguards against favoritism and a shared understanding of good work.

**Redesign the job.** Protected responsibilities, separate roles, or protected teaching time can reduce competition for attention. These require adequate staffing and enforceable boundaries. Simply reserving time does not establish that high-quality work happens within it; separation can also lose useful coordination between tasks.

**Combine safeguards with incentives.** A meaningful quality floor or independent review can constrain harmful shortcuts. A safeguard must detect the relevant problem and have credible consequences. It may introduce waiting time, administrative work, or new measures to game.

These are practical design families. Choosing a work plan is a strategy within an arrangement. Changing rewards, evaluation, or task responsibilities changes that arrangement.

## A worked example

### Weaken the narrow reward and protect broader teaching

Jo can concentrate on tested exercises or distribute lesson preparation and class time across tested skills, discussion, and extended writing. Both kinds of work matter to Mara, but the original bonus depends only on test results. The reward is set before Jo chooses the plan. The simplified benchmark holds the available teaching time and pupils' needs fixed, assumes enforceable payment, and excludes later promotion or reputation rewards.

The stronger test-linked bonus makes a test-heavy plan privately attractive under the stipulated preferences. Jo can value broader learning and still respond to its increased opportunity cost. The tested learning is real; so is the work displaced. The example does not infer misconduct from improved results.

Mara first raises the test target again. That preserves the pressure. A review of lesson plans and student work then supports the hypothetical diagnosis: additional test preparation is taking time from worthwhile activities outside the test's scope. If instead tested and untested skills improved together, this diagnosis would need revision.

For a subsequent period, Mara moderates the narrow bonus, keeps acceptable base pay, and protects time for the broader work. Professional review examines the substance of that work rather than just counting sessions. Assume Mara has authority, the revised workload is feasible, and Jo prefers the balanced plan under the new terms.

In the stipulated outcome, Jo restores discussion and writing while still teaching tested skills. Test results may improve less than under the concentrated plan. No extra lesson time has appeared, and no test scores or learning effects are being reported as measurements.

The change affects incentives and organization. It is not proof that weaker incentives always improve teaching or that any particular balance is best. Removing too much reward can reduce useful effort; superficial review can introduce favoritism or a new target to game. The school needs evidence about actual learning, access, and workload as well as accurate test results.

## Limits and lookalikes

Here, “multitask” means several responsibilities or output dimensions, not necessarily rapid switching between activities. If tasks reinforce each other, rewarding one can help another. Pure capacity shortages, missing skills, noisy outcomes, and deliberate falsification require different diagnoses, although they may coexist.

Hong and colleagues' natural field experiment in Chinese factories found a quantity–quality trade-off from added quantity incentives, statistically clear among workers with flat-rate base pay and weaker among those already paid piece rates. Researchers inspected quality without workers' knowledge. Base-pay structures varied across factories rather than being randomly assigned, limiting that comparison. The linked full text is the 2017 manuscript; publication followed in 2018. [Study](https://fieldexperiments-papers2.s3.amazonaws.com/papers/00609.pdf) · [Published record](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/iere.12278)

The experiment does not establish that every bonus damages quality. Likewise, the value of neglected learning and the actual time trade-off must be investigated before redesigning the school’s rewards.

Multitask distortion specifically requires uneven incentives across interacting duties: real gains in tested skills can displace other valuable learning without anyone finding a loophole.
