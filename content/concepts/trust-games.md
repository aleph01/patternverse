## A recognizable situation

Maya finds an unfamiliar online seller offering goods she wants. Omar asks for payment before dispatch. The sale could benefit both, but once she transfers the money he will control whether to send the goods.

He promises to deliver. Maya asks what evidence makes that promise worth relying on. For this hypothetical benchmark, there is no effective payment protection or recovery mechanism; those safeguards would change the situation.

She must become vulnerable before observing his response. The question is how he will use the discretion that remains after she pays, rather than whether a payment service can constrain it.

## The underlying pattern

**A trust game places an initial, potentially beneficial act of vulnerability before another participant's discretionary response.** The first mover risks being worse off; the second can reciprocate or exploit the opportunity.

Here, trusting means accepting that exposure in the expectation of a beneficial response. Trustworthiness concerns how the second mover uses that discretion. Trusting a person can differ from accepting an impersonal lottery with the same material risks. Fehr's account connects trusting behavior to beliefs, risk preferences, and social preferences—how people value others' outcomes and intentions. [Specialist review](https://docs.iza.org/dp3895.pdf)

There is a family of trust games, not a single invention. Berg, Dickhaut, and McCabe’s 1995 **investment game** became a landmark laboratory design, building on earlier analysis. Participants could transfer some of an endowment, which was multiplied, and the recipient could return some of the proceeds. The online-purchase scenario translates its vulnerability and discretionary response into an everyday setting; it does not reproduce the experiment’s multiplied transfer or every platform’s protections. [Foundational experiment](https://christosaioannou.com/Trust%2C%20Reciprocity%20and%20Social%20History%20GEB%201995.pdf)

The central question is: **What happens after I have given you the ability to disappoint me?**

A trust game leaves a meaningful response to the recipient’s discretion. A commitment mechanism changes that discretion or its consequences; it does not demonstrate that the recipient has become more trustworthy.

## What keeps it going

Maya can keep control of her resources or entrust them to Omar. If she transfers them, Omar can honor the arrangement or keep the advantage for himself. He observes the transfer before deciding; Maya must choose without observing that later response.

For the narrow one-shot benchmark, assume both care only about their own material outcome, know that about each other, and have no enforceable agreement or future relationship consequences. Omar prefers keeping the resources once he controls them. Maya anticipates that choice and refuses the transfer.

This reasoning from the later choice back to the earlier one is **backward induction**. Refusal, together with Omar’s planned appropriation if a transfer occurred, is the subgame-perfect equilibrium under those assumptions: planned choices remain optimal at each decision point.

Both could prefer an honestly completed sale to no sale: Maya values receiving the goods and Omar earns an acceptable return. Yet after receiving payment, a seller caring only about the stipulated own-material outcome prefers keeping the money and goods to incurring delivery costs. Different preferences, including reciprocity or concern for Maya, can change the result; rationality does not require selfishness.

## Why the obvious responses fail

**“Show that cooperation benefits everyone.”** That explains why Maya wants a return. It does not remove Omar’s later incentive to keep control of the resources.

**“Get a stronger promise.”** An unenforceable promise leaves the original options available. Promises may matter to someone who values keeping them; assuming that motivation changes the preference model. Words alone do not establish it.

**“Call sending proof of trust.”** Maya might send because she wants to help Omar, even without expecting repayment. Likewise, a return could reflect generosity or concern about unequal outcomes rather than a response to Maya's action. Cox's three-game experimental design uses comparison conditions to separate these motives more carefully. [Original study](https://econweb.ucsd.edu/~jandreon/Econ264/papers/Cox%20GEB%202004.pdf)

## Possible ways out

**Improve the basis for a voluntary judgment.** Relevant past behavior, verifiable references, and opportunities to ask questions can inform beliefs about the other participant. Their value depends on whether the evidence concerns comparable choices. Successful performance when there was no opportunity to exploit someone provides limited evidence about that opportunity.

**Limit the exposure.** Smaller transfers, restricted access, staged delivery, or reversible steps can reduce the loss before a response is observed. These change the available actions and sometimes the benefits. They help only if the stages can actually be separated; some projects require an indivisible initial commitment.

**Make honoring the arrangement worthwhile later.** Repeated dealings can introduce future gains that would be lost after exploitation. This requires observable behavior, identifiable partners, and a valuable continuation. A vague expectation of meeting again supplies none of those conditions by itself. Known final encounters can undermine the mechanism.

**Change decision rights or enforcement.** A contract, controlled payment mechanism, or trusted intermediary can prevent diversion or attach consequences to it. Someone must implement and fund the safeguard. This creates reliance on enforcement and may reduce the need for interpersonal trust over that particular action.

These are intervention families, not guarantees that a counterpart will become trustworthy.

## A worked example

### A small purchase bounds exposure; it does not enforce delivery

Maya asks for a stronger promise. Omar still controls whether to dispatch after payment. In the complete-information benchmark where he is known to care only about the stipulated own-material return, she still declines. Making the payment smaller alone does not reverse that prediction.

For a practical trial, change the information assumption explicitly: Maya is uncertain about his preferences and conduct. Comparable, verifiable selling history supplies some reason to expect delivery, without proving it. She considers a small order she can use independently and can afford to lose, with no commitment to buy the rest.

Omar still receives that payment before deciding whether to send the order. There is no intermediary preventing appropriation in this version. The smaller order changes the possible loss and available action, while leaving a meaningful discretionary response.

In a stipulated successful continuation, he sends the described goods and they arrive. Maya obtains evidence about this transaction and can consider another. This is an assumed event, not a measured result or an equilibrium prediction under the earlier known-selfish-preferences benchmark.

He could instead keep the payment without delivering. Maya would lose the exposed amount. A successful small sale does not establish how he would act at higher stakes or under different pressures; expected future business might itself have influenced the trial.

If the goods cannot usefully be divided, or Maya cannot bear losing even the smaller payment, the trial is unavailable. Declining can remain reasonable. A protected payment arrangement is another response: it changes control or consequences rather than demonstrating personal trustworthiness. That distinction belongs to [[credible-commitment|Credible Commitment]].

## Limits and lookalikes

The original investment experiment observed positive transfers and returns despite anonymous, one-shot conditions. This challenges a prediction built on exclusively own-money preferences, not game theory as a whole. It does not establish a universal rate of reciprocation. [Berg and colleagues](https://christosaioannou.com/Trust%2C%20Reciprocity%20and%20Social%20History%20GEB%201995.pdf)

When Omar’s preferences are uncertain, Maya’s decision also depends on evidence about his likely response, the consequences of non-delivery, and her ability to bear that loss. Trusting a small initial stage may be acceptable where paying for the full order would not be. No universal confidence cutoff follows from the idea of a trust game.

Trust need not be all-or-nothing or transferable across domains. Delivering one small order does not demonstrate future honesty, capability for a larger order, or reliability when stakes change. The same caution applies to interpreting one return in a laboratory transfer game. Conversely, cautious refusal can reflect exposure, uncertainty, or preferences rather than a general inability to trust.

## Related patterns

[[credible-commitment|Credible Commitment]] changes the later options or consequences. Trust analysis asks how someone will use discretion that remains; safeguards can change that exposure rather than establish trustworthiness.

[[the-stag-hunt|The Stag Hunt]] makes cooperation attractive when the other cooperates. Here the later mover can gain by exploiting an initial cooperative act.

[[the-prisoners-dilemma|The Prisoner's Dilemma]] gives both players incentives to defect. A trust game foregrounds sequence and asymmetric vulnerability; the first participant can withhold the opportunity altogether.

[[bargaining-problems|Bargaining Problems]] asks which terms participants select. A trust problem can remain afterward if someone can abandon those terms once the other has committed.
