## A recognizable situation

Two hunters can stay together to pursue a stag or each take the safer opportunity of hunting a hare. Neither can obtain the stag alone. Both would prefer their share of a successful joint hunt to a hare, but a hunter left pursuing the stag alone loses the dependable alternative.

Each looks at the other and chooses the hare. Both obtain the less rewarding result because neither is confident the partner will stay with the joint plan.

This is a stylized version of the classic story behind the name. It isolates a recognizable predicament: **the better plan is worth choosing if the other person joins, but costly to choose alone.**

## The underlying pattern

**A Stag Hunt is a coordination game in which mutual cooperation is better for everyone, but a less ambitious option protects each person against the other's noncooperation.** It is also called an **assurance game**.

The name comes from the deer-and-hare passage in Rousseau's _Discourse on Inequality_ (1755): a collective hunt depends on participants remaining at their posts, while an accessible hare offers an individual opportunity. That story supplies the image, not a complete modern payoff table. [Original passage](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/11136/pg11136.html)

The crucial distinction from the prisoner's dilemma is that cooperation is a best response to cooperation. Brian Skyrms's account emphasizes the tension between mutual benefit and personal exposure when expectations are uncertain. [_The Stag Hunt_, 2001](https://sites.socsci.uci.edu/~bskyrms/bio/papers/StagHunt.pdf)

## What keeps it going

Asha prefers stag when Ben chooses stag, but hare when he chooses hare. Ben has the same preference. Unlike a prisoner's dilemma, success by the other cooperator does not create a reason to defect; it makes cooperation attractive.

For this one-shot benchmark, each chooses stag or hare before observing the other's current choice. Both know the alternatives and preferences. Successful joint hunting is best for both, hunting hare gives a dependable fallback, and hunting stag alone is worst. Technical hunting risk is set aside to isolate uncertainty about the partner. There is no binding commitment or later reputation reward.

Both stag and both hare are **Nash equilibria**: neither improves by changing alone at either outcome. A hunter unilaterally abandoning the hare does not obtain the joint prize.

The choice under uncertainty depends on the expected response and the consequences of being left alone. There is no universal confidence percentage at which cooperation becomes sensible. Qualitative rankings identify the incentive structure without supplying a probability threshold.

## Why the obvious responses fail

**Explain that cooperation pays more.** Successful stag hunting is best when both join. That comparison omits what happens to someone who commits while the other chooses the hare.

**Label caution selfishness.** Choosing the hare need not express a preference for the other's failure. It can be a response to unreliable availability, uncertain intentions, or previous disappointments.

**Ask one person to go first.** An observable, irreversible commitment can reassure the other, but transfers exposure to the first mover. If action and observation are possible before the second decision, this becomes a sequential game rather than the original simultaneous one.

**Treat a promise as demonstrated capacity.** An intention cannot supply missing time, skills, or resources. Even sincere partners may fail to deliver.

## Possible ways out

These practical recommendations are editorial synthesis; their effectiveness depends on the particular situation.

**Make readiness and intentions more credible.** Agree roles, verify availability, and communicate before costly choices become irreversible. This can support selection of the cooperative equilibrium without changing stage payoffs. Evidence must concern the commitment that matters; agreement about the joint benefit is weaker than credible readiness to perform the necessary part.

**Reduce the exposed participant's loss.** A funded guarantee, reusable preparation, or a workable fallback can make an unsuccessful commitment less damaging. Specify who supplies the protection and what triggers it. This changes payoffs or available responses; its cost belongs in the wider decision.

**Use a smaller trial or staged commitment.** A modest joint project may reveal reliability while limiting exposure. This needs meaningful evidence and enough time to learn. A successful trial does not prove that a larger commitment will work; staging changes timing and may sacrifice some benefits.

**Accept the safer arrangement when assurance is unavailable.** Missing resources or unacceptable losses may make the safer individual option defensible. Achieving the preferred outcome can require someone to bear a real cost, change the project, or take a risk nobody can eliminate.

## A worked example

### Assurance selects a plan; it does not supply a partner

Asha and Ben initially repeat that a stag would be worth more. That leaves each wondering whether the other will abandon the joint attempt. Both still choose hare in the hypothetical cautious continuation.

Before another choice, they discuss the required roles, check that both can participate, and acknowledge the same joint plan. Suppose this evidence gives each sufficient reason to expect the other's cooperation. In the stipulated successful continuation, both choose stag and obtain the jointly preferred result.

The intervention supports selection of an existing equilibrium. It does not change the original ranking or prove that nonbinding messages always work. If either still expects the other to choose hare, the cautious equilibrium remains. A promise that conceals missing capability supplies no assurance.

### A modern application with protection against failure

Two tutors considering a joint course face a similar comparison if each prefers successful joint teaching to a dependable solo class, but is worse off preparing alone. A centre can verify readiness and fund limited compensation for preparation left unusable when the other tutor does not join.

Assume that protection is reliable but limited: joint success remains best, and solo teaching still beats relying entirely on compensation. It can make cooperation attractive under a wider range of beliefs by reducing the exposed tutor's loss. Both-solo can still remain an equilibrium.

That protection changes payoffs rather than merely selecting an outcome. Somebody must fund it, establish eligibility, and honor it. In an illustrative successful joint course no compensation is paid, but reserving the money still has an opportunity cost. The mechanism is not evidence that the centre has improved outcomes.

### What communication experiments add

Charness's 2000 laboratory study used student participants, random rematching, and written one-way messages in Stag Hunt variants. Communication before the sender chose an action supported substantially more efficient coordination than no communication. Reversing that order produced much less consistent results. This shows that nonbinding messages can matter under specific conditions; it validates neither a particular promise nor the centre's guarantee. [Original study](https://econweb.ucsd.edu/~jandreon/Econ264/papers/Charness%20GEB%202003.pdf)

## Limits and lookalikes

The successful joint outcome is **payoff-dominant**: both hunters prefer it to both choosing hare. **Risk dominance** is a formal comparison of how robust equilibria are to uncertainty about others’ choices. The qualitative preference ranking alone does not establish which equilibrium is risk-dominant; different losses and gains can change that comparison. Neither term says which choice is morally better or inevitably selected. [Skyrms’s analysis](https://sites.socsci.uci.edu/~bskyrms/bio/papers/StagHunt.pdf)

Real partnerships may have unequal stakes, uncertain technical success, more participants, or private information. A dependable solo fallback is a simplifying feature here; variants can have different fallback outcomes. Confidence in one partner may be insufficient if several essential participants must deliver.

A sequential **trust game**, where one person transfers resources and another later decides whether to reciprocate, has different timing and incentives. A Stag Hunt is not simply any situation involving trust.

## Related patterns

[[coordination-problems|Coordination Problems]] is the broader family. Here the equilibria differ in rewards and vulnerability, rather than merely offering interchangeable meeting points.

[[the-prisoners-dilemma|The Prisoner's Dilemma]] has an incentive to defect even against a cooperator. In this Stag Hunt, confidence in cooperation can make cooperation individually preferable.
