## A recognizable situation

Mina and Owen arrange to meet outside a concert venue. It has a North entrance and a South entrance. Either would suit both equally well.

Mina waits at North because that is where they met last time. Owen waits at South because that entrance appeared on the ticket. Each has made a plausible guess about the same vague instruction: “Meet outside.”

They both want to be together, and neither needs persuading that meeting is worthwhile. In this hypothetical situation, they need the same usable plan.

## The underlying pattern

**A coordination problem arises when success depends on mutually compatible actions, but participants face uncertainty about which compatible plan others will follow.** Knowing the shared objective does not necessarily identify the action to take.

Game theory examines this through coordination games with multiple equilibria. An **equilibrium** is a combination of strategies where nobody benefits by changing alone. Several combinations may satisfy that test, leaving an **equilibrium-selection problem**: which will people actually expect and implement?

Thomas Schelling's _The Strategy of Conflict_ (1960) gave an influential account of tacit coordination and **focal points**: features people use to align expectations when they cannot communicate. A familiar landmark or precedent can matter because each expects the other to recognize it. Schelling did not claim that every group would read the same clue. [Chapter 3](https://gamef21.classes.ryansafner.com/readings/Schelling-1960-Ch3.pdf)

Compatible actions need not always be identical actions: a caller and a person waiting for a call can coordinate through different roles. In this example, what must match is Mina and Owen’s location and time.

## What keeps it going

Mina wants to go where Owen goes; Owen wants to go where Mina goes. Neither entrance is intrinsically better under the example’s assumptions. Each choice is sensible only in relation to the other person’s choice.

Treat this as one meeting: each chooses North or South before observing the other’s current decision. Both know that matching is preferred to splitting, travel costs are equal, and there is no recovery before the agreed meeting time. Future rewards and penalties are excluded.

Both North and both South are **Nash equilibria**: neither participant gains by switching alone once the other is at that location. A split is not an equilibrium, even though incompatible expectations can produce one. Participants may regret their choices as soon as they discover the mismatch.

The obstacle is a shared expectation that each can reasonably act on. An explicit acknowledged plan can select one existing equilibrium without adding rewards, penalties, or new entrances.

## Why the obvious responses fail

**Repeat the shared goal.** Both already want to meet. “See you outside” still leaves two entrances.

**Choose what seems obvious.** A cue helps only if others recognize it in the same way. The previous meeting and the printed ticket point in different directions.

**Tell both people to be flexible.** If each independently walks to the other entrance, they can pass without meeting. Revisions need a shared rule about who moves and who waits.

**Assume repetition guarantees the best convention.** Experience can establish a default, but a familiar meeting place can become inconvenient or inaccessible. In richer coordination games, learning can also select an equilibrium everyone ranks below another.

## Possible ways out

These intervention families are practical editorial synthesis, not guaranteed effects of sending a message.

**Specify and acknowledge one plan.** A coordinator names the location, time, participants, and current arrangement; both confirm the same details before departure. When interests already align, this can select an existing equilibrium without changing payoffs. It requires communication and credible follow-through. A message sitting unread supplies little assurance.

**Establish a shared convention.** Repeated collaborators can agree a default meeting point or a fallback for lost contact. When communication is unavailable, a recognizable precedent may provide a focal point. Conventions require a shared reference and can become costly when circumstances change; familiarity alone is not evidence of suitability.

**Delegate selection and clarify revisions.** Participants can authorize one person to choose among acceptable alternatives. Replace stale notices and confirm consequential changes. Delegation reduces competing instructions but introduces dependence on the selector's competence, availability, and legitimacy. It does not make an inaccessible location usable.

**Reduce the cost of mismatch.** A messenger, transport between sites, or additional time before the concert may make recovery possible. Someone must supply that capacity and bear its cost. This changes the original game's feasible responses or payoffs; it is more than selecting a meeting point within the unchanged model.

**Negotiate unequal stakes.** If North burdens one friend more, a convention may settle location while distributing costs unfairly. Discuss compensation, alternation, or a different site. Adding such arrangements changes the decision beyond the equal-preference example.

## A worked example

### Replace separate guesses with an acknowledged meeting point

Mina and Owen initially rely on “outside.” Each becomes more confident in a different entrance. Greater confidence in separate interpretations does not bring them together.

Before the next meeting, Mina names North entrance and the time. Owen checks that he means the same entrance and confirms. They agree that a later change requires acknowledgment; if contact is lost, the confirmed point remains the default.

In the stipulated successful outcome, both go North. Either person switching alone would recreate the split. Communication has selected an existing equilibrium without changing their preferences or adding a new entrance. A phone call can make this an easily resolved problem; the label describes the structure, not inevitable difficulty.

An unread message or closed entrance can still defeat the plan. Recovery needs communication, time, a feasible alternative, and clarity about who moves. Those resources are absent from the simplified first-choice benchmark, so adding recovery changes that model.

Two private coin tosses can yield opposite entrances. One shared draw could coordinate them if both observe and follow the same mapping, but they must first agree on that procedure. There is little reason to randomize when they can simply acknowledge one acceptable point.

### What experiments establish

Crawford, Gneezy, and Rottenstreich's 2008 experiments studied one-shot choices with salient labels and different payoff structures. Labels supported frequent coordination when matching rewards were equal, but lost much of their power when the two matching outcomes distributed rewards differently—even with small differences. The paid experiments support a limit on label-based focal points; they do not measure this meeting procedure. [Original article](https://econweb.ucsd.edu/~vcrawfor/CrawfordGneezyRottenstreichAER08.pdf)

## Limits and lookalikes

Coordination problems can combine aligned interests with distributional conflict. In [[battle-of-the-sexes|Battle of the Sexes]], players prefer matching to splitting but favor different matching outcomes. In [[the-stag-hunt|Stag Hunt]], a safer action can compete with a better but more vulnerable coordinated outcome. Neither has this example's two equally rewarding matching choices.

Van Huyck, Battalio, and Beil's 1990 repeated minimum-effort experiments illustrate another boundary: larger laboratory groups could converge toward low-performing outcomes despite a mutually better equilibrium. Their results concern that specific weakest-effort production structure, not an inevitable fate for all large teams. [Original study](https://econweb.ucsd.edu/~jandreoni/Econ264/papers/Van%20Huyck%20et%20al%20AER%201990.pdf)

If someone benefits from breaking the agreed plan even when others follow it, selecting a convention is insufficient. If no compatible feasible plan exists, clearer expectations cannot create one. Communication, preferences, capability, and authority need separate diagnosis.

## Related patterns

[[the-stag-hunt|The Stag Hunt]] and [[battle-of-the-sexes|Battle of the Sexes]] are more specific coordination structures. The former adds exposure around a jointly preferred course; the latter adds disagreement over which coordinated outcome to select. The entrance example isolates the simpler case of equally attractive matching plans.

[[the-prisoners-dilemma|The Prisoner's Dilemma]] differs because unilateral defection remains attractive even against cooperation. Here, matching a known choice is individually attractive.

[[cold-starts-and-critical-mass|Cold Starts and Critical Mass]] adds participation-dependent value: enough relevant people must be present before joining becomes worthwhile.

[[threshold-contribution-problems|Threshold Contribution Problems]] requires a sufficient combined commitment. A clear plan can coordinate contributions, while refunds and collectible pledges address additional risks.
