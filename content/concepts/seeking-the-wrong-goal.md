## A recognizable situation

A support service wants to help customers solve problems. Its managers nevertheless judge success mainly by how quickly calls end. A short call improves the report even when the customer must call again about the same unresolved issue.

Nia, the service lead, asks the team to shorten calls further. Staff give briefer explanations and fewer checks before ending a conversation. The call-length figures are accurate; some customers are still unable to do what they called about.

In this hypothetical service, nobody needs to falsify a record or receive an individual bonus. Faithfully pursuing the official objective can move the service away from its purpose.

## The underlying pattern

**Seeking the Wrong Goal occurs when the objective directing a system's decisions differs from the purpose it is supposed to serve.** The memorable formulation is: **success on the measure can be failure for the service.**

Donella Meadows describes this named trap in _Thinking in Systems_ (2008), distinguishing it from Rule Beating: the system can produce exactly the specified result without evading anything. This is a structural account, not a theorem that measurement necessarily causes harm. [Meadows, pp. 138–141](https://www.sustainable.soltechdesigns.com/meadows.pdf)

The ingredients are a purpose, an operational objective, and decisions that respond to that objective. A **proxy** is an observable stand-in for something harder to assess. Trouble arises when improving the proxy can select actions that undermine the purpose, and relevant counterevidence does not change the decision.

## What keeps it going

A goal defines which shortfalls require correction. If calls are longer than the approved target, the procedure favors actions that end them sooner. A balancing feedback loop—action that reduces a measured gap—can therefore work properly around the wrong variable.

Unresolved problems appear in another place or later period: repeat contacts, complaints, abandoned attempts, or users silently giving up. If those consequences do not alter the decision rule, the service can continue celebrating the wrong achievement.

Calls are events. Unresolved cases can accumulate as a stock when newly opened problems exceed genuine resolutions or other explicitly recorded exits. A caller giving up removes a case from an administrative queue only if the process defines that exit; it does not mean the need has been met. Repeat calls are not automatically new independent problems.

The proposed harmful link needs evidence. Some short calls fully resolve simple requests. Call duration is a useful operational measure; it becomes a wrong goal when shortening it takes priority over the authorized purpose and relevant contrary evidence.

The U.S. Government Accountability Office distinguishes activities, outputs, and outcomes, and explains why ongoing measurement and broader evaluation answer different questions. A recorded output does not itself establish a contribution to the intended outcome. [GAO's definitions](https://www.gao.gov/assets/a77278.html)

## Why the obvious responses fail

**Tighten the duration target.** A stricter demand can intensify premature call endings rather than improve resolution.

**Audit the timestamps more closely.** Correct timestamps establish the length of calls. They cannot establish that the service has chosen the right thing to optimize.

**Add a resolution dashboard without changing decisions.** A secondary indicator remains decorative if staffing, scheduling, review, and priorities still depend overwhelmingly on speed.

**Replace one number with another universal score.** Maximizing counted resolutions can encourage easy-case selection or superficial closure. A better goal needs meaningful definitions, constraints, and judgment; no label makes a measure adequate.

## Possible ways out

**Reconnect the objective to an authorized purpose.** The service's governing body, with affected users, must decide what counts as success and whose needs have priority. Disagreement about those values cannot be settled by a more precise instrument.

**Change the decision rule, not just its label.** Let substantive resolution influence call handling, staffing, and review. Retain call duration as a capacity measure rather than the overriding objective. Adding a resolution label changes little if decisions still depend mainly on how quickly calls end.

**Add explicit constraints where aggregation hides harm.** Access for difficult cases, accessibility, safety, and feasible waiting commitments can restrict which call-handling plans are acceptable. Such safeguards may reduce the maximum headline count. If no plan meets them all, the authority must fund capacity, change timing or scope, or acknowledge a lower service level.

**Evaluate the link to outcomes.** Examine linked cases, substantive resolution, unresolved repeat contacts, abandoned calls, and access for difficult cases, alongside user experience and periodic independent review. These observations can test whether premature endings contribute to the problem. Improvement after several simultaneous changes would not isolate that cause. Evaluation costs time, and delayed outcomes or outside influences can complicate attribution. These are practical design choices, not a guaranteed cure.

## A worked example

### Let the purpose govern how calls are handled

Nia first asks for shorter calls. The report improves while repeat contacts about unresolved problems persist. She initially requests more accurate timing, but the times were already correct. The issue is the objective directing decisions.

She reviews a sample of linked cases, customer reports, repeat contacts, and actual completion of the requested tasks. For this hypothetical diagnosis, some shorter calls leave necessary explanation and checking undone. External product faults and genuinely complex cases are considered separately rather than attributed automatically to staff.

The governing manager confirms the purpose: help users achieve the agreed resolution, subject to safety, accessibility, and feasible waiting commitments. The service gives staff permission and time to complete necessary checks, links repeat calls to the same case, and reviews substantive resolution alongside access for difficult cases. Call duration remains a capacity indicator rather than the overriding objective.

Assume staff have the relevant competence, authority, and time at the revised workload. In the stipulated improvement, more sampled users can complete the task and fewer need to repeat the same unresolved request. This is a hypothetical continuation, not a measured change or a guarantee that longer calls are always better.

Some conversations take longer, so fewer new calls may fit within unchanged staffing. Waiting can increase. If genuine resolution and promised access cannot both be maintained, management must add usable capacity, change scope or timing, or acknowledge the lower service level. Changing the score creates no extra staff hours.

The intervention changes the operational goal and decision rule. Better logging alone would not do that. Nor is paying individuals a new bonus necessary: the wrong goal can operate through ordinary management priorities without a separate incentive contract.

## Limits and lookalikes

A limited measure is not inherently a wrong goal. Call duration describes resource use and can help assess access and staffing. It need not be abandoned when it ceases to be the overriding objective. Likewise, Australia's statistical agency explicitly defines GDP as a production measure, not comprehensive economic welfare. Misusing it as an exhaustive welfare objective is different from finding it useful for production analysis. [ABS, section 8.20](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/detailed-methodology-information/concepts-sources-methods/australian-system-national-accounts-concepts-sources-and-methods/edition-8/chapter-8-gross-domestic-product/measures-gdp)

Donald Campbell's 1976 discussion warns that using indicators for consequential decisions can distort the processes they describe. He identifies his supporting evidence as predominantly anecdotal; this is not a universal mathematical law. His account of employment services concentrating on easier placements also illustrates why replacing an activity count with an outcome count can leave exclusion unresolved. [Campbell, pp. 49–52](https://www.humanlearning.systems/uploads/08%20Assessing%20the%20Impact%20of%20Planned%20Social%20Change.pdf)

The support example does not establish that every repeat call reflects a failed previous interaction. New faults, changing needs, and user circumstances matter. A case marked resolved also needs an adequate definition and verification; administrative closure can differ from a useful result. Longer observation may reveal recurrence or exclusion hidden by an early improvement.

## Related patterns

[[rule-beating|Rule Beating]] concerns adaptation around a constraint. Here faithful selection under the stated objective is enough; both mechanisms can coexist.

[[multitask-incentive-distortion|Multitask Incentive Distortion]] explains effort diverted among duties by uneven rewards. A wrong system goal can operate without delegation or individual bonuses.

[[drift-to-low-performance|Drift to Low Performance]] lowers a standard after disappointing results. Here the target could stay fixed and still point toward the wrong achievement.
