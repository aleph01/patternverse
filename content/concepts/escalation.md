## A recognizable situation

Eastland sees its neighbor Westland acquiring more weapons. Its leaders worry that their defenses are falling behind and approve a buildup. Westland interprets that buildup as a threat and orders more weapons of its own.

Each government describes its response as necessary protection. Neither feels durably safer, because the other's response keeps moving the standard for what counts as enough.

This hypothetical arms race illustrates a classic escalation pattern. The question is how defensive responses can become a continuing source of pressure for the other side; it is not a claim that any particular historical conflict had equal responsibility or a simple cause.

## The underlying pattern

**Escalation occurs when one actor's intensification prompts a counter-response that becomes a reason for the first actor to intensify further.** The distinctive feature is a closed response loop, often organized around relative position: being louder, better defended, or harder to ignore than the other side.

The systems archetype belongs to the system dynamics tradition. Daniel H. Kim's 1999 account examines reciprocal action and perception delays; Meadows's _Thinking in Systems_ describes goals set by trying to surpass another actor's state. Neither account makes every increase in activity evidence of escalation. [Kim](https://thesystemsthinker.com/escalation-the-underlying-structure-of-war/), [Meadows, pages 124–126](https://www.sustainable.soltechdesigns.com/meadows.pdf)

The compact formulation is: **my response becomes your provocation; your response becomes mine.**

## What keeps it going

Locally, Eastland is trying to correct a perceived disadvantage. Westland is doing the same. These corrective responses can each look like **balancing feedback**: action intended to close a gap between the position someone wants and the position they perceive.

Together, they form **reinforcing feedback**. More activity on one side raises pressure on the other, whose response feeds pressure back. Each side treats its own action as a response and the other's as an initiative.

Information and action take time. A procurement decision may respond to an earlier perceived threat and become visible only after a production delay. Kim emphasizes how delays and extrapolations can magnify perceived threats. Merely speeding communication does not resolve conflicting goals. [Feedback and delay](https://thesystemsthinker.com/escalation-the-underlying-structure-of-war/)

Escalation can also occur without a deliberate decision to outdo someone. In Shergill and colleagues' 2003 experiment, six pairs instructed to reproduce the force just applied to a finger generated increasing forces. A separate force-matching task implicated differences between self-generated and externally generated sensation. This small laboratory study illustrates a specific perceptual mechanism; it does not establish how governments assess threats or prove that all conflicts follow that mechanism. [Original paper](https://8limbsus.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/03/shergill-2003-two-eyes-for-an-eye.-the-neuroscience-of-force-escalation.pdf)

## Why the obvious responses fail

**Get sufficiently far ahead to end the contest.** A larger response may become the other side's new reference point. Deterrence is one possible outcome; capitulation is not guaranteed by greater intensity.

**Ask everyone to be reasonable.** Both may already regard their own conduct as measured. They need a workable alternative to the perceived cost of restraint.

**Match the other side indefinitely.** Matching preserves dependence on the other's behaviour. Small additions or perception errors can turn it into a ratchet.

**Judge restraint by the next response alone.** Delayed reactions may still arrive after someone changes course. Conversely, silence could reflect preparation rather than accommodation. Timing needs interpretation, not automatic optimism.

## Possible ways out

**Check the threat and the response path.** Distinguish current deployments from announced procurement, identify what each side perceived, and compare decision dates with later deliveries. Simultaneous growth alone does not establish reciprocal causation; independent threats may affect both.

**Use a reference beyond relative superiority.** Define the purposes and limits of a security policy rather than treating every rival increase as an automatic new target. Choosing an adequate standard is contested and needs credible threat assessment; changing words does not remove real danger.

**Negotiate observable reciprocal limits.** An agreement can constrain specified acquisitions or deployments, with shared definitions, verification, and a process for alleged breaches. Those requirements consume resources and depend on actual authority, access, and compliance. A promise by itself does not create them.

**Make bounded restraint possible.** One side can decline to match an increase, potentially interrupting the response path. It may also increase exposure if the other side exploits it. Unilateral restraint, reciprocal reductions, and independently enforced restrictions have different conditions and costs.

Meadows discusses unilateral restraint and negotiated controls as intervention families. Applying them to any real security setting requires much more evidence than this archetype supplies. [Structural responses](https://www.sustainable.soltechdesigns.com/meadows.pdf)

## A worked example

### Replace an open-ended comparison with verified limits

Eastland answers Westland's buildup with new procurement. Westland responds to Eastland's announcement, including equipment not yet delivered. Leaders on each side see the next acquisition as restoring a defensive balance. Appeals to recognize the cost leave that response rule intact.

For the hypothetical diagnosis, records show that each round responds substantially to the other side's perceived increase. Arms already held are an accumulated stock; deliveries add to it and retirement removes from it. Orders are commitments in a pipeline, not equipment already deployed. Production and assessment delays mean today's inventory can reflect earlier decisions.

Suppose both governments have authority to negotiate a reciprocal limit on a defined category, permit credible verification, and agree how outstanding orders, retirements, and breaches will be handled. The agreement covers deliveries already committed as well as new approvals, so simply pausing orders cannot disguise an expanding stock.

In the stipulated compliant outcome, both stop the specified additional procurement and follow the agreed treatment of pending deliveries. The stock need not fall immediately: reductions require actual retirement or removal. Verification and the breach process give each side a way to assess restraint other than matching every announcement.

This changes the response relationship and available commitments. It does not establish that the agreed level is safe, settle every security concern, or guarantee compliance. Monitoring costs resources; some uncertainty remains, and a breach can restart the race.

If access for verification is refused, definitions can be evaded, or another threat independently drives procurement, the proposed agreement may not interrupt the mechanism. A simplified reciprocal map cannot decide an actual government's security obligations or distribute responsibility for harm.

## Limits and lookalikes

The narrative establishes no particular rate of increase. Response strengths, budgets, and delays can produce different paths. Procurement, delivery, and retirement differ: equipment accumulates only when additions exceed removals. Budgets and productive capacity can restrain growth while leaving an expensive stand-off. The diagram explains a hypothesized response loop rather than a quantified arms model.

Mutual feedback does not imply equal power, responsibility, or harm. When restraint increases vulnerability, credible protection or third-party enforcement may need to precede reductions.

Passing an issue to a higher authority is also called escalation; it need not involve reciprocal intensification.

**Escalation of commitment** is a neighboring concept: increasing resources devoted to a chosen course despite adverse feedback, often connected with responsibility for earlier choices. It can occur without a rival's counter-action. Staw's 1976 investment-decision study examines that different mechanism. [Original study](https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/biases/16_Organizational_Behavior_Human_Performance_27_%28Staw%29.pdf)
