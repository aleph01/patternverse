## A recognizable situation

Rosa manages a pasture that has supported an expanding herd. Each successful season encourages another increase in grazing commitments. The field still contains plenty of forage, so the next increase appears affordable.

What the visible supply conceals is that animals are consuming more than grows back each season. Stored forage covers the difference. By the time a shortage becomes unmistakable, grazing has also damaged the pasture's ability to renew itself.

Rosa cuts demand back to the old level. It still exceeds the damaged pasture's regrowth. This hypothetical predicament is more serious than reaching a ceiling: the activity has weakened the support on which its former level depended.

## The underlying pattern

**Overshoot occurs when population or activity exceeds what its supporting system can sustain. Collapse is a subsequent large decline in that population, output, or function as essential support is exhausted or damaged.** Overshoot need not end in collapse; timely adjustment or resource recovery can produce other outcomes.

**Carrying capacity** means the level supportable under specified conditions. It can change with resource condition, technology, consumption requirements, and the time horizon. A full store is not proof of a sustainable replenishment rate. Using a reserve is not itself overshoot: the defining problem is pressure beyond sustainable support, and collapse adds a major subsequent loss of activity or function.

The pattern draws on population–resource ecology and system dynamics. Donella Meadows, Dennis Meadows, Jørgen Randers, and William Behrens III examined it in the 1972 _Limits to Growth_. Their global simulations explored conditional behavior modes, not exact dated forecasts. Meadows later illustrated how renewable-resource models can settle, oscillate, or collapse under different feedback and regeneration assumptions. [Original report](https://collections.dartmouth.edu/archive/text/meadows/diplomatic/meadows_ltg-diplomatic.html), [Meadows, pp. 69–72](https://www.sustainable.soltechdesigns.com/meadows.pdf)

## What keeps it going

Forage reserve is a **stock**: accumulated edible material. Regrowth adds to it; grazing removes it. If removals exceed additions, the difference comes from the reserve. Production can therefore look healthy while its foundation deteriorates.

Growth can reinforce itself: successful production finances or encourages larger commitments. The balancing response—scarcity, lower productivity, or deliberate restraint—may arrive after breeding, purchasing, or contracts have committed future demand.

Degradation can introduce another reinforcing loop: depleted cover weakens renewal, weaker renewal accelerates depletion, and the remaining reserve falls faster. This relationship must be demonstrated in a real case. Rosa’s hypothetical example assumes it explicitly; declining output alone would not prove it.

## Why the obvious responses fail

**Stop growing but maintain current use.** A stable demand can remain above renewal. Ending expansion does not automatically end overshoot.

**Return to the old extraction rate.** That rate may have been supportable before the resource was damaged. It need not be supportable afterward.

**Extract more efficiently.** Lower costs or better equipment can preserve output while enabling deeper depletion. Efficiency helps when it reduces total pressure; increasing extraction efficiency is a different intervention.

**Rely on the remaining buffer.** A larger reserve buys time. It cannot indefinitely finance a persistent deficit, and it may conceal the need to act before recovery becomes harder.

## Possible ways out

**Track renewal and condition alongside output.** Resource managers need stock trends, removal rates, recruitment or regrowth, and evidence about degradation. Project the consequences of commitments already made. Profitable sales or a strong current harvest do not answer those questions.

**Reduce pressure before the vulnerable state is reached.** Managers with authority over access, stocking, or production can limit new commitments and reduce existing demand. Build a margin for uncertain renewal and implementation delays. The costs may include lower income, fewer animals, cancelled plans, or exclusion from a livelihood; allocation and transition support matter.

**Protect or restore the supporting resource.** Rest, habitat repair, and changes in damaging practices may improve recovery prospects. They require relevant expertise, funding, and time. Reducing pressure stops one source of harm; it does not establish how quickly renewal will recover.

**Change requirements when support cannot be restored in time.** A smaller operation, different production method, or alternative resource may be necessary. Imports can help locally while transferring pressure elsewhere. The system boundary must include that cost rather than treating substitution as free capacity.

These are intervention families, not assurances of recovery. Forecasting a threshold more precisely is useful only if decisions can change before commitments or damage make that information ineffective.

## A worked example

### Act on the supporting resource before output makes failure obvious

Rosa judges expansion by the forage she can still supply. Each successful season encourages larger grazing commitments. Meanwhile, reserve cover declines because consumption exceeds renewal. Apparent output is being supported partly by drawing down accumulated material.

In this hypothetical case, continued grazing also damages the pasture’s capacity to regrow. That is an explicit causal assumption, not something a falling reserve alone establishes. Roots, cover, soil condition, weather, and competing explanations would need investigation in a real pasture.

Rosa first cuts grazing back to a formerly sustainable level. That fails because the damaged pasture now renews more slowly. The old allowance describes the previous condition, not the resource she currently has.

The earlier alternative is to monitor reserve condition and renewal while limiting new commitments before damage occurs. Rosa reduces grazing demand and retains protective cover, accepting less short-term production. In the stipulated prevention case, renewal remains intact and forage support avoids the collapse described in the unchecked case.

Reducing demand requires real decisions: a smaller herd, feed from elsewhere, access to another pasture, or lost income. The diagram supplies none of those resources. Imported feed or grazing can also move pressure beyond the boundary rather than eliminate it.

After damage, reducing removals can prevent further deterioration without restoring the former productive capacity quickly. Recovery may require time and additional restoration, and some losses may persist. If no renewal damage occurred, bringing grazing into line with renewal could instead stabilize the reserve without that further recovery problem.

The distinction is the relationship between depletion and future replenishment. Overshoot means exceeding sustainable support; collapse adds a severe contraction. Neither a universal reserve threshold nor inevitable collapse follows from the name.

## Limits and lookalikes

Seasonal reserve use can be deliberate and sustainable if later replenishment restores it. A one-off external disaster, ordinary recession, or temporary overload does not by itself establish overshoot. Nonrenewable depletion can also cause collapse without the particular renewal-damage mechanism illustrated here.

Hutchings and Reynolds's 2004 analysis and review of marine fishes found that recovery after severe declines was often slow and varied among groups, including populations where fishing pressure had fallen. This is observational population evidence, not a controlled test of one damage threshold. The authors discuss continuing exploitation, life history, habitat, and other possible influences; their results do not show that reducing fishing is futile or that collapse is invariably irreversible. [Research and limitations](https://www.cbd.int/doc/articles/2004/A-00162.pdf)

The pasture's rates and trigger are not estimates transferable from fisheries. Collapse can mean a major reduction rather than extinction, and recovery may remain possible on a longer horizon.
