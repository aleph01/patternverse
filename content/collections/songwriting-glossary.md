**Reference collection · Songwriting**

Use the material below for a quick lookup or comparison. The related patterns at the end provide practical routes back into writing.

This glossary uses the course’s conventions and links each distinction to a fuller explanation. Definitions are working musical tools within the course’s stated scope, not rules governing every tradition. For chart shorthand, see [[songwriting-reading-the-examples]].

**Accidental.** A sharp, flat, or natural sign that changes or clarifies a written pitch. A sharp or flat does not necessarily request a black keyboard key. Read [[songwriting-pitch-and-intervals|Intervals and Melodic Shape]].

**Anticipation.** An event placed before its expected musical boundary, such as a voice entering before the next bar or a chord arriving early. Read [[songwriting-syncopation-and-anticipation|Pull an Entrance Across the Beat]].

**Arpeggio.** The notes of a chord played in succession rather than attacked together. Its rhythm and order can create a distinct accompaniment gesture. Read [[songwriting-build-and-hear-chords|Choose Chords by Their Notes]].

**Arrangement.** The organization of musical parts, their roles, entrances, register, texture, and changes across a piece. Read [[songwriting-give-each-part-a-job|Give Each Part a Job]].

**Assonance.** A relationship formed through repeated vowel sounds. It can connect words without an exact end rhyme. Read [[songwriting-rhyme-and-lyric-sound|Let Sound Support the Lyric]].

**Backbeat.** A recurring rhythmic emphasis often placed on beats two and four in common four-beat grooves. Its role depends on the surrounding pattern. Read [[songwriting-interlock-the-groove|Interlock the Groove]].

**Bar or measure.** One written group of musical time. A four-beat 4/4 bar contains four quarter-note beats whether or not every beat has a new attack. Read [[songwriting-beats-bars-and-meter|Group the Beat Around the Phrase]].

**Bass line.** A low musical part that can establish harmony, connect chords, contribute groove, sustain a center, or carry a memorable identity. Read [[songwriting-write-a-bass-line|Write a Bass Line]].

**Beat.** A recurring pulse used as a reference for rhythm. The word can also mean a drum pattern in ordinary production language; context matters. Read [[songwriting-pulse-and-tempo|Set the Pace for the Phrase]].

**Borrowed chord.** A chord drawn from a parallel mode while the larger tonal center remains. Fm in a C-major context is a common introductory example. Read [[songwriting-borrow-a-chord|Borrow a Chord]].

**Bridge.** A contrasting section whose relationship to the surrounding song can change perspective or prepare a meaningful return. It need not last eight bars. Read [[songwriting-earn-the-bridge|Earn the Bridge]].

**Cadence.** An articulated phrase ending involving musical relationships such as harmony, melody, rhythm, and placement. A chord pair alone does not establish every ending. Read [[songwriting-progressions-and-arrival|Decide Where the Harmony Arrives]].

**Chord.** A grouping of pitches heard harmonically. The course begins with triads and later adds sevenths, added notes, and suspended collections. Read [[songwriting-build-and-hear-chords|Choose Chords by Their Notes]].

**Chord tone.** A note belonging to the underlying chord as described. Other melodic notes can still have deliberate and useful roles. Read [[songwriting-melody-and-harmony|Support or Challenge the Melody Note]].

**Chorus.** A recurring focal section, often with repeated words and recognizable music. Loudness and new chords are not universal requirements. Read [[songwriting-make-the-chorus-arrive|Make the Chorus Arrive]].

**Circle of fifths.** A map ordering pitch centers by fifth relationships and organizing familiar key signatures. It supports orientation and transposition rather than choosing songs automatically. Read [[songwriting-circle-of-fifths|Transpose the Song to Fit the Voice]].

**Compound meter.** Meter whose ordinary beat divides into three. Typical 6/8 has two dotted-quarter beats, not six equal larger beats. Read [[songwriting-beats-bars-and-meter|Group the Beat Around the Phrase]].

**Consonance and dissonance.** Descriptions of pitch relationships and their relative stability or friction within a musical context. They are not simple synonyms for good and bad. Read [[songwriting-melody-and-harmony|Support or Challenge the Melody Note]].

**Contour.** The pattern of a melodic line’s rises, falls, and repeated levels. It describes direction without specifying every interval or duration. Read [[songwriting-contour-and-register|Give the Melody a Destination]].

**Countermelody.** A secondary melodic line heard against a primary one. Its placement should account for the lead’s rhythm, register, and focal role. Read [[songwriting-give-each-part-a-job|Give Each Part a Job]].

**Demo.** A clear recording or representation that communicates the essential song for its current purpose. It need not reproduce a professional release. Read [[songwriting-finish-and-learn|Finish and Learn]].

**Diatonic.** Drawn from the notes of the specified scale or key collection. Minor-key music can use variable scale degrees beyond one natural-minor list. Read [[songwriting-chord-families-and-numbers|Think in Chord Numbers]].

**Dominant.** The fifth scale degree, a chord built there, or a harmonic function depending on context. Dominant-seventh quality and dominant function are distinct descriptions. Read [[songwriting-progressions-and-arrival|Decide Where the Harmony Arrives]].

**Drone.** A sustained or recurring pitch reference that can help establish a center beneath changing material. Read [[songwriting-modes-drones-and-key-changes|Change the Tonal World Deliberately]].

**Dynamics.** Differences and changes in performance loudness. They interact with register, density, and rhythm rather than determining energy alone. Read [[songwriting-shape-energy-over-time|Shape Energy over Time]].

**Enharmonic.** Different written names for the same sounding keyboard pitch in this course’s equal temperament, such as F-sharp and G-flat. Read [[songwriting-pitch-and-intervals|Intervals and Melodic Shape]].

**Form.** The large-scale organization of a piece through time: what returns, what contrasts, and how those spans relate. Read [[songwriting-choose-a-song-form|Choose a Form That Serves the Song]].

**Formal model.** A description of musical organization, such as verse–chorus, binary (two main sections), ternary (three main parts with a return), or theme and variations. A reusable technique such as layered entry can work inside several models. Sectional, developmental and process-driven listening are complementary perspectives on that organization. Read [[songwriting-choose-a-song-form|Choose a Form That Serves the Song]].

**Groove.** The combined rhythmic relationship of musical parts, including their attacks, durations, emphasis, and timing around a shared reference. Read [[songwriting-interlock-the-groove|Interlock the Groove]].

**Half step or semitone.** The smallest adjacent pitch step in the course’s twelve-tone keyboard system. E–F and B–C are natural-note examples. Read [[songwriting-pitch-and-intervals|Intervals and Melodic Shape]].

**Harmonic rhythm.** The rate and pattern of chord changes. A familiar progression can behave differently when some chords last longer than others. Read [[songwriting-progressions-and-arrival|Decide Where the Harmony Arrives]].

**Harmony.** The organization and succession of combined pitches, including relationships implied by a melody or bass rather than explicitly played block chords. Read [[songwriting-build-and-hear-chords|Choose Chords by Their Notes]].

**Hook.** A distinctive musical, rhythmic, lyrical, or sonic feature that gives attention and memory something to return to. Read [[songwriting-write-a-hook|Write a Hook]].

**Interval.** The distance between pitches, named through letter distance and quality or measured in semitones. C–E is a major third. Read [[songwriting-pitch-and-intervals|Intervals and Melodic Shape]].

**Inversion.** The arrangement in which a chord member other than its root is the lowest sounding chord note. C/E places E beneath a C chord. Read [[songwriting-inversions-and-voice-leading|Connect Chords with Small Moves]].

**Key.** A tonal organization around a center, commonly major or minor in this course. A note inventory alone may not settle which center is heard. Read [[songwriting-scales-and-tonal-home|Establish a Tonal Home]].

**Key signature.** A notated set of recurring sharps or flats associated with a key or pitch collection. It does not prohibit later accidentals. Read [[songwriting-circle-of-fifths|Transpose the Song to Fit the Voice]].

**Leading tone.** A pitch one semitone below a local tonic that can point toward it in a tonal context, such as G-sharp approaching A. Read [[songwriting-point-to-a-temporary-home|Point to a Temporary Home]].

**Melisma.** Several pitches sung on one syllable. A melody’s note count therefore need not equal its lyric’s syllable count. Read [[songwriting-words-against-music|Make Speech and Melody Agree]].

**Meter.** Recurring organization of beats and their divisions into larger groups. It is distinct from tempo and from one surface rhythm. Read [[songwriting-beats-bars-and-meter|Group the Beat Around the Phrase]].

**Modal mixture.** Borrowing notes or chords from a parallel major/minor mode while retaining the wider center. Read [[songwriting-borrow-a-chord|Borrow a Chord]].

**Mode.** A pitch organization described relative to a center, such as D Dorian. Merely beginning a scale on a note does not establish that note as home. Read [[songwriting-modes-drones-and-key-changes|Change the Tonal World Deliberately]].

**Modulation.** An established change of tonal center within a piece. Its strength depends on phrasing and context, not one compulsory duration. Read [[songwriting-modes-drones-and-key-changes|Change the Tonal World Deliberately]].

**Motif.** A compact recognizable musical identity that can be repeated or developed. Rhythm, contour, and sound can contribute alongside exact pitches. Read [[songwriting-start-with-a-motif|Start with a Motif]].

**Non-chord tone.** A note outside the underlying chord as currently named. Its timing, preparation, duration, and continuation can explain its musical role. Read [[songwriting-melody-and-harmony|Support or Challenge the Melody Note]].

**Octave.** The interval between a pitch and the next pitch with the same note name above or below it; twelve semitones in this tuning. Read [[songwriting-pitch-and-intervals|Intervals and Melodic Shape]].

**Parallel major/minor.** Major and minor keys or modes sharing a tonic, such as C major and C minor. This differs from a relative pair. Read [[songwriting-borrow-a-chord|Borrow a Chord]].

**Passing note.** A note connecting other notes through a line’s motion, often stepwise. Its role depends on duration, accent, and context. Read [[songwriting-melody-and-harmony|Support or Challenge the Melody Note]].

**Pedal point.** A sustained or recurring pitch continuing beneath changing harmony. Each combined sonority still needs to be heard in context. Read [[songwriting-write-a-bass-line|Write a Bass Line]].

**Phrase.** A coherent musical span with a particular shape and boundary. It can cross bar lines and need not match every accompaniment boundary. Read [[songwriting-phrases-and-breathing|Let the Phrase Breathe]].

**Pickup.** One or more notes leading into a stronger position or the beginning of a complete phrase or bar. Read [[songwriting-syncopation-and-anticipation|Pull an Entrance Across the Beat]].

**Pitch class.** A pitch identity considered without octave placement, such as all C notes. Doubling a chord member in another octave does not add a new pitch class. Read [[songwriting-build-and-hear-chords|Choose Chords by Their Notes]].

**Pre-chorus.** A section that links verse to chorus and helps establish an approaching focal section. It is useful only when that preparation earns its time. Read [[songwriting-jobs-of-song-sections|Give Each Section a Purpose]].

**Prosody.** The relationship between language’s rhythm and emphasis and its musical setting; broader songwriting usage can concern shared expressive intention. Read [[songwriting-words-against-music|Make Speech and Melody Agree]].

**Refrain.** A recurring line or passage that can sit within a larger verse rather than form a separate full chorus. Read [[songwriting-jobs-of-song-sections|Give Each Section a Purpose]].

**Register.** The pitch region in which material is placed. Moving a whole melody by an octave changes register while preserving its intervals. Read [[songwriting-contour-and-register|Give the Melody a Destination]].

**Relative major/minor.** A pair sharing a key signature, such as C major and A minor, while organizing their material around different tonics. Read [[songwriting-scales-and-tonal-home|Establish a Tonal Home]].

**Rest.** Counted musical time with no new sounded event in the part. Silence does not stop the beat count. Read [[songwriting-subdivision-and-rests|Make Space Part of the Rhythm]].

**Rhythm.** The pattern of events and durations through time, including sounding notes and rests. Read [[songwriting-subdivision-and-rests|Make Space Part of the Rhythm]].

**Roman numeral.** A symbol describing a chord’s relationship to a key. I–vi–IV–V in C means C–Am–F–G, without specifying duration or voicing. Read [[songwriting-chord-families-and-numbers|Think in Chord Numbers]].

**Root.** The pitch from which a chord is named and constructed. It remains the root when another chord tone is in the bass. Read [[songwriting-build-and-hear-chords|Choose Chords by Their Notes]].

**Scale.** An ordered pitch collection. Its notes and the center heard through emphasis and phrasing are related but different concepts. Read [[songwriting-build-a-scale|Build a Scale from Steps]].

**Secondary dominant.** The dominant of a temporary destination chord, such as E7 approaching Am inside a C-centered song. Read [[songwriting-point-to-a-temporary-home|Point to a Temporary Home]].

**Sequence.** A related musical idea repeated at another pitch level. Exact interval transposition and movement within a scale can produce different notes. Read [[songwriting-develop-without-losing-the-idea|Develop Without Losing the Idea]].

**Simple meter.** Meter whose ordinary beat divides into two. Common 3/4 and 4/4 are examples. Read [[songwriting-beats-bars-and-meter|Group the Beat Around the Phrase]].

**Strophic form.** A form using essentially recurring music for successive stanzas. New words and performance can carry the development. Read [[songwriting-choose-a-song-form|Choose a Form That Serves the Song]].

**Subdivision.** Smaller reference units within a beat. Two eighth notes or four sixteenths can fill one quarter-note beat in the relevant context. Read [[songwriting-subdivision-and-rests|Make Space Part of the Rhythm]].

**Suspension.** A prepared note held into a new harmony and then resolved in a voice-leading account. A sus chord symbol names a related collection without requiring that full event. Read [[songwriting-melody-and-harmony|Support or Challenge the Melody Note]].

**Swing.** Unequal spacing within paired subdivisions. Its ratio varies; it is distinct from shifting an entire part ahead of or behind the beat. Read [[songwriting-swing-and-timing|Give the Groove a Consistent Feel]].

**Syncopation.** A departure from an established pattern of metrical emphasis, including offbeat accents and durations that cross expected strong positions. Read [[songwriting-syncopation-and-anticipation|Pull an Entrance Across the Beat]].

**Tempo.** The rate of the specified beat, often given in beats per minute. A number should identify its beat unit when the meter makes that ambiguous. Read [[songwriting-pulse-and-tempo|Set the Pace for the Phrase]].

**Tessitura.** The region where a melody spends much of its time, distinct from merely its highest and lowest possible notes. Read [[songwriting-contour-and-register|Give the Melody a Destination]].

**Texture.** The combined arrangement of musical layers, including density, roles, and relationships between lines. Read [[songwriting-give-each-part-a-job|Give Each Part a Job]].

**Through-composed.** A form developing substantial new music as it proceeds rather than relying on a fixed recurring chorus. It can still contain recurring details. Read [[songwriting-choose-a-song-form|Choose a Form That Serves the Song]].

**Timbre.** The character of a sound that helps distinguish voices and instruments even when pitch and loudness are similar. Read [[songwriting-choose-a-sonic-setting|Choose the World Around the Song]].

**Tonal center or tonic.** The pitch heard as a principal point of orientation or home in a passage. Its establishment depends on musical evidence. Read [[songwriting-scales-and-tonal-home|Establish a Tonal Home]].

**Tonicization.** Temporary emphasis of a non-tonic destination without necessarily establishing a lasting new key. Read [[songwriting-point-to-a-temporary-home|Point to a Temporary Home]].

**Transposition.** Moving all relevant pitches by the same interval while retaining their relationships, such as moving the whole song from C to D. Read [[songwriting-circle-of-fifths|Transpose the Song to Fit the Voice]].

**Triad.** Three distinct pitch classes arranged as a stack of thirds: root, third, and fifth. Repeated octave doublings do not change its basic identity. Read [[songwriting-build-and-hear-chords|Choose Chords by Their Notes]].

**Verse.** A recurring section commonly carrying changing words and new information within a familiar musical frame. Read [[songwriting-make-the-second-verse-count|Make the Second Verse Count]].

**Voice leading.** The movement of individual lines from one harmony to another, including held common tones and stepwise or leaping connections. Read [[songwriting-inversions-and-voice-leading|Connect Chords with Small Moves]].

**Voicing.** The spacing, doubling, and arrangement of chord notes across registers. It differs from the chord’s root and from which member is in the bass. Read [[songwriting-inversions-and-voice-leading|Connect Chords with Small Moves]].

**Whole step or tone.** A distance of two semitones in the course’s tuning, such as C to D or E to F-sharp. Read [[songwriting-pitch-and-intervals|Intervals and Melodic Shape]].
