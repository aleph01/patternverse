**Reference collection · Songwriting**

Use the material below for a quick lookup or comparison. The related patterns at the end provide practical routes back into writing.

The course's musical examples use plain note names and counted durations. This page explains the conventions so that a reader can reconstruct a phrase on a keyboard, another instrument, or a simple music editor without first learning a complete notation system.

## Pitches and octaves

C4 means middle C in this course. D4 is the D above it; C5 is the next C above C4. Some software labels octaves differently, so confirm the actual pitch when moving an example between programs. A4 is the familiar tuning-reference A above middle C.

A sharp raises a named note by one semitone; a flat lowers it by one. F-sharp and G-flat refer to the same keyboard pitch in twelve-tone equal temperament, though their written harmonic roles can differ. E–F and B–C are adjacent semitone pairs without an intervening black key.

See [[songwriting-pitch-and-intervals]] and the [[songwriting-glossary|glossary]] for interval names.

## Durations and rests

Unless an example explicitly says otherwise, a parenthesized number is a duration measured in quarter-note beats: E4(1) lasts one beat, A4(2) lasts two, and rest(1) occupies one beat without a new sound. Four beats fill a 4/4 bar.

`E4(1), G4(1), A4(2)` therefore lasts exactly one bar in 4/4. Two notes of duration 0.5 divide a beat equally. A dash in an attack grid means no new attack; it does not necessarily mean a sustained sound has ended. A duration table states the note endings explicitly.

A tie continues one pitch without a new attack. A dot adds half of a written note's value: a dotted quarter note lasts 1.5 quarter-note beats. In typical 6/8, the larger beat is a dotted quarter; an example using that meter names its tempo unit so “84 BPM” is not ambiguous.

## Chords and bars

C means C-major triad, C–E–G. Am means A minor, A–C–E. A vertical bar separates measures. In `C | C | Am | Am`, each chord occupies a full bar. In `F(2), G(2)`, the two chords share a four-beat bar, two beats each.

C/E means a C chord with E in the bass. C7 includes B-flat; Cmaj7 includes B natural. Cadd9 adds D without automatically requesting a seventh. Csus4 replaces the third E with F in the basic suspended collection. [[songwriting-sevenths-added-notes-suspensions]] explains the fuller conventions.

Chord charts identify the accompaniment's requested harmony. A singer can use passing notes and sustained colors beyond that triad. Those notes still deserve to be heard against the accompaniment; a short chart is not a rule that every melody note must be a chord tone.

## Notes, scales, keys, and chords

A note names one pitch; an interval measures a distance. A scale arranges a collection of notes, while a tonal center is the pitch heard as home. A chord combines notes: C can name one pitch, but C on a chord chart requests the C-major chord, C–E–G. For a guided introduction, follow [[songwriting-part-3|the five foundation lessons]]. [[songwriting-build-a-scale|Build a Scale from Steps]] isolates the construction of the note palette.

## Relationship numbers

Roman numerals describe chord roots relative to a key. In C major, I–vi–IV–V means C–Am–F–G. Uppercase indicates major and lowercase minor; a diminished quality is labeled explicitly. These numerals do not state chord duration or voicing.

Scale-degree numbers describe single notes rather than chords. Duration numbers describe time. The examples label each usage rather than asking one number to mean all three. The slash in V/vi means “dominant of vi,” which differs from the bass-note slash in C/E.

## Standard notation as an optional second view

A staff locates pitches vertically and places events from left to right in time. A clef establishes the pitch reference; a key signature records recurring accidentals; a time signature identifies the written metric framework. Note shapes and rests express duration, while bar lines group time. Reading those symbols can become useful, but the course's complete duration tables remain available without them.

The diagrams are explanatory views rather than full engraved scores. A contour drawing preserves direction, not necessarily exact interval size. An arrangement curve summarizes planned activity, not measured listener emotion. Captions and surrounding prose explain what each drawing represents.

## Base versions and alternatives

The [[songwriting-example-songbook|songbook]] is the reference for Porch Light and Platform Three. A pattern may shorten a progression, transpose the song, or change one chord to illustrate a relationship. Such versions are named as sketches or alternatives. They do not silently replace the base song.
