**Archived course draft · 30 October 2026.** This version has been set aside and is retained for reference. A new course is being designed from a fresh starting point.

A song grows through decisions: what stays recognizable, where a phrase breathes, which note a chord supports, what the next verse reveals, and when the work is finished. This collection offers 49 lessons on reusable songwriting patterns and the musical foundations behind them, arranged in a suggested sequence from a first seed to a complete song.

Use it as a field guide while writing, or follow the eight parts as a course. You do not need to read every article or learn all the theory first. Each pattern names a situation, offers a useful move, works through original musical examples, and explains its limits. Technical explanations sit beside the decision they support; the reference collections supply a refresher when needed.

This is the unpublished review edition. The piano-roll player lets you hear selected original examples, follow their notes, compare melody with chords, and choose piano, guitar, or bass guitar sounds for each part. **Build a chord progression** lets you arrange Roman numerals, change chord durations, transpose, and hear the result. Your sketch is saved on this device. [[songwriting-chord-families-and-numbers|Think in Chord Numbers]] connects the editor to the writing decision. This edition has no interactive practices.

## Find the idea at the right scale

A **concept** explains a relationship: an interval, a chord inversion, syncopation, or a musical section. A **pattern** offers a reusable response to a writing situation: develop a motif, introduce a layer, or prepare a return. A **lesson** guides you from the necessary concept to a pattern and an example. An **example** is a particular passage you can inspect or hear. A **practice** gives you a separate opportunity to apply the idea; this review edition currently offers listening comparisons and transfer prompts.

The same technique can act at several scales. Repetition can connect two figures, phrases, sections, or a whole piece. Adding a bass to one phrase changes its texture; reserving that entrance until late in a piece can help shape its form. Hear the local change first, then ask what its placement contributes over time.

## Build your musical foundations

If notes, scales, keys, and chords are new, enter at [[songwriting-part-3|Part 3: Notes, Keys, and Chords]]. Its first five lessons introduce one relationship at a time. Each has a worked comparison; the first four have short playable examples, and the fifth connects them to the chord editor.

1. [[songwriting-pitch-and-intervals|Intervals and Melodic Shape]] — Hear and count the distance between two notes.
2. [[songwriting-build-a-scale|Build a Scale from Steps]] — Use whole and half steps to construct a note palette.
3. [[songwriting-scales-and-tonal-home|Establish a Tonal Home]] — Hear which note feels like a point of rest.
4. [[songwriting-build-and-hear-chords|Choose Chords by Their Notes]] — Hear scale notes separately, then together as a chord.
5. [[songwriting-chord-families-and-numbers|Think in Chord Numbers]] — Connect those chords to a key and distinguish note degrees from chord numbers.

Continue to the circle of fifths and harmonic arrival after those relationships make sense. Modes, borrowing, and altered minor harmony return in Part 6. This is a suggested entry route; an experienced writer can still begin with a particular songwriting problem.

## Follow the eight-part route

1. [[songwriting-part-1|Start Making Songs]] — Identify and capture an idea, hear how repetition works, and finish a miniature.
2. [[songwriting-part-2|Rhythm and Groove]] — Choose pace and grouping, make space, shape entrances, and coordinate the parts.
3. [[songwriting-part-3|Notes, Keys, and Chords]] — Connect pitch distances, scales, tonal home, and chord families, then transpose and shape harmonic arrival.
4. [[songwriting-part-4|Melody and Hooks]] — Develop a motif, aim its contour, relate it to harmony, give it breath, and protect a hook.
5. [[songwriting-part-5|Lyrics and Meaning]] — Find a situation and speaker, choose images and sound, fit words to music, and develop the thought.
6. [[songwriting-part-6|Bass Movement and Harmonic Color]] — Connect voices, direct the bass, and choose extensions, borrowing, temporary emphasis, or a new tonal world.
7. [[songwriting-part-7|The Whole Song]] — Give sections their jobs, make returns matter, choose a form, and shape the approach and ending.
8. [[songwriting-part-8|Arrange, Revise, and Finish]] — Choose a sound world, assign instrumental roles, shape energy and delivery, and finish a clear version.

For a first complete song, start with Part I, then take the rhythm, melody, and lyric patterns your material needs. Continue to Parts VII and VIII for the whole form and demo. Part VI is optional depth: a song does not need chromatic harmony before it deserves an ending.

If you already write songs, enter through the problem table or a particular pattern. Earlier links are useful refreshers, not admission requirements. The ordering groups related decisions and gradually expands the scale of the work; it does not prescribe the order in which inspiration must happen.

## Follow two original songs

**Porch Light** begins with a three-note seed and develops into a vocal song about welcome and responsibility. Its canonical setting is C major, 4/4, at 84 quarter notes per minute. The lyric, chord chart, counted melody, 52-bar form, and labeled alternatives are preserved in the [[songwriting-example-songbook|original songbook]]. The same material returns when a different writing decision needs examination.

**Platform Three** is a D-centered instrumental sketch that develops through bass timing, upper figures, texture, and register. It offers an alternative to treating every song as a sequence of vocal verses and choruses. Neither example imitates a named artist's melody or words.

A teaching comparison may change the key, a chord, or a phrase ending. Those versions are explicitly labeled so a local comparison does not silently replace the canonical song.

## Keep references beside the patterns

- [[songwriting-reading-the-examples|Reading the examples]] explains the course's pitch names, duration numbers, chord symbols, and Roman numerals.
- [[songwriting-example-songbook|Original songbook]] holds the complete examples in one place.
- [[songwriting-glossary|Glossary]] supplies concise definitions with links back to practical uses.
- [[songwriting-listening-guide|Listening guide]] connects selected patterns to verified official artist sources and primary educational references.
- [[songwriting-troubleshooting|Troubleshooting]] starts from a draft's symptoms and points to useful combinations of patterns.

These are reference collections, rather than songwriting patterns in their own right. Listening studies offer bounded interpretations of audible relationships; they do not claim to reveal an artist's intention. Commercial lyrics, scores, and recordings are not reproduced here.

The main scope is English-language pop, rock, folk, and electronic songwriting using twelve-tone equal temperament. Regular meter, functional harmony, rhyme, and separate choruses are available choices within that scope, with modal, groove-based, and less sectional alternatives included. The examples explain particular decisions rather than impose one definition of a successful song.
