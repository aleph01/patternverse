Good decisions do not require perfect certainty. They require a useful question, attention to consequences and preferences, and a way to learn what matters.

The original decision patterns cover different parts of that work: reversibility, framing, failure modes, experiments, trade-offs, learning over time, uncertain outcomes, and the value of information. The collection is a repertoire rather than a required sequence. Choose the move that fits the decision in front of you.

Many articles include practice instructions or a linked offline guided worksheet. Earlier responses remain visible as the steps unfold. Answers stay in the current view unless you choose Save to workspace; saved responses remain on this device. Keep any decision-journal snapshot you want to revisit. The worksheets do not make decisions for you, calculate authoritative scores, or create reminders.

## Inspect reasoning and choose an approach

[[six-thinking-hats]] separates thinking modes; [[ladder-of-inference]] examines selected observations and conclusions. [[hard-choice-model]] helps choose a proportionate decision process, and [[cynefin-framework]] examines the fit between context and response.

[[decision-matrix]] makes weighted comparisons and their sensitivity visible. [[second-order-thinking]] traces later consequences; [[inversion]] derives safeguards from unwanted outcomes. [[ooda-loop]] links orientation, action, and actual review, while [[match-investment-to-evidence]] separates problem evidence from solution evidence before increasing a commitment.


[[let-ideas-travel-before-you-judge-them]] guides physical circulation and independent ratings of proposals, followed by attention to what the scores leave uncertain. Its results guide inquiry, rather than establish objective quality or decision authority.

[[obliquity]] explores broad purposes that develop through meaningful activity and experience. Its optional reflection, [[explore-an-oblique-route]], helps identify an activity worth trying while keeping direct requirements and later review visible.

[[satisficing]] gives a search an acceptance standard and a stopping point. [[set-a-good-enough-stopping-rule]] helps distinguish requirements from preferences and identify when to accept, investigate further, or revise the search.

[[build-a-web-of-goals]] uses a visual map and everyday examples to connect recurring activities through shared purposes, inspect their costs, and adapt when an expected benefit fails.

[[five-minute-technique]] makes starting a bounded trial rather than an implicit agreement to finish. [[try-a-five-minute-start]] includes a timer and a choice to continue, stop, or adjust.
