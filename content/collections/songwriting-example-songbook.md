**Reference collection · Songwriting**

Use the material below for a quick lookup or comparison. The related patterns at the end provide practical routes back into writing.

The detailed examples in this course are original teaching compositions. They are not transcriptions or imitations of the linked commercial songs. This songbook preserves their base versions and labels alternatives so that a later example can change one feature without changing the reference silently.

Read [[songwriting-reading-the-examples]] for note and duration conventions. Standard notation is optional; the tables below account for the musical time directly.

## Porch Light: the core idea

**Setting:** C major; 4/4; quarter note = 84. The initial instrumental seed is E4(1), G4(1), A4(2). Its rising shape becomes a recurring connection rather than the literal melody of every sung line.

**Situation:** A person returns late to a shared home. The first verse notices familiar objects; the second reveals a message about the cost of waiting. The bridge admits that welcome cannot perform the speaker's responsibility. The final chorus keeps its words but is heard after that turn.

### Complete lyric

**Verse 1**

> Your blue coat hangs beside the door.  
> My key turns slowly in the lock.  
> The hall is smaller than before.  
> I wait beneath the kitchen clock.

**Chorus**

> Leave the porch light on.  
> I can find the road.  
> You don't have to wait awake.  
> I can make it home.

**Verse 2**

> Your note is folded by the phone.  
> The kettle cools beside the chair.  
> You wrote, “I've waited here alone.”  
> I read it twice and leave it there.

**Chorus** repeats.

**Bridge**

> I thought a light could do my part,  
> And keep the words I wouldn't say.  
> But you have waited through the dark.  
> I'll carry more than words today.

**Final chorus** repeats the chorus's words. The changed context and a more direct delivery carry the turn; rewriting the last line is a separate alternative, not part of this reference version.

### Verse melody

The following eight bars can carry either verse. Each listed note lasts one quarter-note beat. The lines contain eight syllables in the intended reading, one per note; a performer can adjust local articulation while preserving the counted span. “Folded,” “kettle,” and “waited” each have two syllables.

The accompaniment does not have to attack on every sung syllable. The verse's nearly continuous quarter-note delivery is a deliberately plain base version. The phrasing patterns show where rests and revised durations can improve a particular performance.

A singer can take a short breath by releasing a sustained ending slightly early while the bar's count continues. A version with written rests should record their exact durations.

### Bridge melody

Each bar contains eight equal eighth notes, each lasting half a quarter-note beat. The eight syllables of its lyric line fit those eight events. “Wouldn't” uses two syllables, and “carry” uses two in this setting. The bridge is a compact contrast to the longer chorus notes.

### Intro, outro, and arrangement

The four-bar intro uses the motif-development sketch from pattern 20: seed, held G and rest, seed again, descending answer. The outro uses F | G | C | C; it recalls the seed, then lets a sustained C close the final bar. The instrumental seed may be adjusted there so the final pitch actually reaches C rather than simply repeating its open A ending indefinitely.

An initial arrangement uses one chord instrument and the seed, adds a sparse bass under the verse, introduces a clearer groove for the chorus, reduces the bridge, and adds one backing voice late in the final chorus. Those layers are optional compositional choices. The song's words and essential musical relationships remain legible in a simple voice-and-accompaniment demo.

### Labeled alternatives

**The sixteen-bar miniature:** Four four-bar passages use C | Am | F | G; the same again; F | G | Am | G; then C | Am | F(2), G(2) | C. This lasts about 46 seconds. It is the early instrumental study, not a shortened performance of the complete lyric.

**Transposition to D:** The verse becomes D | D | Bm | Bm | G | G | A | A. Every melody note moves up two semitones; the seed becomes F-sharp4–A4–B4. The singer's comfort determines whether that move is useful.

**Borrowed-chord ending:** An alternative eight-bar phrase gives C two bars, Am two, F one, Fm one, and C two. Check any held A against the Fm chord's A-flat. This changes both harmony and schedule relative to the base verse.

**Secondary-dominant approach:** C | E7 | Am | Am | F | F | G | G preserves the verse length and gives Am a local approach. Check any G natural against E7's G-sharp.

**No separate chorus:** A verse–refrain version appends a two-bar title line to each eight-bar verse. Its ten-bar units need revised connections and do not inherit the full song's 52-bar map automatically.

## Platform Three: a contrasting instrumental sketch

**Setting:** D Dorian, D–E–F–G–A–B–C; 4/4; quarter note = 104. This piece develops through a persistent center, interlocking rhythms, and texture rather than a narrative lyric or a separate vocal chorus.

### One-bar low pattern

| Start, measured from bar beginning | Event | Duration in beats |
| ---------------------------------- | ----- | ----------------- |
| 0                                  | D2    | 1                 |
| 1                                  | Rest  | 1.5               |
| 2.5, the “and” of beat 3           | A2    | 0.5               |
| 3                                  | Rest  | 1                 |

This accounts for four beats. A recurring quiet subdivision or drum reference can keep the empty positions legible. A kick can meet the first D while another attack occurs on beat 3; those drum events do not require the bass to duplicate them.

### Upper figures

The first two-bar figure uses E4(0.5) on the “and” of beat 2 and F4(1) on beat 4 in bar one; the rest of that bar is silent. Bar two uses A4(1), G4(1), E4(1), and rest(1). The bass remains the tonal anchor rather than letting this small high phrase alone determine home.

A later two-bar answer uses B4(1), A4(1), G4(1), rest(1), followed by F4(1), E4(1), D4(2). B natural makes the Dorian palette explicit. Replacing it with B-flat is a D-natural-minor comparison and should be labeled as such.

### Complete 32-bar form

| Bars  | Material                                               | Development                                            |
| ----- | ------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| 1–8   | Bass pattern and sparse time reference                 | Establish center and rhythmic identity                 |
| 9–16  | Add the first upper figure, then its answer            | Introduce register and modal color                     |
| 17–24 | Remove bass for four bars, then sustain D              | Expose the upper material and change its support       |
| 25–32 | Return the bass; move its A to beat 4 in selected bars | Restore the reference with a selective rhythmic change |

At the stated tempo, 32 bars last approximately **1 minute 14 seconds**, before any free ending. The final two bars reduce upper activity and finish on D. An open-ended loop is a possible alternative, but this version chooses a definite close.

The higher figures need not play continuously throughout their spans. Their entrances and gaps are the arrangement. Added chords such as G over the D reference remain optional and must be checked as combined sonorities rather than assumed neutral background.
