## Fixed article structure

Use these exact level-two headings, in this order, for every ML article:

1. **Purpose and problem** — identify the purpose and open with a recognizable situation.
2. **Core idea** — state the reusable design response and the central mental model.
3. **How it works** — explain the mechanism; use level-three headings for topic-specific or advanced details.
4. **Worked examples** — develop two or three concrete contexts as useful, with the main illustration and any optional explorer inside this section. Distinguish fictional cases from documented implementations.
5. **Assumptions for correctness** — make the necessary input, model, information, and operating conditions explicit.
6. **When to use it** — describe useful situations and how to recognize when another approach may fit better.
7. **Tradeoffs and operating costs** — explain what the pattern gains, gives up, and requires to maintain.
8. **Common mistakes and limitations** — cover misapplication, broken assumptions, and what the pattern does not establish.
9. **Related patterns** — explain the relationship to neighboring concepts.
10. **Sources and further reading** — cite authoritative references and distinguish illustrative data from evidence.

This adopts the Data Engineering articles' shared explanatory progression with fixed visible headings for the ML guide. Topic-specific headings belong at level three. Keep prose connected and example-led within this structure. Keep recommendation metadata in the package JSON, and omit guided practices. Interaction is optional and does not require a separate top-level section or placeholder in static-only articles.

The ordered heading contract is recorded in `machine-learning.json` and checked against every article in that package by the focused content tests.

## Editorial standard

Write for a reader who knows basic ML but is meeting this pattern for the first time. Preserve the ten sections, factual scope, source provenance, and meaningful limitations. Keep recommendation metadata separate and omit guided practices.

- Establish the problem, then one mechanism, then its consequence. Introduce alternatives after the reader has followed the main path.
- Give symbols a concrete role before combining them. Walk through one numerical calculation; retain complete derivations and implementation settings in optional explanations where useful.
- Distinguish learning a representation, fitting a task model, and evaluating the result. A teaching simulator should illustrate a stated part of that process without standing in for the entire pattern.
- Give each section a job: assumptions state conditions; use cases support selection; tradeoffs describe costs and competing objectives; limitations explain failures and the boundary of the claim.
- Put each qualification next to the claim it limits. Do not repeat the same warning in prose, caption, preview, and result. Preserve the distinction between invented examples, computed teaching results, and documented implementations.
- Captions tell readers what to notice and why it matters. Keep exact source values and calculations available, while avoiding unnecessary trailing zeros in explanatory drawings.
- Use level-three headings for examples and optional mathematical detail. Link neighboring articles that are already integrated.
- Explorers use ordinary task language, identify what each control changes, and retain reset controls and explicit assumptions. Separate primary results from inspection tables. ML examples expand with their content so the article remains the scrolling surface on phones; the sandbox and permission boundary remain unchanged.

## Behavioral Testing

Behavioral Testing adds an original article of approximately 1,400 words, separate recommendation metadata, desktop/phone SVG illustrations, and an executable seven-case sentiment suite. It distinguishes known-label assertions, score invariance, and non-increasing score relations. Worked contexts cover simple negation, irrelevant name attribution, and image transformations. Fictional scores and rules are separated from documented CheckList and DeepTest implementations. Additional primary sources are the ML Test Score rubric and Google's Rules of ML.
