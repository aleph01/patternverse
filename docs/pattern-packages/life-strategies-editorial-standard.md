# Life Strategies editorial standard

Life Strategies gathers ways to discover what matters, pursue it, and respond to the difficulties along the way. Its shared question is: **What could I change about my approach that would make this situation more workable?**

Each article develops a recognizable difficulty, a strategic shift, and concrete examples of what changes. Use the outline below as shared coverage, allowing headings and order to follow the material. Aim for a substantial field-guide article, normally about 800–1,500 words, with examples earning their space.

## Common article outline

### 1. The situation

Open with an everyday scene that makes the difficulty recognizable. Show what someone would ordinarily try and where that response gets them stuck.

### 2. The strategic shift

Explain the pattern's central move in plain language. Include one memorable sentence that captures it.

For the Five-Minute Technique: _Make a small commitment before deciding whether to make a larger one._

### 3. Why it can help

Explain what changes in attention, expectations, incentives, behavior, or relationships between goals. Distinguish research findings from plausible practical explanations. Keep the explanation specific to the strategy.

### 4. See it in action

Make examples and explanatory visuals central to the teaching. Develop two or three examples across suitable contexts. Include different outcomes: the strategy helps, reveals another problem, or suggests stopping. Show what the person actually does, what changes, and what remains difficult.

Where useful, add a diagram showing the relationship being changed: a sequence, feedback loop, branching choice, or web of goals. Explain how to read it. Build a Web of Goals depends on its diagrams and worked examples; preserve both when refining the article.

Make an explicit visual decision for each article. A diagram should explain a relationship or process. An urge need not follow a predictable curve, and a five-minute attempt need not lead to continued work. Avoid visuals that imply those outcomes are guaranteed.

### 5. When to reach for it

Describe the signals that make the strategy relevant. Help readers recognize the situation in their own lives and understand what must be in place for the strategy to help.

### 6. Where it goes wrong

Address common misunderstandings, overuse, tradeoffs, and limits. Explain what the pattern cannot resolve and when another approach is needed. Keep a qualification beside the claim it affects.

For Urge Surfing, this includes turning observation into a demand that the urge disappear. For the Five-Minute Technique, it includes making the short trial a concealed obligation to finish.

### 7. Connections

Connect the strategy to two or three neighboring patterns. Explain whether they complement it, address a different obstacle, or offer an alternative. Link to articles that exist; keep prospective names as plain text.

### 8. Sources and lineage

Identify where the idea comes from, acknowledge adaptations, and state the strength and limits of supporting evidence. Evidence for a broader program does not automatically establish the effectiveness of one exercise. Identify fictional scenarios as examples rather than reported outcomes.

## Optional companion practice

Keep the practice separate from the article. Offer one when it lets someone experience or apply the strategic shift, observe what happens, and choose a next step. The practice should be self-contained and allow stopping or adjusting. Rest, support, or reconsidering a demand can be valid responses.

Use the existing Pattern Language renderer for guided practices. Keep explanation and substantial examples in the article rather than turning the article into a worksheet.

## Repository conventions

The canonical collection is `content/collections/life-strategies.md`. It contains Barbell Strategy, Prefer the Smallest Useful Experiment, Obliquity, Build a Web of Goals, Satisficing, Five-Minute Technique, and Urge Surfing. Prefer the Smallest Useful Experiment also carries the plain-language alias Try Before You Commit; reuse that article for limited experiences of a possible life change. Preserve additional collection memberships when relevant; membership in Life Strategies does not require a new verse or replacement of existing subject context.

Follow the existing entity schema and keep richer recommendation metadata in each pattern's `life-patterns-*.json` authoring package. Include approximately 8–15 natural descriptions of the situations in which someone might seek the pattern. Keep stable IDs and file paths when collection names change.

Reuse the existing concept-graphic registry. For article diagrams, use editable native SVG where suitable, meaningful alternative text, explanatory captions, and the existing asset manifest. Check both desktop and narrow layouts. A concept graphic and an explanatory article diagram serve different purposes.

Validate canonical content, resolve links, and check any executable practice before delivery. Review the examples, the explanation of the strategic shift, and the limits together; consistent coverage matters more than identical headings.
