# Songwriting patterns: editorial blueprint

Status: unpublished first edition. All course entities use `status: draft`; they are visible in development and excluded from production. No publication, push, or pull request is authorized.

## Reader and outcome

The reader has some experience making or listening closely to music and wants useful ways to write a song. Curious beginners remain welcome, but the collection does not require everyone to start with a theory curriculum. Lead with a recognizable writing situation and an actionable choice. Supply enough vocabulary beside the example to understand the choice, with optional reference material for a deeper refresher. The destination is a complete original song and an intelligible simple demo.

The scope is primarily English-language songwriting in pop, rock, folk, and electronic traditions using twelve-tone equal temperament. Mark style-specific expectations. Major/minor, functional harmony, rhyming, choruses, and regular meter are possibilities rather than laws. Optional harmonic depth must not become an entrance requirement for writing.

## Structure and dependencies

`chapters.json` is the durable ordered plan: 49 patterns, eight parts, stable source IDs, situations, responses, boundaries, and related-pattern relationships. Every main entry is a reusable songwriting pattern. Each part is an ordered draft collection inside a parent collection. The five supporting guides are reference collections with meaningful related-pattern items; a glossary is not presented as a pattern. Previous/next links offer a suggested route without turning the sequence into mandatory prerequisites.

The 2026-10-26 restructuring preserves the worked material and breadth while replacing 25 theory-led entries with practical decisions. For example, the circle of fifths supports **Transpose the Song to Fit the Voice**, and diatonic harmony supports **Think in Chord Numbers**. Each begins with **Use when**, gives a concrete move before its technical explanation, includes original comparisons, names a boundary, and links to two patterns with distinct uses. The 23 existing practical patterns receive the same clear entry contract and relationship links without rewriting their already useful examples. Theory explains a move rather than providing the article's reason to exist.

Keep stable IDs, musical examples, illustrations, and the player/editor bindings. Former concept URLs redirect to their actual pattern or reference-collection route when that draft entity is present. No global Concept entity or unrelated library content is renamed.

The suggested route moves from a seed to rhythm and harmony, then melody and words, optional harmonic color, whole-song form, arrangement, and finishing. Readers may begin with the troubleshooting collection or a relevant pattern. Earlier links are optional refreshers, recorded as `helpful_before`; there are no enforced prerequisites. Harmonic color in Part VI can wait while the reader builds a first complete song.

## Establish the standard first

The representative standard is **Transpose the Song to Fit the Voice**, **Write Through Images**, and **Choose a Form That Serves the Song**. These calibrate technical explanation, creative writing, and whole-song choices. Each needs a recognizable situation, a concrete response, substantial original comparisons, an explanation of limits, useful neighboring patterns, and attribution where claims need checking. Continue without a mandatory approval gate.

## Original examples

**Porch Light** is the recurring vocal song: C major, 4/4, quarter note = 84; seed E4–G4–A4 with durations 1–1–2 quarter-note beats. The standard verse has eight bars C | C | Am | Am | F | F | G | G. The eight-bar chorus is F | G | C | Am | F | G | C | C. Clearly label substitutions, transpositions, and alternative endings instead of quietly changing the base version. The title phrase is “Leave the porch light on.”

**Platform Three** is the contrasting instrumental/groove sketch: D-centered, 4/4, quarter note = 104. Its first palette D–E–F–G–A–B–C is D Dorian; D and A anchor the bass. Development comes through rhythm, texture, and register, with a D-minor version for a controlled comparison. Neither example imitates a named artist's melody or lyrics.

Provide a canonical songbook with complete original lyrics for Porch Light, bar-by-bar chord sheets, explicit note/duration examples, alternatives, and an arrangement map. An instrumental sketch can have an expressive arc without a vocal chorus. The miniature from pattern 6 is deliberately smaller than the final song.

## Pattern quality

Keep each pattern as long as its decision and examples require. Preserve the substantial worked material, but do not add fundamentals merely to meet a word target. Put the reader's musical problem first, then a usable response and a concrete comparison. Explain why it works and when it does not. Distinguish neighboring patterns: identity from a foreground hook, section purpose from whole-song order, pace from rhythmic activity, and transposition from modulation. The main through-example recurs with new analytical work; contrasting original examples establish transfer.

The initial reading edition excluded interactive practices, executable practice fences, custom widgets, and exercise forms. On 2026-10-26 the user authorized a musical player and chose a piano-roll view, then requested a Roman-numeral editor focused on chord progressions. Those follow-ups add playback of original examples and a local chord sketch with transposition, duration, and playback controls; see `player-design.md`. Interactive practices and exercise forms remain outside this edition. Worked examples remain complete explanations, and listening references use links. Do not turn every article into a worksheet.

## Evidence and listening

Use primary educational sources (Open Music Theory, university material, Ableton's own educational publications, and Berklee's writing instruction) to check terminology. Make the article prose, examples, and diagrams original. Cite claims next to the explanation or in a focused sources paragraph. Maintain a source register distinguishing technical reference, official recording, and our listening interpretation. Do not copy third-party lyrics, scores, recordings, or diagrams. Public access does not imply permission to redistribute.

Choose listening studies for a clear connection to the particular songwriting decision, then review the course-wide balance of artists, eras, and traditions. Include classical repertoire, classic songs, contemporary voices, and electronic music without defaulting to the Beatles or Radiohead. Retain a small number of their especially useful examples, including the live/studio comparison of Radiohead’s “True Love Waits.” Exclude “Creep” and Taylor Swift from the reference selection, as requested by the user. All 49 articles now contain one developed anchor study. `listening-integration.json` records current coverage and the limits of its source checks; the listening guide indexes the actual article studies. The other choices remain alternatives in `listening-candidates.md` and `listening-candidates.json`. Written integration does not imply that every reference has an embedded player or a checked excerpt endpoint. Repeated works need different listening questions and one home for a fuller analysis; do not replace one overused artist with another. Describe audible features with bounded interpretations; do not invent authorial intent or historical origin stories. Avoid precise timestamps that have not been checked against the linked recording.
