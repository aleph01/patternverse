# Listening examples: three candidates for every songwriting pattern

**Integration update (26 October 2026):** one selected anchor is now written into each of the 48 pattern articles. The 96 other candidates remain alternatives. See [current integration record](listening-integration.json) for the actual selections, headings, source checks, and playback limits. The proposals below preserve their original editorial purposes; they do not imply three embedded or fully transcribed recordings per article.

Editorial proposal · 26 October 2026 · Unpublished

This proposal covers all 48 pattern articles in their current order: **144 candidate placements**, each with a particular songwriting decision to illuminate. It includes classical repertoire as well as classic songs, contemporary pop, folk, jazz, soul, hip-hop, and several strands of electronic music. The three choices are alternatives and contrasts, not a requirement to add three lengthy analyses to every article.

My recommendation is to give each article one short anchor example and two optional listening comparisons. Keep **Porch Light** and **Platform Three** as the original worked examples that readers can follow across the course. The external references should make the writing choices easier to hear, rather than replace those examples or turn each pattern into a history lesson.

The listening angles below are **editorial proposals**, not claims about what a creator intended. Sources were checked for work/recording identity and, where relevant, documented process. Links go to artist or label releases, licensed recordings, performing institutions, archives, interviews, or score editions; a score/catalog/notes link is labeled as such and is not presented as a free recording. This is not an audio audition or a completed harmonic transcription of every work. Exact excerpts, timestamps, keys, note spellings, and edition-specific bar numbers still need checking when an example is selected. Specific cautions appear where they affect the choice.

No lyrics, commercial recordings, or published score extracts are reproduced here. These candidates have not been inserted into the course articles.

## Selection brief

The selection should feel like a varied listening library, with every reference chosen for the particular writing move it makes audible. Avoid defaulting to the same few rock artists when a different tradition or era offers an equally clear example.

- Exclude **“Creep”** and **Taylor Swift** from this course’s reference selection.
- Keep **“True Love Waits”**, including the live/studio comparison in article 47.
- Retain four Beatles placements where they have distinct uses: **“Let It Be”** (chord numbers), **“Blackbird”** (development), **“Tomorrow Never Knows”** (tonal world), and **“Strawberry Fields Forever”** (alternate versions).
- Retain three Radiohead placements with different purposes: **“Everything in Its Right Place”** (focused listening), **“15 Step”** (meter), and **“True Love Waits”** (alternate realizations).
- Keep classical repertoire and a wide range of classic and modern songs, including electronic music. Do not replace one overused artist with another. Repeated works should have a distinct listening question and a single home for any fuller analysis.

## Part 1 — Start Making Songs

### 1. Protect the Song's Identity

- **Beethoven — Symphony No. 5, movement I.** Follow the short opening rhythmic idea through changes of pitch, orchestration, and scale. It could help readers name what remains recognizable when the surface changes. [Boston Symphony work notes](https://www.bso.org/works/beethoven-symphony-no-5) · classical; notes.
- **Leonard Cohen / Jeff Buckley — “Hallelujah.”** Compare one shared melodic phrase or refrain across two very different vocal and instrumental settings. Ask what preserves the song’s identity while the recording’s character changes. The versions also differ in their selection of verses, so do not describe the entire lyric as unchanged. [Cohen’s official recording page](https://www.leonardcohen.com/video/hallelujah), [Buckley’s official Bearsville performance](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=y8AWFf7EAc4) · singer-songwriter / alternative song.
- **Daft Punk — “Around the World.”** Ask what identifies the recording before considering the repeated vocal phrase: the bass movement, rhythmic relationships, and timbres. This keeps the pattern applicable to dance music whose identity is not reducible to a sung melody. [Official video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=K0HSD_i2DvA) · house.

### 2. Begin with What You Have

- **Schubert — “Gretchen am Spinnrade,” D 118.** Consider the piano's revolving figure as a possible compositional entry: what kind of vocal line and situation could such motion invite? Present this as an imagined writing route, not evidence that Schubert actually composed accompaniment first. [Schubert Digital work record](https://schubert-digital.acdh-dev.oeaw.ac.at/W-00026.html) · art song; catalog.
- **New Order — “Blue Monday,” original single.** Treat the drum-machine pattern, sequenced material, and bass as possible entry points for building a section. Compare that imagined starting route with the band’s own account when preparing the study; the finished recording alone cannot establish the order in which its parts were written. [Licensed original single](https://open.spotify.com/album/0YdEEZG4XiZN7AOxuNrmaR), [band interview](https://songexploder.net/new-order) · electronic / post-punk; process source available.
- **Bicep — “Apricots,” on _Isles_.** Treat the relationship between vocal sample, repeating harmony, and percussion as a sound-led entry point. Ask which element a beginner could keep and develop into a first section. Do not infer the actual studio chronology from the finished track. [Artist/label release](https://bicep.bandcamp.com/album/isles) · electronic.

### 3. Repeat with a Reason

- **Ravel — _Boléro_.** Compare the stability of the recurring material with the changing orchestral color and accumulation. This can show that purposeful repetition does not require rewriting the tune on every return. [Score-and-recording study](https://timbreandorchestration.org/learn-about-orchestration/scores-and-recordings/ravel/bolero) · orchestral.
- **Nia Archives — “Crowded Roomz.”** Contrast the recurring vocal material with the activity around it. It could demonstrate how a listener holds onto a simple emotional statement while a jungle groove keeps the surface moving. [Label introduction and release links](https://www.universalmusic.ca/2024/01/19/nia-archives-confronts-the-loneliness-of-life-on-tour-on-new-single-crowded-roomz/) · contemporary jungle.
- **Four Tet — “Two Thousand and Seventeen,” on _New Energy_.** Follow the plucked figure and mark changes around its returns. This is an approachable example of development through context, layering, and small changes rather than an escalating series of new melodies. [Artist release](https://fourtet.bandcamp.com/album/new-energy) · electronic.

### 4. Listen Like a Songwriter

- **Bach — Prelude in C major, BWV 846.** Set one listening question: where does the repeating broken-chord texture change its harmonic direction? It gives beginners an audible constant against which to notice change. [Netherlands Bach Society performance](https://www.bachvereniging.nl/en/bwv/bwv-846) · keyboard.
- **Radiohead — “Everything in Its Right Place,” on _Kid A_.** Make three separate passes for keyboard, lead voice, and manipulated vocal material. A simple entrance map could distinguish an observation about the recording from an interpretation of its mood. [Official album stream](https://radiohead.bandcamp.com/album/kid-a) · electronic/art rock.
- **Bonobo — “Kerala,” on _Migration_.** Track the sampled vocal and the entrances around it, then compare that listening map with the label's account of the track's development. This is a useful place to distinguish what can be heard from what requires a process source. [Artist/label release and notes](https://bonobomusic.bandcamp.com/album/migration) · electronic.

### 5. Capture Musical Seeds

- **Beethoven — Symphony No. 5 sketches, discussed by Leonard Bernstein.** Use a very small sketch-to-work comparison to show that preserving an idea can preserve options, including versions later rejected. Bernstein's discussion is an interpretation of surviving sketches, not an eyewitness account of composition. [Bernstein estate lecture and transcript](https://leonardbernstein.com/lectures/television-scripts/omnibus/beethovens-fifth-symphony) · documented sketches.
- **Billie Eilish — “everything i wanted,” with FINNEAS.** The original voice memos in their interview would make the difference between a captured seed and a polished recording concrete. Focus on what the rough document preserved well enough to return to. [Artist interview with voice memos](https://songexploder.net/billie-eilish) · contemporary pop; process.
- **Bonobo — “Kerala.”** The official release notes describe a rough version played while touring before the album version. It could support a short case about retaining a useful draft while testing it in a real listening context. [Label account and recording](https://bonobomusic.bandcamp.com/album/migration) · electronic; process.

### 6. Finish a Tiny Song

- **Schumann — “Von fremden Ländern und Menschen,” _Kinderszenen_, Op. 15 No. 1.** Map the compact opening, contrast, and return. It offers a complete miniature whose sense of proportion matters more than fitting the labels verse, chorus, and bridge. [Henle edition](https://www.henle.de/de/Kinderszenen-op.-15/HN-44) · piano; score edition.
- **Vashti Bunyan — “Diamond Day.”** Map how a brief vocal piece establishes a scene, repeats a manageable idea, and comes to a close. It could make finishing a small song feel achievable without requiring a contrasting bridge, a dramatic climax, or a full-band arrangement. [Licensed song release](https://music.apple.com/us/song/269891652) · folk miniature.
- **J Dilla — “Waves,” on _Donuts_.** Listen for a complete gesture made from sampled material within a short span. It would broaden “tiny song” into a finished musical miniature without pretending that an instrumental beat must contain a lyric or chorus. [Official Stones Throw release](https://dilla.bandcamp.com/album/donuts) · instrumental hip-hop.

## Part 2 — Rhythm and Groove

### 7. Set the Pace for the Phrase

- **Schubert — “Der Leiermann,” _Winterreise_, D 911 No. 24.** Compare the recurring accompaniment with the measured vocal phrases. This could show how a steady instrumental motion leaves room for an unhurried delivery. [Historical edition record](https://schubert-online.at/activpage/werke_einzelansicht.php?herkunft=allewerke&top=7&werke_id=656) · art song; catalog.
- **Joni Mitchell — “Both Sides Now,” _Clouds_ and the later orchestral recording.** Compare the pace and spacing of the same lyric in two settings. The question is how the delivery changes, not which recording has the “correct” tempo. [1969 album](https://jonimitchell.com/music/album.cfm?id=3), [2000 album](https://www.jonimitchell.com/music/album.cfm?id=22&o=3) · folk/orchestral song.
- **Massive Attack — “Teardrop.”** Hear the busier repeating instrumental material beneath the spacious vocal. It could help readers separate the underlying pulse, subdivisions, and rate of sung information before deciding to speed up a song. [Official video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=u7K72X4eo_s) · trip-hop.

### 8. Group the Beat Around the Phrase

- **Chopin — Waltz in D-flat major, Op. 64 No. 1.** Compare the three-beat dance framework with the many quicker notes passing through it. This is a clear way to show that the number of note attacks is not the meter. [Score and MIDI edition](https://www.mutopiaproject.org/cgibin/piece-info.cgi?id=483) · piano waltz.
- **The Animals — “House of the Rising Sun,” original hit recording.** Follow the rolling accompaniment and compare a two-large-beat compound count with its smaller subdivisions. Fix the count to the selected score/recording before illustrating 6/8; do not call every flowing arpeggio compound meter. [Official artist audio](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MJkr0DWbhTk) · folk rock.
- **Radiohead — “15 Step,” on _In Rainbows_.** Use the recurring five-beat grouping as an alternative to four-beat expectations. Keep the first demonstration physical and audible: where does the pattern start again, even while the individual sounds change? [Official album stream](https://radiohead.bandcamp.com/album/in-rainbows) · contemporary rock/electronic rhythm.

### 9. Make Space Part of the Rhythm

- **Webern — Six Bagatelles for string quartet, Op. 9, No. 1.** Notice how short gestures and their separations define the phrase. This is an optional listening contrast, not an invitation to teach atonal harmony before discussing a rest. [Publisher edition and preview](https://www.universaledition.com/en/6-Bagatelles/P0272041) · chamber music; score edition.
- **Billie Eilish — “when the party's over.”** Attend to the ends of vocal phrases and the space before a response. It could show why adding accompaniment into every opening may weaken the delivery. [Official video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pbMwTqkKSps) · sparse contemporary pop.
- **James Blake — “Limit to Your Love.”** Contrast the piano-and-voice passages, gaps, and bass entrances. This is a vivid example of silence and withheld low-frequency energy becoming part of the arrangement's rhythm. [Official video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oOT2-OTebx0) · electronic soul.

### 10. Pull an Entrance Across the Beat

- **Scott Joplin — “The Entertainer.”** Compare the melody's offbeat activity with the left hand's reference pattern. A short, carefully counted phrase could make syncopation easier to hear without shifting the entire accompaniment. [Library of Congress score](https://www.loc.gov/item/2023864238/) · ragtime; historical score.
- **Stevie Wonder — “Superstition.”** Follow one entrance in the clavinet riff against the steady beat. The lesson would isolate a rhythmic relationship instead of reducing the whole groove to the instruction “play off the beat.” [Official artist audio](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=egqv1mtos6A) · funk.
- **Beyoncé — “BREAK MY SOUL.”** Compare vocal and keyboard attacks with the house pulse. It could connect an anticipated entrance to dance music readers may already know. Choose and count a specific short passage before labeling an attack as an anticipation. [Official release links](https://beyonce.lnk.to/BREAKMYSOUL) · contemporary house/pop.

### 11. Give the Groove a Consistent Feel

- **Duke Ellington and His Orchestra, featuring Ivie Anderson — “It Don't Mean a Thing (If It Ain't Got That Swing).”** Follow the band's shared phrasing rather than treating swing as a fixed mathematical ratio. Specify the early Ellington/Anderson recording when choosing the listening link. [Smithsonian archival recording notes](https://folkways-media.si.edu/docs/folkways/artwork/FW02804.pdf) · jazz; liner notes.
- **D'Angelo — “Untitled (How Does It Feel).”** Compare the vocal placement with the instrumental reference. It offers a useful distinction between relaxed placement around a shared pulse and an ensemble simply losing time. Avoid assigning an exact millisecond offset without measurement. [Official video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SxVNOnPyvIU) · neo-soul.
- **J Dilla — “Time: The Donut of the Heart,” on _Donuts_.** Listen for the recurring relationship among sample, kick, and snare. The point is a repeatable feel, not a claim that random timing variation reproduces Dilla's approach or that all his tracks use one formula. [Official label release](https://dilla.bandcamp.com/album/donuts) · hip-hop.

### 12. Interlock the Groove

- **Bach — Invention in C major, BWV 772.** Trace how the two lines answer, overlap, and exchange material. This makes an illuminating historical comparison for arranging complementary parts, while keeping contrapuntal imitation distinct from a drum groove. [Score and MIDI edition](https://www.mutopiaproject.org/cgibin/piece-info.cgi?id=40) · keyboard counterpoint.
- **Fela Kuti — “Water No Get Enemy,” on _Expensive Shit_.** Follow one accompaniment part at a time before hearing the composite groove. It would demonstrate how repeated parts can be relatively economical individually while producing a rich ensemble relationship. [Official label release](https://felakuti.bandcamp.com/album/expensive-shit-1975) · Afrobeat.
- **KH / Four Tet — “Looking at Your Pager.”** Map the recurring vocal fragment against bass and percussion. It could show how a sample can take a rhythmic role inside the groove rather than sit above it as a continuous lead melody. [Artist release](https://fourtet.bandcamp.com/track/looking-at-your-pager) · electronic/dance.

## Part 3 — Notes, Keys, and Chords

### 13. Preserve the Distances in a Melody

- **Beethoven — the “Ode to Joy” theme, Symphony No. 9, movement IV.** Begin with the tune's repeated notes and stepwise motion. Its familiarity can help separate interval relationships from the octave, instrument, or key in which the theme appears. [Performing institution's work notes](https://handelandhaydn.org/program-notes-beethoven-symphony-no-9/) · classical; notes.
- **Bill Withers — “Lean on Me.”** Compare the repeated notes and neighboring steps in a short vocal phrase with its larger pitch movements. A checked interval sketch could show why preserving the distances matters when moving a familiar tune to another starting note. [Official audio](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gOZgo8gMIoM) · soul.
- **Kraftwerk — “The Model,” _3-D The Catalogue_ recording.** Follow the recurring instrumental melody through the electronic setting. It could demonstrate that intervals remain useful when there is no acoustic instrument to watch. Use this named version consistently rather than mixing it with the original studio recording. [Official artist audio](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=C3nL7c34VK0) · electronic pop.

### 14. Establish a Tonal Home

- **Bach — Cello Suite No. 1 in G major, BWV 1007, Prelude.** Hear how a single melodic instrument can suggest harmony and a center through its arpeggios and returns. It is a strong counterexample to needing a full block chord to establish home. [Lucia Swarts / Netherlands Bach Society performance](https://www.bachvereniging.nl/en/bwv/bwv-1007) · solo cello.
- **Miles Davis — “So What,” _Kind of Blue_.** Attend to bass, recurring responses, and sustained modal regions. It could establish that a center can feel clear without a stream of conventional dominant-to-tonic cadences. [Official album and preview](https://www.milesdavis.com/albums/kind-of-blue/) · modal jazz.
- **Floating Points, Pharoah Sanders, and the London Symphony Orchestra — _Promises_, Movement 1.** Follow the recurring figure and the space around it. This is a useful discussion of tonal orientation and ambiguity: a passage need not announce a textbook cadence to give the ear a reference. [Official release](https://floatingpoints.bandcamp.com/album/promises) · electronic/jazz/orchestral.

### 15. Choose Chords by Their Notes

- **Bach — Prelude in C major, BWV 846.** Reduce a few broken-chord groups to their sounding pitch collections. This makes the difference between a chord's notes and the order in which those notes are played tangible. Start with simple groups before addressing the more complex harmony later. [Official performance](https://www.bachvereniging.nl/en/bwv/bwv-846) · keyboard.
- **Bob Marley and the Wailers — “Three Little Birds.”** Take a small, checked accompaniment passage and identify the notes inside its basic chords. Compare chord identity with the offbeat articulation and spacing that give the recording its character; a triad label describes only part of what is heard. [Official video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HNBCVM4KbUM) · reggae.
- **Aphex Twin — “Avril 14th,” on _Drukqs_.** Its piano texture could bridge electronic listeners into hearing the pitches inside an accompaniment. Reduce a selected passage carefully; not every simultaneous pitch collection should be forced into a plain triad label. [Official Warp release](https://aphextwin.warp.net/release/71102-aphex-twin-drukqs?lang=en_US) · piano/electronic artist; release page.

### 16. Think in Chord Numbers

- **Pachelbel — Canon in D.** Number the repeating harmonic framework and retain its durations while the upper parts change. It provides an especially clear comparison between a progression and the much richer musical result built on it. [Score and MIDI edition](https://www.mutopiaproject.org/cgibin/piece-info.cgi?id=2047) · Baroque ground/canon.
- **The Beatles — “Let It Be.”** Compare chord names with Roman numerals in one short passage, then move the same relationships to another key. Keep the actual sequence and phrase lengths visible rather than substituting a generic four-chord loop. [Official performance page](https://www.thebeatles.com/let-it-be-3) · classic pop.
- **Daft Punk featuring Pharrell Williams and Nile Rodgers — “Get Lucky.”** Use the recurring progression to discuss relative chord relationships and harmonic rhythm. It is also a useful boundary case: agree on and explain the tonal-center reading before presenting a single Roman-numeral analysis as settled. [Official audio](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5NV6Rdv1a3I) · disco/pop.

### 17. Transpose the Song to Fit the Voice

- **Schubert — “An die Musik,” in high- and low-voice editions.** Compare the same song in two published voice ranges, checking both ends of the line and the accompanying harmony. This is stronger evidence of transposition than assuming that two singers' very different arrangements differ only in key. [High-voice edition](https://www.halleonard.com/product/1143/franz-schubert-15-selected-songs-high-voice), [low-voice edition](https://www.halleonard.com/product/1144/franz-schubert-15-selected-songs-low-voice) · art song; publisher editions.
- **“Amazing Grace,” using the NEW BRITAIN tune.** Use the historical tune as the basis for a newly made two-key demonstration, asking where the low and high notes sit for a particular singer. Name the tune: the text has been paired with other melodies. [Archive of historical tune sources](https://www.hymnologyarchive.com/amazing-grace) · hymn; historical scores. This candidate needs an original demonstration, not a claim about an artist's choice of key.
- **Robyn / Calum Scott — “Dancing on My Own.”** Compare the vocal ranges and registers in the two named recordings, then test a suitable key for the reader. **Conditional choice:** verify their keys and any melodic changes first; the comparison also changes pace, arrangement, and delivery, so it cannot isolate transposition by itself. [Robyn's official video page](https://www.robyn.com/videos/v/dancing-on-my-own-official-video), [Scott's official video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q31tGyBJhRY) · electronic pop / ballad.

### 18. Decide Where the Harmony Arrives

- **Mozart — Piano Sonata in C major, K 545, movement I.** Compare a passage that keeps moving with a clearly articulated phrase ending. A short score-backed example could separate harmonic direction, melodic punctuation, and the feeling of an arrival. [Score and MIDI edition](https://www.mutopiaproject.org/cgibin/piece-info.cgi?id=998) · Classical piano.
- **Sam Cooke — “A Change Is Gonna Come.”** Compare the endings of recurring vocal passages with the contrasting middle section. It could show how melody, harmony, and orchestral pacing cooperate to make an arrival feel earned. Select a short phrase and check its cadence before turning the listening observation into a harmonic diagram. [Official artist audio](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Z5zDRtEC0x0) · soul.
- **Bicep — “Glue,” on _Bicep_.** Ask where arrivals are felt when recurring harmony works with entrances, texture, and rhythmic release. It is a useful contrast with a classical cadence; do not invent a new chord change for every perceived rise in energy. [Artist/label release](https://bicep.bandcamp.com/album/bicep) · electronic.

## Part 4 — Melody and Hooks

### 19. Start with a Motif

- **Beethoven — Symphony No. 5, movement I.** Take the opening rhythmic cell as a deliberately tiny unit and compare a few of its responses. Here the focus is building a phrase from the unit; article 1 uses the same work to discuss identity across larger changes. [Work notes](https://www.bso.org/works/beethoven-symphony-no-5) · classical.
- **The White Stripes — “Seven Nation Army.”** Isolate the recurring guitar figure and follow its relationship to the vocal and fuller ensemble passages. It is a clear example of a tiny pitch-and-rhythm idea carrying a large share of a song’s identity without being the whole song. [Official video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0J2QdDbelmY) · riff-based rock.
- **Jon Hopkins — “Open Eye Signal,” on _Immunity_.** Follow the recurring bass figure as the track develops around it. It offers an electronic example of a small unit capable of sustaining a much longer structure. [Artist/label release](https://jonhopkins.bandcamp.com/album/immunity) · electronic.

### 20. Give the Melody a Destination

- **Schubert — “Ellens Gesang III” (“Ave Maria”), D 839.** Sketch the rise, sustained emphasis, and release of a selected vocal phrase. Specify the original song setting rather than silently switching between its German text and later Latin adaptations. [Schubert critical-edition database](https://schubert-ausgabe.de/en/research/schubert-database/?FORM%5Bdetail%5D=475&cursor=925) · art song; catalog.
- **Kate Bush — “Wuthering Heights.”** Compare the register and contour of its contrasting vocal passages. The useful question is what a high register does to the character and intensity of a phrase, not whether beginners should imitate Bush's range. [Official video, version 1](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-1pMMIe4hb4) · art pop.
- **SOPHIE — “Is It Cold in the Water?”** Compare the vocal line's register with the surrounding electronic activity. It could show how a melodic destination is supported by sound and space, rather than explained by the highest pitch alone. [Licensed song release](https://music.apple.com/us/album/is-it-cold-in-the-water/1709023350?i=1709023358) · experimental electronic pop.

### 21. Support or Challenge the Melody Note

- **Bach — “Air,” Orchestral Suite No. 3 in D major, BWV 1068, movement II.** Follow a sustained upper note while other voices move beneath it. It gives a concrete route into hearing a note's changing relationship to harmony without changing the note itself. [Netherlands Bach Society performance](https://www.bachvereniging.nl/en/bwv/bwv-1068) · orchestral.
- **Amy Winehouse — “Love Is a Losing Game.”** Follow a held or recurring vocal note through a short harmonic passage, listening for how the accompaniment changes its expressive weight. Check the actual pitches before labeling a note as a chord tone, suspension, or other tension; a sustained note is not automatically a suspension. [Licensed song release](https://music.apple.com/us/song/1422677786) · contemporary soul.
- **Portishead — “Roads,” on _Dummy_.** Listen to the expressive relationship between the vocal and the repeating keyboard harmony. It could introduce tension that is carried by a note's context and delivery rather than by a complicated new melody. [Official artist audio](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7nxWP9BhI7w) · trip-hop.

### 22. Let the Phrase Breathe

- **Schumann — “Träumerei,” _Kinderszenen_, Op. 15 No. 7.** Draw phrase arcs through the piano melody and notice where an instrumental line suggests breathing. This is an analogy for phrasing, not evidence that an instrumental phrase has the same physical limits as a singer. [Urtext edition](https://www.henle.de/de/Kinderszenen-op.-15/HN-44) · piano; score edition.
- **Arooj Aftab — “Mohabbat,” on _Vulture Prince_.** Attend to the length, release, and space around vocal gestures. It could widen the course's idea of a phrase beyond short English-language pop lines; provide a concise contextual explanation without imitating a tradition through a generic rule. [Artist/label release](https://aroojaftab.bandcamp.com/album/vulture-prince) · contemporary song rooted in ghazal traditions.
- **James Blake — “Retrograde.”** Compare the recurring vocal figure with the phrasing of the lead line. It could show how accompaniment continues to hold a listener's attention while the singer leaves room between statements. [Official video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6p6PcFFUm5I) · electronic soul.

### 23. Write a Hook

- **Mozart — _Eine kleine Nachtmusik_, K 525, movement I.** Isolate the opening gesture and ask why it is easy to recognize before the listener knows the whole movement. “Hook” is our songwriting comparison here, not Mozart's historical label for the phrase. [Score and MIDI edition](https://www.mutopiaproject.org/cgibin/piece-info.cgi?id=900) · Classical ensemble music.
- **Michael Jackson — “Billie Jean.”** Compare the bass hook with the sung refrain. It is a strong example of multiple memorable features taking different roles, rather than one hook having to carry every part of a song. [Official video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Zi_XLOBDo_Y) · pop/funk.
- **Kylie Minogue — “Can't Get You Out of My Head.”** Follow the recurring vocal cell and its relationship to the electronic groove. It could demonstrate a compact hook whose repetition is part of the song's subject and character. [Official video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=c18441Eh_WE) · electronic pop.

### 24. Develop Without Losing the Idea

- **Beethoven — Symphony No. 6, “Pastoral,” movement I.** Compare repetitions, extensions, and changes of context in the opening material. It offers development that can feel expansive without requiring constant replacement of the basic idea. [Boston Symphony work notes](https://www.bso.org/works/beethoven-symphony-no-6-pastoral) · classical.
- **The Beatles — “Blackbird.”** Follow a recurring accompaniment gesture as phrases and harmonic positions change. It could show how a recognizable playing idea can connect varied material rather than remaining an identical loop under everything. [Official song page](https://www.thebeatles.com/blackbird) · acoustic song.
- **Four Tet — “Two Thousand and Seventeen.”** Return to the repeating figure, this time comparing two separated passages and listing what changed around it. Article 3 establishes why repetition works; this article would examine the particular development choices. [Artist release](https://fourtet.bandcamp.com/album/new-energy) · electronic.

## Part 5 — Lyrics and Meaning

### 25. Find the Central Idea

- **Dolly Parton — “Jolene.”** Identify the specific speaker, addressee, and urgent request that organize the lyric. It is a clear example of turning a broad theme such as jealousy into an immediate situation. [Artist's album page and description](https://www.dollyparton.com/album/jolene) · country.

- **Daft Punk — “Something About Us.”** Compare one plain-triad teaching version with a checked version of the richer keyboard sonority. ### 34. Borrow a Chord

### 35. Point to a Temporary Home

- **Ray Charles — “Georgia on My Mind,” _Live at Montreux 1997_.** Select a short passage where a dominant chord directs the ear toward a local target, then follow what happens after that arrival. ## Selection and preparation notes

- **Choose by the pattern's decision.** An unfamiliar example with a clear audible feature is more useful than a famous song with a tenuous connection. A beginner should understand the listening question without knowing the artist or reading notation.
- **Use the range deliberately.** There is no need for a classical/pop/electronic quota within every article.
