## Teaching boundaries and examples

- **P23 — Perceptual selection:** Two people visit the same hypothetical studio flat separately. Nadia, a night worker, attends to a blind and street noise while imagining daytime sleep. Ben considers his father's route through the furniture during lunch visits. The differences concern attention and knowledge, rather than emotional adjectives applied to an identical inventory. Neither encounter completes a suitability assessment. Profession does not determine every observation, and plausible concern is not evidence of actual thought.
- **P24 — Narrative closeness:** Eleni waits for her first public reading after six years of private writing. A contextual account reports her nervousness; a closer version lets her response colour the language. The comparison identifies the orientation lost along with the gain in immediacy. Movement toward and away from the reading has a local purpose. A separate invented first-person departure distinguishes an earlier self's belief in independence from a later narrator's knowledge of an aunt's help. Greater distance, reflection, long sentences, and overview remain legitimate choices.
- **P25 — Changes of organizing perspective:** Mina wants to sell a piano before moving; Owen wants it delivered before a pupil's first lesson. A private deadline initially arrives before its owner is clear. An earlier anchor makes the transfer readable. Alternatives use a stronger section boundary, reveal the deadline in dialogue while retaining Mina's viewpoint, or establish an omniscient narrator. The article distinguishes a perspective change from external observation and from merely correcting a pronoun. A heading cannot substitute for distinctive attention or orient a change of time and place by itself.
- **P26 — Speech that affects the exchange:** Rae asks to borrow Tomas's van despite having returned it late before. Each response changes the next proposal until they agree on a bounded trip. A separate station phone call shows Leah and a friend changing the form of assistance without requiring an argument. Information can act, direct questions can receive direct answers, and companionship can have a function. Physical gestures support an exchange but cannot supply the consequential response that its words lack.

- **P27 — Supported implication:** Nell is packing to move out after eight months with her mother, Ruth. A tin used for their weekly Tuesday pie makes the future of the shared meal relevant to a practical exchange. Nell brings another guest into the plan, and Ruth responds by arranging a chair. The implication concerns an imminent continuation, not a verdict that all feelings or future visits are settled. The article tests what disappears without the shared habit, how responses acknowledge an unasked question, and when direct speech or further narration can help.
- **P28 — Speaker ownership:** A producer, editor, and interview guest discover missing audio. Attribution determines who proposes using a rehearsal and who objects to that use. Selective tags and relevant action establish ownership; added decoration could instead impose an interpretation. A separate backstage cue makes a whisper consequential because it limits who can hear. The article addresses re-entry, third speakers, tag placement, action timing, and useful repetition of names without a fixed ratio or ban on delivery details.

P23 concerns what is available and salient through a perceiver. P24 concerns how closely the telling follows experience within a perspective. P25 concerns changes of organizing perspective and the permissions established by the narrator. P26 concerns what speech does, P27 concerns meaning it leaves unspoken, and P28 concerns speaker identification. P29 gives a scene an immediate concern without taking over these exchange-level jobs. The future G01 synthesizes these with other choices that shape voice.

All people and exchanges above are original fiction. No real person's inner experience or dialogue is reconstructed. The nonfiction discussions require an identified basis for attributed thought, distinguish recollection from verbatim inner language, and preserve the sequence and substance of recorded speech. A formal transition or plausible purpose cannot establish evidence.

## Sources and metadata

[Voice-family metadata](voice-articles.json) records use cases, goals, mechanisms, limits, ten distinct recommendation queries per article, related IDs, explicit visual decisions, and inspected-source provenance. These fields stay outside strict frontmatter and do not imply a new recommendation feature.

- **P23:** John Larison's Oregon State guide, [What is Point of View?](https://liberalarts.oregonstate.edu/wlf/what-point-of-view), supports the distinction between narrative perspective and ranges of access. No source fictional example or categorical claim about first-person heroes, second-person limitations, or one form being most natural is adopted.
- **P24:** Emma Darwin's [Six Thoughts about Psychic Distance](https://emmadarwin.substack.com/p/itchy-bitesized-29-six-thoughts-about) supports different distances, movement along the spectrum, and retrospective narration. The article reproduces no numbered scale or source exercise and claims no direct review of John Gardner's book passages.
- **P25:** Ursula K. Le Guin's [Book View Cafe discussion, Session 1](https://www.ursulakleguin.com/bvc-navigating-the-ocean-of-story-session-1), specifically her answer to Patricia, defends multiple and unlimited viewpoints while stressing the skill needed to handle them. The transition and boundary comparisons are editorial development; no historical generalization or literary example is adopted.
- **P23–P25:** Lauren Kessler's [Character: The beating heart of narrative](https://niemanstoryboard.org/2023/05/11/profiles-character-development-reporting-sources/), specifically “Ask rather than assume,” supports grounding nonfiction inner experience in an identified account rather than projection. Recollection, attribution across accounts, and exact inner phrasing receive editorial applications rather than a claim of additional source rules.
- **P26:** Noemi Zeigler's [Dialogue Tools and Techniques](https://human.libretexts.org/Courses/Laney_College/Scriptwriting_for_Video_Broadcast_and_Digital_Media_%28Zeigler%29/11%3A_Dialogue/11.02%3A_Dialogue_Tools_and_Techniques), specifically “Writing Dialogue From Want,” connects speech to an immediate objective. Nicholas O'Connell's [Writing Classes for Dialogue](https://www.thewritersworkshop.net/creating-strong-characters-in-fiction-nonfiction/) discusses interaction, resolving conflict, reaching consensus, and action beats. The article adopts no universal requirement for conflict, behaviour before words, or indirect answers. The exchanges, cooperation comparison, and nonfiction applications are original editorial development.

- **P27:** Noemi Zeigler’s [Dialogue Tools and Techniques](https://human.libretexts.org/Courses/Laney_College/Scriptwriting_for_Video_Broadcast_and_Digital_Media_%28Zeigler%29/11%3A_Dialogue/11.02%3A_Dialogue_Tools_and_Techniques), specifically “Subtext,” distinguishes spoken content from underlying meaning. Suzzanna Matthews’s UCD Writing Centre handout, [Revising and Editing Fiction](https://www.ucd.ie/writingcentre/t4media/Creative%20Writing%20Revising%20and%20Editing%20Fiction.pdf), supplies dialogue-context and staging guidance on pages 2–3. No source example, claim that people rarely mean what they say, or universal interpretation of a gesture is adopted. Direct acknowledgment and limits on inference are editorial development.
- **P28:** Matthews’s dialogue-tag guidance on pages 2–3 supports simple attribution and relevant staging. Brandi Reissenweber’s Gotham answer, [Is it necessary to indicate who is speaking for every line of dialogue?](https://www.writingclasses.com/toolbox/ask-writer/is-it-necessary-to-indicate-who-is-speaking-for-every-line-of-dialogue), explains how tags, paragraphing, and action identify speakers without tagging every turn. No source literary extract, attribution quota, or direct review of Stephen King’s book passages is offered. Early identification, sound reach, and nonfiction applications are editorial development.

Sources were inspected on 25 October 2026. No source exercise, course attendance, full book-passage review, or empirical claim about perception or reader response is offered. Book assignments in the index remain research leads.
