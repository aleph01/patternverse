## Distinct teaching roles and examples

- **[P17 — Vary Sentence Length Deliberately](../../content/patterns/writing-vary-sentence-length-deliberately.md):** A fictional stage manager prepares a dress rehearsal while a necessary actor has not arrived. A revision gathers routine preparations and gives the absence a separate beat. A workshop proposal joins a commitment to the decision it supports. The article acknowledges changes of phrasing, grouping, and structure, rather than treating the comparison as an experiment isolating word count. It examines regularity with a purpose and rejects a fixed equation between short sentences and fast pace.
- **[P18 — Put Weight at the End](../../content/patterns/writing-put-weight-at-the-end.md):** A rehearsal-room funding update gives either a consequence or a funding source the final attention while retaining attribution. A maintenance-cost qualification distinguishes unfunded work from a report's silence. A translator's advance-copy scene examines paragraph arrival and the difference between rearranging invented actions and preserving a factual sequence. A cancellation notice keeps the answer first. The article concerns local closure, not the ending of a whole work or obligatory dramatic last words.
- **[P19 — Use Parallel Form for Related Ideas](../../content/patterns/writing-use-parallel-form-for-related-ideas.md):** A language-course comparison checks the information before matching its presentation. Workshop instructions make each item fit a shared grammatical opening without erasing responsibility. Two siblings' reckoning demonstrates the perspective carried by a composed contrast, while quotes and estimates preserve unequal confidence within a comparison. The article distinguishes structural correspondence from repeated wording, equal length, and equal evidence.
- **[P20 — Repeat with a Purpose](../../content/patterns/writing-repeat-with-a-purpose.md):** A student representative's repeated requests accumulate distinct obstacles; a studio's recurring deposit label preserves orientation; a restaurant conversation changes the role of “after service” without changing its words. The article distinguishes purposeful verbal recurrence from redundancy, factual flattening, and a whole-work motif. No required number of repetitions, guaranteed memory effect, or automatic ban on repeated words is proposed.
- **[P21 — Punctuate for Pace and Meaning](../../content/patterns/writing-punctuate-for-pace-and-meaning.md):** A hall-repair update compares continuity, specification, and a marked qualification. Clause boundaries and a courtyard-room comparison show how punctuation affects meaning as well as movement. A student's cancelled class examines contextualized narrative fragments. The article does not reduce punctuation to breath marks or present a comprehensive grammar reference; it retains scope, allows rebuilding an overloaded sentence, and distinguishes readable intention from accidental ambiguity.
- **[P22 — Break an Established Form for Emphasis](../../content/patterns/writing-break-an-established-form-for-emphasis.md):** A teacher closing a training centre moves from a repeated “he told” construction to a reversed role. A magazine proposal shifts from matched benefit statements to a genuine funding question. Each example has an identifiable formal baseline and departure. The article distinguishes this technique from narrative surprise, compulsory short endings, and invented regularity that misrepresents facts or voice.

These neighboring choices interact but keep distinct teaching jobs. P03 concerns subject–verb proximity within a sentence; P17 concerns lengths and complexity across a passage; P18 concerns the information placed at closure; P19 concerns grammatical correspondence; P20 concerns recurrence of language; P21 concerns internal punctuation; P22 concerns a marked departure from an established form. P39 remains the planned treatment of changing images and motifs across a whole work.

The rehearsal, telephone attempts, workshop proposal, funding report, translator scene, cancellation notice, courses, workshop instructions, siblings, cost comparison, hall requests, studio payment, restaurant, roof report, courtyard rooms, cancelled class, training-centre closure, and magazine proposal are original, hypothetical teaching material. No claim about real organizations, agreements, financial offers, or measured reading time is made. Practical examples establish their local facts instead of prescribing universal procedures.

## Sources and metadata

These fields stay outside strict article frontmatter and do not imply a new recommendation feature.

- **P17:** Ursula K. Le Guin's [Book View Cafe discussion, Session 1, Part 5](https://www.ursulakleguin.com/bvc-navigating-the-ocean-of-story-session-1-part-5), specifically the answer to Linda, treats length as one element of pacing and recommends variety appropriate to context. Purdue OWL's [Strategies for Variation](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/academic_writing/sentence_variety/index.html) discusses length, structure, rhythm, and emphasis.
- **P18:** Duke Graduate School's [Cohesion, Coherence, and Emphasis](https://sites.duke.edu/scientificwriting/lesson-2-cohesion-coherence-and-emphasis/), specifically Principle 1 on final position and emphasis. The article treats this as craft guidance; it claims no universal response or measured memory benefit. Paragraph closure, qualified scope, factual sequence, and answer-first alternatives are editorial extensions.
- **P19:** Purdue OWL's [Parallel Structure](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/mechanics/parallel_structure.html), covering words, phrases, clauses, and lists; the Australian Government Style Manual's [Parallel structure for lists](https://www.stylemanual.gov.au/style-manual-resources/government-writing-handbook/editors-tips/parallel-structure-lists), on lead-ins and the distinction from repeated openings. The article adopts no source exercise, fixed equal-length requirement, or assertion that matching forms imply equal support.
- **P20:** Ursula K. Le Guin's [Gollancz Festival 2015 Q&A](https://www.hachette.co.uk/ursula-k-le-guin-2/2015/10/18/ursula-le-guin-answers-your-questions/), specifically the answer to Keegan Spindler on deliberate repetition and clarity; Raymond Malewitz's Oregon State guide [What is Anaphora?](https://liberalarts.oregonstate.edu/wlf/what-anaphora), specifically its definition of recurring beginnings. No source speech or poem, etymological claim, or measured mnemonic effect is adopted.
- **P21:** Purdue OWL's [Brief Overview of Punctuation](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/punctuation/punctuation/index.html), including independent and dependent clauses, comma boundaries, semicolons, colons, parentheses, and dashes. The article uses the source's grammatical guidance without imposing fixed pause durations or a universal hierarchy of emotional effects. Its narrative-fragment and emphasis comparisons are editorial development.
- **P22:** Regent University Writing Lab's [Creating Emphasis](https://www.regent.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/Regent-University-Writing-Lab-Creating-Emphasis.pdf), specifically points 4 and 5 on creating and breaking a verbal pattern. No source example, historical assertion, fixed repetition count, or assertion that a different emphasis device cannot be overused is adopted. Role reversal, voice, and evidential cautions are editorial development.

Sources were inspected on 25 October 2026. Source notes distinguish their guidance from original examples and editorial extensions. The book assignments in the index remain research leads. No full book-passage review is claimed, and no source exercise is reproduced.
