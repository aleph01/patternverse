## Reader, scope, and organizing principle

The reader writes stories, essays, explanations, reports, or everyday professional prose and wants to make deliberate choices. Explain craft terminology before depending on it. The primary scope is English-language prose, including fiction and factual narrative. Poetry, screenwriting, specialist scientific reporting, and comprehensive grammar instruction are possible later extensions.

Group patterns and their companion guides by the problem they address. Tag each by scale and applicability so readers can also browse sentence, paragraph, scene, and whole-work choices. The first eight families concern effects in the text; the ninth concerns the writer's process. These are complementary entry points, not a compulsory sequence or universal aesthetic.

A pattern earns its place when it identifies a recognizable situation, offers a repeatable move, and explains the resulting tradeoff. Clarity and concision matter, but so do ambiguity, texture, humor, surprise, intimacy, and sound. A revision can become longer and better for its purpose. Distinguish English-specific syntax advice from principles that may transfer to other languages.

## How to read the annotations

Each pattern has a proposed stable article ID, a scale/applicability label, a one-sentence principle, a recognizable use case, source leads, and a boundary note. The guides compare tools and alternatives rather than claiming one intervention; their planned content kind is a concept/reference. T01 is an annotated section within the foundations reference. **Scope** describes the primary application, not every possible transfer.

**All source assignments below remain research leads.** Author and publisher pages confirm the books and broad coverage recorded in the source register; they do not establish that each book teaches each proposed pattern in these words. ## 1. Clarity and orientation

The task is to make relationships followable without flattening the subject. These patterns concern comprehension, not necessarily agreement or simplicity of thought.

### P01. Move from Familiar to New

**Draft article:** [writing-move-from-familiar-to-new.md](../../content/patterns/writing-move-from-familiar-to-new.md)

**Proposed ID:** `writing-move-from-familiar-to-new` · **Scope:** sentence–paragraph; general prose.

- **Principle:** Begin from information the reader can already identify, then use it to introduce the next unfamiliar idea.
- **Useful when:** Individual sentences make sense, but the reader cannot follow their progression.
- **Source leads:** [WB](#source-register) for cohesion; [GB](#source-register) for connections between parts.
- **Boundary and tradeoff:** Concerns information order, while P06 names logical relationships; deliberate disorientation may justify a different order after the reader has enough footing.

### P02. Put Actors in Subjects and Actions in Verbs

**Draft article:** [writing-put-actors-in-subjects-and-actions-in-verbs.md](../../content/patterns/writing-put-actors-in-subjects-and-actions-in-verbs.md)

**Proposed ID:** `writing-put-actors-in-subjects-and-actions-in-verbs` · **Scope:** sentence; general prose, especially explanation.

- **Principle:** When an action and its responsible actor matter, give them visible grammatical roles instead of burying them in abstract nouns.
- **Useful when:** Readers cannot tell who does what in sentences about implementation, evaluation, or change.
- **Source leads:** [WB](#source-register) for actions and characters; [CL](#source-register) for sentence tools.
- **Boundary and tradeoff:** Addresses grammatical packaging, while P08 sharpens verb meaning; familiar alias “prefer active voice” needs qualification because passive voice can preserve focus or appropriately leave an actor unnamed.

### P03. Keep Subjects and Verbs Close

**Draft article:** [writing-keep-subjects-and-verbs-close.md](../../content/patterns/writing-keep-subjects-and-verbs-close.md)

**Proposed ID:** `writing-keep-subjects-and-verbs-close` · **Scope:** sentence; general prose.

- **Principle:** Move long interruptions away from the subject–verb connection when they make the main action hard to hold in mind.
- **Useful when:** A sentence makes the reader wait through qualifications before discovering what happens.
- **Source leads:** [WB](#source-register) for sentence shape; [CL](#source-register) for sentence construction.
- **Boundary and tradeoff:** More specific than shortening a sentence; a carefully delayed verb can produce suspense, so compare comprehension and rhythm rather than enforcing a word-distance limit.

### P04. Make References Unambiguous

**Draft article:** [writing-make-references-unambiguous.md](../../content/patterns/writing-make-references-unambiguous.md)

**Proposed ID:** `writing-make-references-unambiguous` · **Scope:** sentence–paragraph; general prose.

- **Principle:** Make pronouns, demonstratives, names, and recurring terms point to something the reader can identify.
- **Useful when:** “This,” “it,” “they,” or a changed label leaves several plausible referents.
- **Source leads:** [WB](#source-register) for cohesion; [GB](#source-register) for distinguishing voices and connecting statements.
- **Boundary and tradeoff:** Resolves reference rather than vocabulary difficulty in P10; repeating a noun may be clearer than elegant variation, while intentional uncertainty about identity needs a distinct purpose.

### P05. Give Each Paragraph a Main Job

**Draft article:** [writing-give-each-paragraph-a-main-job.md](../../content/patterns/writing-give-each-paragraph-a-main-job.md)

**Proposed ID:** `writing-give-each-paragraph-a-main-job` · **Scope:** paragraph; general prose.

- **Principle:** Shape a paragraph around a recognizable function such as establishing a scene, explaining a mechanism, or advancing a reason.
- **Useful when:** Paragraphs feel like arbitrary blocks or combine several unfinished lines of thought.
- **Source leads:** [WB](#source-register) for section framing and coherence; [CL](#source-register) for larger writing tools.
- **Boundary and tradeoff:** Concerns a paragraph's local function, while G02 compares whole-work structures; dialogue, associative prose, and deliberate fragments need not follow a topic-sentence formula.

### P06. Signal the Real Relationship

**Draft article:** [writing-signal-the-real-relationship.md](../../content/patterns/writing-signal-the-real-relationship.md)

**Proposed ID:** `writing-signal-the-real-relationship` · **Scope:** sentence–section; general prose.

- **Principle:** Use transitions and surrounding syntax to show whether an idea adds, contrasts, explains, qualifies, or follows from another.
- **Useful when:** Readers must guess why the next statement is there, or “therefore” conceals a missing inference.
- **Source leads:** [GB](#source-register) for connecting parts; [WB](#source-register) for cohesion.
- **Boundary and tradeoff:** Signals an existing relationship; it cannot supply the missing evidence or reasoning addressed in P42, and explicit signposts can become intrusive when order already makes the relationship clear.

## 2. Precision and economy

The task is to make wording earn its place. Retain texture and nuance when they contribute to the intended experience. The positive-wording tip is covered in T01 within the foundations reference.

### P07. Choose the Specific Word

**Draft article:** [writing-choose-the-specific-word.md](../../content/patterns/writing-choose-the-specific-word.md)

**Proposed ID:** `writing-choose-the-specific-word` · **Scope:** word–sentence; general prose.

- **Principle:** Replace a broad or approximate label with the degree of specificity the reader needs to understand the distinction.
- **Useful when:** Words such as “thing,” “nice,” or “problem” leave the important property unnamed.
- **Source leads:** [CL](#source-register) for diction; [JM](#source-register) for word choice.
- **Boundary and tradeoff:** Concerns lexical accuracy, while P12 selects a revealing detail; more technical or narrower wording is worse if the reader cannot use it or the evidence does not support it.

### P08. Let the Verb Do the Work

**Draft article:** [writing-let-the-verb-do-the-work.md](../../content/patterns/writing-let-the-verb-do-the-work.md)

**Proposed ID:** `writing-let-the-verb-do-the-work` · **Scope:** word–sentence; general prose.

- **Principle:** Choose a verb that carries the intended action and remove modifiers that merely repeat or compensate for it.
- **Useful when:** Adverbs prop up weak verbs or explain a manner already established by the action and context.
- **Source leads:** [SK](#source-register) for the adverb discussion to verify; [CL](#source-register) for verb choices.
- **Boundary and tradeoff:** Familiar aliases include “avoid adverbs” and “use strong verbs”; keep modifiers that add distinct information, and do not replace an accurate ordinary verb with an exaggerated action.

### P09. Cut Redundancy

**Draft article:** [writing-cut-redundancy.md](../../content/patterns/writing-cut-redundancy.md)

**Proposed ID:** `writing-cut-redundancy` · **Scope:** phrase–whole work; general prose.

- **Principle:** Remove wording that repeats an already available meaning without adding emphasis, clarity, rhythm, or development.
- **Useful when:** A passage repeats its point in several forms but does not advance it.
- **Source leads:** [WB](#source-register) for concision; [CL](#source-register) for economy.
- **Boundary and tradeoff:** Extends beyond verb modifiers in P08; intentional repetition in P20 can serve memory or emotion, so judge function before deleting recurring words.

### P10. Choose Terms the Reader Can Use

**Draft article:** [writing-choose-terms-the-reader-can-use.md](../../content/patterns/writing-choose-terms-the-reader-can-use.md)

**Proposed ID:** `writing-choose-terms-the-reader-can-use` · **Scope:** word–section; explanatory and professional prose.

- **Principle:** Use ordinary language where it preserves meaning and introduce necessary specialist terms with enough explanation to make them usable.
- **Useful when:** Jargon or unexplained labels keep the intended audience outside the discussion.
- **Source leads:** [WB](#source-register) for reader-oriented style; [GB](#source-register) for voice in academic writing.
- **Boundary and tradeoff:** About shared vocabulary, unlike reference tracking in P04; expert readers may need an exact technical term, and removing it can obscure an important distinction.

## 3. Concrete experience

The task is to connect language with something the reader can perceive, imagine, or infer. Narrative nonfiction examples must remain faithful to the available record.

### P11. Show What Matters, Summarize the Rest

**Draft article:** [writing-show-what-matters-summarize-the-rest.md](../../content/patterns/writing-show-what-matters-summarize-the-rest.md)

**Proposed ID:** `writing-show-what-matters-summarize-the-rest` · **Scope:** paragraph–whole work; fiction and factual narrative.

**Search aliases:** Show, Don't Tell; Dramatize What Matters; Alternate Scene and Summary Deliberately.

- **Principle:** Choose which moments deserve expanded attention, render them through action, perception, and dialogue, and summarize intervals whose main purpose is to connect or contextualize them.
- **Useful when:** The text announces important emotions or changes without making them tangible, or spends as much space on routine intervals as on consequential encounters.
- **Source leads:** [BU](#source-register) for showing and telling; [LG](#source-register) for narrative control; [JM](#source-register) for nonfiction arrangement.
- **Boundary and tradeoff:** This is the primary treatment of dramatization, narrative duration, and scene-versus-summary pacing. P16 removes explanatory commentary after the evidence is already present; P37 chooses unit boundaries. Summary can itself be vivid, and factual reconstruction must remain supported.

### P12. Choose the Telling Detail

**Draft article:** [writing-choose-the-telling-detail.md](../../content/patterns/writing-choose-the-telling-detail.md)

**Proposed ID:** `writing-choose-the-telling-detail` · **Scope:** sentence–scene; general prose, especially narrative.

**Search aliases:** Replace Cliché with Observed Particulars; Choose a Revealing Detail.

- **Principle:** Select a distinctive, revealing particular in place of generic inventory or stock description, so the detail carries meaning specific to this subject.
- **Useful when:** A person or place has plenty of description but no distinctive impression, or a familiar expression substitutes for observing what is actually there.
- **Source leads:** [BU](#source-register) for characterization, setting, and comparison; [GS](#source-register) for close-reading investigation; [JM](#source-register) for diction and reporting.
- **Boundary and tradeoff:** Own the replacement of stock description with a revealing observation. Route lexical specificity to P07, empty phrasing to P09, and stale comparisons to P15. Sensory presence in P14 is a separate decision; one gesture does not prove an entire character.

### P13. Make Abstractions Tangible

**Draft article:** [writing-make-abstractions-tangible.md](../../content/patterns/writing-make-abstractions-tangible.md)

**Proposed ID:** `writing-make-abstractions-tangible` · **Scope:** paragraph–section; explanatory prose and essays.

- **Principle:** Connect an abstract claim to a concrete instance, consequence, or comparison the reader can inspect.
- **Useful when:** Readers can repeat a definition but cannot recognize what it means in practice.
- **Source leads:** [CL](#source-register) for movement between general and particular; [WB](#source-register) for reader-oriented explanation.
- **Boundary and tradeoff:** Explains concepts rather than staging a scene as in P11; an example illustrates a claim but does not alone establish its generality, which requires P42 and P44.

### P14. Engage the Relevant Senses

**Draft article:** [writing-engage-the-relevant-senses.md](../../content/patterns/writing-engage-the-relevant-senses.md)

**Proposed ID:** `writing-engage-the-relevant-senses` · **Scope:** sentence–scene; narrative and descriptive prose.

- **Principle:** Use the sensory information that matters to the observer and situation to make an experience physically legible.
- **Useful when:** Description is visually generic or disconnected from the body's experience of a place or event.
- **Source leads:** [BU](#source-register) for setting and imagery; [LG](#source-register) for narrative practice.
- **Boundary and tradeoff:** Sensory presence differs from the interpretive significance of P12; avoid a five-senses checklist, and in nonfiction include only supported perceptions with clear attribution where needed.

### P15. Use Metaphor to Reveal a Relationship

**Draft article:** [writing-use-metaphor-to-reveal-a-relationship.md](../../content/patterns/writing-use-metaphor-to-reveal-a-relationship.md)

**Proposed ID:** `writing-use-metaphor-to-reveal-a-relationship` · **Scope:** phrase–paragraph; general prose.

- **Principle:** Choose a comparison that illuminates a useful relationship while remaining aware of where the comparison stops fitting.
- **Useful when:** A concept is hard to grasp or a description lacks an intelligible shape.
- **Source leads:** [BU](#source-register) for comparison; [CL](#source-register) for expressive effects.
- **Boundary and tradeoff:** More specific than adding an example in P13; a vivid analogy is not evidence, and an unsuitable metaphor can quietly distort the reader's understanding.

### P16. Remove Explanatory Echoes

**Draft article:** [writing-remove-explanatory-echoes.md](../../content/patterns/writing-remove-explanatory-echoes.md)

**Proposed ID:** `writing-remove-explanatory-echoes` · **Scope:** sentence–scene; narrative and essays.

**Search aliases:** Leave Room for Inference; Trust the Reader.

- **Principle:** When the draft already supplies enough evidence for the intended inference, remove commentary that merely explains the same implication again.
- **Useful when:** A passage presents a revealing action, image, or exchange and immediately follows it with an unnecessary statement of what it means.
- **Source leads:** [GS](#source-register) for reader response; [LG](#source-register) for narrative control.
- **Boundary and tradeoff:** The revision deletes an interpretive gloss from already adequate material. P11 selects and renders material; P27 constructs an unspoken concern in dialogue; P09 cuts explicit repetition. Retain explanation when the inference is uncertain or necessary knowledge would otherwise be missing.

## 4. Rhythm and emphasis

The task is to shape how prose moves through time and attention. Sound choices depend on voice, genre, and the intended reading experience.

**Proposed ID:** `writing-vary-sentence-length-deliberately` · **Scope:** sentence–paragraph; general prose.

### P18. Put Weight at the End

**Proposed ID:** `writing-put-weight-at-the-end` · **Scope:** sentence–paragraph; general prose.

- **Boundary and tradeoff:** A local placement technique rather than the whole-work ending in G03; an instruction or direct answer may need its most important information first.

### P19. Use Parallel Form for Related Ideas

### P20. Repeat with a Purpose

**Proposed ID:** `writing-repeat-with-a-purpose` · **Scope:** phrase–section; general prose.

- **Principle:** Repeat words or structures when their verbal recurrence adds emphasis, orientation, rhythm, or emotional pressure.

- **Boundary and tradeoff:** Own verbal recurrence for emphasis, cohesion, and sound. P39 owns an image or object changing meaning across a work; P36 owns preparation and fulfillment. Distinguish these effects from functionless repetition in P09.

### P21. Punctuate for Pace and Meaning

**Proposed ID:** `writing-punctuate-for-pace-and-meaning` · **Scope:** sentence; general prose.

- **Principle:** Choose punctuation that makes grammatical relationships legible and shapes the pauses, continuities, or interruptions the passage needs.

- **Boundary and tradeoff:** Punctuation contributes more than breath marks; distinguish a purposeful fragment from an accidental ambiguity, and do not use this article as a substitute for a full grammar reference.

### P22. Break an Established Form for Emphasis

**Draft article:** [writing-break-an-established-form-for-emphasis.md](../../content/patterns/writing-break-an-established-form-for-emphasis.md)

**Proposed ID:** `writing-break-an-established-form-for-emphasis` · **Scope:** sentence–paragraph; general prose.

**Search aliases:** Establish a Pattern, Then Change It.

- **Principle:** Establish a recognizable syntactic or rhythmic form, then depart from it where the change should attract attention.
- **Useful when:** A decisive contrast or turn passes without emphasis even though the surrounding sentence forms could establish an expectation.
- **Source leads:** [CL](#source-register) for pattern and variation tools to verify; [GS](#source-register) for expectation through close reading.
- **Boundary and tradeoff:** Show an actual formal baseline and a marked departure from it. P19 creates grammatical correspondence; P20 creates verbal recurrence; P33 manages narrative information; P36 prepares later significance. Avoid turning this into a general article on surprise.

## 5. Voice, perspective, and dialogue

The task is to make perception and speech purposeful. Define viewpoint, narrator, distance, voice, and subtext as distinct concepts in the supporting glossary. Use G01 to compare how the individual tools combine into a voice.

### P23. Filter Through the Viewpoint

**Draft article:** [writing-filter-through-the-viewpoint.md](../../content/patterns/writing-filter-through-the-viewpoint.md)

**Proposed ID:** `writing-filter-through-the-viewpoint` · **Scope:** sentence–scene; fiction and attributed factual narrative.

- **Principle:** Select what a particular perceiver can know and is likely to notice, using their concerns and experience to govern what becomes salient.
- **Useful when:** A scene inventories everything in the room, or gives a viewpoint holder knowledge and observations unrelated to their experience or present concern.
- **Source leads:** [LG](#source-register) and [BU](#source-register) for point of view.
- **Boundary and tradeoff:** Change which details are available or salient, rather than merely changing adjectives. The same room seen by two people should demonstrate perceptual selection. P24 controls closeness, P25 signals viewpoint changes, and the voice guide in G01 compares broader stylistic choices; nonfiction needs support for attributed perceptions.

### P24. Control Narrative Distance

**Draft article:** [writing-control-narrative-distance.md](../../content/patterns/writing-control-narrative-distance.md)

**Proposed ID:** `writing-control-narrative-distance` · **Scope:** sentence–scene; narrative prose.

- **Principle:** Choose how closely the prose follows immediate perception and thought, and move between intimacy and overview with a readable purpose.
- **Useful when:** Important moments feel remote or every minor event receives exhausting interior attention.
- **Source leads:** [JG](#source-register) for distance terminology to verify; [LG](#source-register) and [BU](#source-register) for perspective.
- **Boundary and tradeoff:** Distance can change without changing the viewpoint holder in P25; closeness is not inherently superior, and reported interior experience in nonfiction requires an evidential basis.

### P25. Make Viewpoint Shifts Legible

**Draft article:** [writing-make-viewpoint-shifts-legible.md](../../content/patterns/writing-make-viewpoint-shifts-legible.md)

**Proposed ID:** `writing-make-viewpoint-shifts-legible` · **Scope:** scene–whole work; narrative prose.

- **Principle:** Establish who can perceive or know what, and give readers enough orientation when that organizing perspective changes.
- **Useful when:** Readers accidentally attribute one character's knowledge or thoughts to another.
- **Source leads:** [LG](#source-register) and [BU](#source-register) for point of view.
- **Boundary and tradeoff:** Familiar alias “avoid head-hopping” should not become a ban on multiple perspectives; P23 concerns viewpoint texture, and controlled omniscience has different permissions from a limited viewpoint.

### P26. Make Dialogue an Action

**Draft article:** [writing-make-dialogue-an-action.md](../../content/patterns/writing-make-dialogue-an-action.md)

**Proposed ID:** `writing-make-dialogue-an-action` · **Scope:** exchange–scene; fiction and documented narrative.

- **Principle:** Let a speech turn pursue something—reassurance, evasion, permission, dominance, understanding—and affect the next turn.
- **Useful when:** Characters exchange information but the relationship or situation remains inert.
- **Source leads:** [BU](#source-register) for dialogue and characterization; [GS](#source-register) for narrative investigation.
- **Boundary and tradeoff:** Concerns what speech does, while P27 concerns what it leaves unsaid; not every exchange needs open conflict, and nonfiction dialogue must stay faithful to its record.

### P27. Let Dialogue Carry Subtext

**Draft article:** [writing-let-dialogue-carry-subtext.md](../../content/patterns/writing-let-dialogue-carry-subtext.md)

**Proposed ID:** `writing-let-dialogue-carry-subtext` · **Scope:** exchange–scene; fiction and carefully interpreted factual narrative.

- **Principle:** Use the relationship between spoken words, context, and behavior to make an unspoken concern perceptible.
- **Useful when:** Characters state every motive or emotion directly and the exchange has no interpretive depth.
- **Source leads:** [BU](#source-register) for dialogue; [GS](#source-register) for inference through close reading.
- **Boundary and tradeoff:** A focused application of inference in P16; distinguish productive implication from obscurity, and mark interpretations of real speakers instead of claiming access to hidden motives.

### P28. Keep Speech Attribution Clear and Light

**Draft article:** [writing-keep-speech-attribution-clear-and-light.md](../../content/patterns/writing-keep-speech-attribution-clear-and-light.md)

**Proposed ID:** `writing-keep-speech-attribution-clear-and-light` · **Scope:** sentence–exchange; dialogue in narrative prose.

- **Principle:** Use unobtrusive tags and relevant action beats to identify speakers without repeatedly explaining the performance of their words.
- **Useful when:** Readers lose track of speakers or decorative attribution competes with the dialogue.
- **Source leads:** [SK](#source-register) for dialogue-tag advice to verify; [BU](#source-register) for dialogue presentation.
- **Boundary and tradeoff:** Familiar alias “said is enough”; P08 covers redundant modifiers more generally, and unusual speech verbs or manner details can earn their place when they communicate necessary information.

## 6. Narrative movement

The task is to make developments consequential. Goal-and-obstacle patterns are useful options; observational, associative, and other less conflict-driven narratives also need room in the collection. For pacing through expanded scenes and compressed intervals, use P11 in the concrete-experience family; do not author a second scene/summary article here.

### P29. Give the Scene a Want

**Draft article:** [writing-give-the-scene-a-want.md](../../content/patterns/writing-give-the-scene-a-want.md)

**Proposed ID:** `writing-give-the-scene-a-want` · **Scope:** scene; fiction and factual narrative.

- **Principle:** Make a scene's active desire, intention, or pressing concern legible so the reader knows what would matter to the person experiencing it.
- **Useful when:** A scene has activity but no discernible direction or stake in what happens next.
- **Source leads:** [BU](#source-register) for characterization and plot; [SK](#source-register) for plot and character.
- **Boundary and tradeoff:** Own the identification and presentation of a want, including quiet or contradictory wants. Leave resistance and costs to P30 and the consequential before/after shift to P34. The governing question in P35 operates across the work; nonfiction must distinguish reported desire from interpretation.

### P30. Make Resistance Matter

**Draft article:** [writing-make-resistance-matter.md](../../content/patterns/writing-make-resistance-matter.md)

**Proposed ID:** `writing-make-resistance-matter` · **Scope:** scene–sequence; narrative prose.

- **Principle:** Introduce a constraint, incompatible aim, or meaningful cost that changes what pursuing a character's want requires.
- **Useful when:** Success feels automatic or obstacles merely delay an outcome without changing its significance.
- **Source leads:** [BU](#source-register) for plot; [GS](#source-register) for investigating narrative development.
- **Boundary and tradeoff:** Own the design of constraints and costs, rather than a complete scene formula. P29 establishes the want; P32 interprets what a decision reveals about character; P34 locates the change. Resistance need not be physical danger or culminate in a revealing choice.

### P31. Make Events Cause Events

**Draft article:** [writing-make-events-cause-events.md](../../content/patterns/writing-make-events-cause-events.md)

**Proposed ID:** `writing-make-events-cause-events` · **Scope:** scene–whole work; narrative prose.

- **Principle:** Connect developments through consequences, responses, and constraints so later events arise intelligibly from earlier ones.
- **Useful when:** A synopsis reads as “and then” followed by another unrelated incident.
- **Source leads:** [BU](#source-register) for plot and structure; [GS](#source-register) for narrative close reading.
- **Boundary and tradeoff:** Causal design differs from merely signaling “because” in P06; coincidence and associative structures remain possible, and nonfiction must not invent causal connections to improve a plot.

### P32. Reveal Character Through Choice

**Draft article:** [writing-reveal-character-through-choice.md](../../content/patterns/writing-reveal-character-through-choice.md)

**Proposed ID:** `writing-reveal-character-through-choice` · **Scope:** scene–whole work; fiction and factual narrative.

- **Principle:** Let a consequential decision expose priorities, contradictions, or changes that a descriptive label cannot establish on its own.
- **Useful when:** A character is called brave, selfish, or conflicted but never faces a revealing alternative.
- **Source leads:** [BU](#source-register) for characterization; [SK](#source-register) for character development.
- **Boundary and tradeoff:** Uses pressure from P30 without requiring a moral test in every scene; a real person's single action does not establish a permanent trait, and character can also emerge through habits and perception.

### P33. Manage the Reader's Questions

**Draft article:** [writing-manage-the-readers-questions.md](../../content/patterns/writing-manage-the-readers-questions.md)

**Proposed ID:** `writing-manage-the-readers-questions` · **Scope:** paragraph–whole work; narrative prose and narrative explanation.

- **Principle:** Open an intelligible uncertainty, provide enough context to care, and time information so discovery sustains attention.
- **Useful when:** Readers are either confused about what matters or already know everything that would make them continue.
- **Source leads:** [GS](#source-register) for reader engagement; [CL](#source-register) for larger writing effects.
- **Boundary and tradeoff:** Includes curiosity, suspense, and dramatic irony as different arrangements of knowledge; factual instructions may require immediate answers, and withholding basic orientation is not automatically suspense.

### P34. Let the Scene Change Something

**Draft article:** [writing-let-the-scene-change-something.md](../../content/patterns/writing-let-the-scene-change-something.md)

**Proposed ID:** `writing-let-the-scene-change-something` · **Scope:** scene; narrative prose.

- **Principle:** Compare the situation before and after a scene, identify the consequential change, and locate the turn that produces it.
- **Useful when:** A scene could disappear without altering anything the reader carries forward.
- **Source leads:** [GS](#source-register) for narrative development; [BU](#source-register) for plot and structure.
- **Boundary and tradeoff:** Own the before/after comparison in knowledge, options, relationship, expectation, or understanding. Do not reteach the goals in P29 or obstacles in P30. A quiet perceptual change can qualify; P31 connects consequences between units.

The task is to choose a shape that develops the material. Chronology, causal progression, thematic grouping, braided strands, and associative arrangements can each be useful. G02 compares structural routes; G03 helps choose an ending using the specific tools below.

## 8. Argument and persuasion

The task is to make a claim assessable and worth considering. Rhetorical force should remain answerable to evidence, fair representation, and stated uncertainty.

### P40. Make the Main Claim Findable

**Draft article:** [writing-make-the-main-claim-findable.md](../../content/patterns/writing-make-the-main-claim-findable.md)

**Proposed ID:** `writing-make-the-main-claim-findable` · **Scope:** paragraph–whole work; argumentative and professional prose.

- **Principle:** State the proposition the reader is being asked to accept clearly enough to distinguish it from a topic, question, quotation, or list of facts.
- **Useful when:** Readers can identify the subject and supporting material but cannot say which position or proposed answer belongs to the writer.
- **Source leads:** [GB](#source-register) for taking a position; [WB](#source-register) for framing.
- **Boundary and tradeoff:** Own the identification and expression of the claim. The structure guide in G02 compares ordering options; the existing Minto Pyramid article owns the answer–reasons–evidence hierarchy. An exploratory essay may discover its answer gradually; a findable claim need not always be its first sentence.

### P41. Enter the Conversation Fairly

**Draft article:** [writing-enter-the-conversation-fairly.md](../../content/patterns/writing-enter-the-conversation-fairly.md)

**Proposed ID:** `writing-enter-the-conversation-fairly` · **Scope:** paragraph–section; argumentative prose.

- **Principle:** Represent the relevant existing position accurately enough that readers can understand what the new contribution answers, extends, or disputes.
- **Useful when:** An argument seems to attack nobody in particular, caricatures an opponent, or hides the issue under discussion.
- **Source leads:** [GB](#source-register) for conversation, summary, quotation, and response.
- **Boundary and tradeoff:** Establishes the starting conversation; P43 addresses a serious challenge to the writer's own claim, and fair representation does not require giving every fringe claim equal space.

### P42. Connect Evidence to the Claim

**Draft article:** [writing-connect-evidence-to-the-claim.md](../../content/patterns/writing-connect-evidence-to-the-claim.md)

**Proposed ID:** `writing-connect-evidence-to-the-claim` · **Scope:** paragraph–section; explanatory and argumentative prose.

- **Principle:** Write the inferential bridge between evidence and conclusion, exposing the assumption that makes the evidence relevant to this particular claim.
- **Useful when:** A statistic, quotation, or example is present, but the reader cannot inspect how it supports the conclusion.
- **Source leads:** [GB](#source-register) for quoting and responding; [WB](#source-register) for coherent explanation.
- **Boundary and tradeoff:** Own the inferential bridge, not the architecture of the whole argument. Link to the Minto Pyramid for that structure. P44 controls the warranted reach and confidence of a conclusion, while P46 marks source status in the prose; a transition in P06 cannot supply a missing inference.

### P43. Answer the Strongest Objection

**Draft article:** [writing-answer-the-strongest-objection.md](../../content/patterns/writing-answer-the-strongest-objection.md)

**Proposed ID:** `writing-answer-the-strongest-objection` · **Scope:** paragraph–whole work; argumentative prose.

- **Principle:** Address a reasonable challenge that would materially affect the claim, revising or qualifying the position where the challenge succeeds.
- **Useful when:** A piece is persuasive only to readers who already agree or omits an obvious competing explanation.
- **Source leads:** [GB](#source-register) for the naysayer and forms of response.
- **Boundary and tradeoff:** Goes beyond representing the discussion in P41; avoid performative objections that are easy to dismiss, and concede a decisive objection rather than treating every challenge as something to overcome.

### P44. Match the Claim to the Evidence

**Draft article:** [writing-match-the-claim-to-the-evidence.md](../../content/patterns/writing-match-the-claim-to-the-evidence.md)

**Proposed ID:** `writing-match-the-claim-to-the-evidence` · **Scope:** sentence–whole work; factual and argumentative prose.

- **Principle:** Adjust the claim's quantifiers, confidence, and stated conditions to match what the evidence actually warrants.
- **Useful when:** The evidence is relevant and its inferential connection is understandable, but a limited example becomes an "always," a possibility becomes certainty, or excessive hedging weakens a supported conclusion.
- **Source leads:** [GB](#source-register) for qualified responses; [WB](#source-register) for ethics of clarity.
- **Boundary and tradeoff:** Own the strength, scope, and conditions of the conclusion. P42 exposes why evidence is relevant; P46 marks its provenance. Use worked examples in which those other tasks are already satisfied, so qualification is the distinct revision rather than decorative caution.

### P45. Explain What Is at Stake

**Draft article:** [writing-explain-what-is-at-stake.md](../../content/patterns/writing-explain-what-is-at-stake.md)

**Proposed ID:** `writing-explain-what-is-at-stake` · **Scope:** paragraph–whole work; explanatory and argumentative prose.

- **Principle:** Show whom the question affects and what understanding, action, or consequence depends on the answer.
- **Useful when:** A technically coherent piece leaves readers asking why the issue deserves their attention.
- **Source leads:** [GB](#source-register) for significance; [WB](#source-register) for framing the reader's problem.
- **Boundary and tradeoff:** Establishes relevance rather than the fictional resistance in P30; make consequences proportionate and supported instead of inflating urgency or appealing only to fear.

### P46. Separate the Record from the Interpretation

**Draft article:** [writing-separate-the-record-from-the-interpretation.md](../../content/patterns/writing-separate-the-record-from-the-interpretation.md)

**Proposed ID:** `writing-separate-the-record-from-the-interpretation` · **Scope:** sentence–whole work; factual prose, including memoir.

- **Principle:** Mark in the prose who observed, remembered, reported, quoted, or inferred each consequential statement, so readers can distinguish the record from its interpretation.
- **Useful when:** An account reads smoothly but conceals whether a detail comes from a record, another person, uncertain recollection, reconstruction, or authorial inference.
- **Source leads:** [GB](#source-register) for quoting and voice distinctions; [JM](#source-register) for reporting; [WB](#source-register) for ethical style.
- **Boundary and tradeoff:** Own attribution and provenance in the written account. Link to existing reasoning and debrief models rather than reteaching them. P42 explains an inferential bridge; P44 limits claim strength. Do not imply that reconstructed dialogue or private thoughts are documented.

## 9. Drafting and revision

The task is to develop a process that exposes problems and supports purposeful changes. These practices should work without prescribing one temperament, daily quota, or universal sequence.

### P47. Write Toward a Reader and an Effect

**Draft article:** [writing-write-toward-a-reader-and-an-effect.md](../../content/patterns/writing-write-toward-a-reader-and-an-effect.md)

**Proposed ID:** `writing-write-toward-a-reader-and-an-effect` · **Scope:** planning–revision; general prose.

- **Principle:** Choose a plausible reader and an intended experience or change in understanding so writing decisions have something concrete to serve.
- **Useful when:** Advice pulls in conflicting directions or a draft tries to satisfy every imaginable audience.
- **Source leads:** [SK](#source-register) for the ideal-reader discussion identified by the author guide; [WB](#source-register) for readers' expectations.
- **Boundary and tradeoff:** Defines intention rather than gathering actual responses in P54; an imagined reader is a working model, not evidence that every real reader will react alike.

### P48. Separate Drafting from Editing

**Draft article:** [writing-separate-drafting-from-editing.md](../../content/patterns/writing-separate-drafting-from-editing.md)

**Proposed ID:** `writing-separate-drafting-from-editing` · **Scope:** drafting process; general prose.

- **Principle:** When evaluation blocks discovery, give generating material and judging it distinguishable periods of attention.
- **Useful when:** The writer keeps polishing the first paragraph and never discovers the rest of the piece.
- **Source leads:** [BU](#source-register) for process and freewriting investigation; [JM](#source-register) for drafting and revision.
- **Boundary and tradeoff:** A process option rather than a ban on revising while composing; P52 orders kinds of revision after material exists, and some writers discover their next thought through local revision.

### P49. Read Like a Writer

**Draft article:** [writing-read-like-a-writer.md](../../content/patterns/writing-read-like-a-writer.md)

**Proposed ID:** `writing-read-like-a-writer` · **Scope:** learning and revision; general prose.

- **Principle:** Connect an effect experienced as a reader to observable choices in the text, then test a comparable move in original material.
- **Useful when:** Reading inspires admiration but does not translate into usable craft knowledge.
- **Source leads:** [GS](#source-register) for close reading; [BU](#source-register) for craft examples; [SK](#source-register) for reading and writing.
- **Boundary and tradeoff:** Studies technique rather than reproducing a distinctive author's phrasing or mannerisms; one successful passage offers a possibility rather than proving a universal rule.

### P50. Capture Material Before You Need It

**Draft article:** [writing-capture-material-before-you-need-it.md](../../content/patterns/writing-capture-material-before-you-need-it.md)

**Proposed ID:** `writing-capture-material-before-you-need-it` · **Scope:** observation and preparation; general prose.

- **Principle:** Keep retrievable notes of observations, questions, phrases, and sources so a later draft can draw on particulars rather than vague recollection.
- **Useful when:** The writer has a subject but cannot recover the examples, sensory detail, or provenance needed to develop it.
- **Source leads:** [BU](#source-register) for process and journal-keeping investigation; [JM](#source-register) for reporting.
- **Boundary and tradeoff:** Supports P12 and P46 without making a notebook a requirement; distinguish direct quotations from paraphrases at capture time, and avoid collecting indefinitely instead of writing.

### P51. Reverse-Outline the Draft

**Draft article:** [writing-reverse-outline-the-draft.md](../../content/patterns/writing-reverse-outline-the-draft.md)

**Proposed ID:** `writing-reverse-outline-the-draft` · **Scope:** revision; paragraph–whole work; general prose.

- **Principle:** Describe what each existing unit actually does so missing steps, repetition, and structural mismatches become visible.
- **Useful when:** A draft feels wrong but rereading the prose does not reveal where its organization fails.
- **Source leads:** [WB](#source-register) for coherence and framing; [GB](#source-register) for substantial revision; verify direct treatment of reverse outlining before attribution.
- **Boundary and tradeoff:** A diagnostic procedure distinct from choosing an order in G02; allow units with emotional or associative functions, rather than forcing every paragraph into a claim-and-proof outline.

### P52. Revise from Structure to Sentences

**Draft article:** [writing-revise-from-structure-to-sentences.md](../../content/patterns/writing-revise-from-structure-to-sentences.md)

**Proposed ID:** `writing-revise-from-structure-to-sentences` · **Scope:** revision; general prose.

- **Principle:** Address purpose, selection, and arrangement before spending extensive effort polishing passages likely to change or disappear.
- **Useful when:** A beautifully edited draft still has a weak argument, a missing scene, or an unworkable order.
- **Source leads:** [JM](#source-register) and [BU](#source-register) for revision; [GB](#source-register) for substantial revision.
- **Boundary and tradeoff:** Uses diagnosis from P51 but covers prioritizing interventions; the movement is iterative rather than rigid, because a difficult sentence can reveal an underlying conceptual problem.

### P53. Read Aloud to Hear the Draft

**Draft article:** [writing-read-aloud-to-hear-the-draft.md](../../content/patterns/writing-read-aloud-to-hear-the-draft.md)

**Proposed ID:** `writing-read-aloud-to-hear-the-draft` · **Scope:** revision; sentence–whole work; general prose.

- **Principle:** Listen to a draft to expose awkward phrasing, unintended repetition, unclear attribution, or rhythms that silent familiarity hides.
- **Useful when:** A passage looks acceptable but sounds unnatural, monotonous, or difficult to follow.
- **Source leads:** [LG](#source-register) for sound; [CL](#source-register) for craft habits; verify the particular exercise before attribution.
- **Boundary and tradeoff:** Diagnoses effects addressed by P17–P21 without requiring conversational prose; text-to-speech is an alternative listening method, and silent reading, accessibility needs, and intended medium also matter.

### P54. Seek Feedback Against the Intended Effect

**Draft article:** [writing-seek-feedback-against-the-intended-effect.md](../../content/patterns/writing-seek-feedback-against-the-intended-effect.md)

**Proposed ID:** `writing-seek-feedback-against-the-intended-effect` · **Scope:** revision and reader testing; general prose.

- **Principle:** Ask suitable readers where their understanding, attention, or response changed, then use that evidence to choose revisions aligned with the work's purpose.
- **Useful when:** General praise, conflicting prescriptions, or defensive discussion makes feedback hard to use.
- **Source leads:** [LG](#source-register) for writing groups; [SK](#source-register) for the ideal reader; [BU](#source-register) for revision.
- **Boundary and tradeoff:** Tests the working intention in P47; reader experience is evidence, while a reader's proposed fix is a hypothesis, and consensus is not a requirement for an effective or distinctive piece.

## Overview and capstone guides

These three reclassified topics remain part of the collection as planned concept/reference articles. Give them comparative examples and primary links rather than repeating the patterns' full teaching or imposing a pattern-style worksheet.

### G01. How Voice Emerges from Choices

**Proposed ID:** `writing-how-voice-emerges-from-choices` · **Planned kind:** concept/reference · **Scope:** sentence–whole work; general prose and dialogue.

- **Purpose:** Show how patterns of attention, diction, syntax, sound, attitude, and omission combine into an authorial, narratorial, or character voice.
- **Useful when:** The writer wants a more recognizable voice but treats it as an accent, a catchphrase, or an abstract quality independent of concrete choices.
- **Coverage and source leads:** Compare original versions of one passage using [LG](#source-register) for narrative voice and [GB](#source-register) for voice in academic prose as research leads.
- **Primary tools:** P07 and P10 for diction; P17–P22 for sound and form; P23–P25 for perspective; P26–P28 for dialogue.
- **Boundary and tradeoff:** Explain combinations and their effects; let the linked patterns own their individual interventions. Changing viewpoint selection is not merely changing adjectives. Avoid stereotypes or the implication that every narrator must sound like the author.

### G02. Choosing a Structure

**Proposed ID:** `writing-choosing-a-structure` · **Planned kind:** concept/reference · **Scope:** section–whole work; general prose.

- **Purpose:** Compare chronological, causal, question-led, thematic, braided, and answer-first routes according to the reader's needs and the work's intended experience.
- **Useful when:** Interesting material has no workable order, or the draft follows the writer's discovery sequence without helping readers understand its parts.
- **Coverage and source leads:** Use contrasting outlines of the same original material; investigate document framing in [WB](#source-register) and arrangement in [JM](#source-register).
- **Primary tools:** P01 for local information order; P31 for causal development; P33 for information timing; P35 for a governing question; P38 for background placement; P40 for making a claim identifiable. Link to the existing [Minto Pyramid](../../content/patterns/minto-pyramid.md) for the answer–reasons–evidence hierarchy.
- **Boundary and tradeoff:** Help select a route and compare costs rather than retelling every ordering technique. Answer-first structure suits some practical messages; chronology, uncertainty, and associative movement can serve other purposes. P51 diagnoses the actual order through a reverse outline.

### G03. Designing an Ending

**Proposed ID:** `writing-designing-an-ending` · **Planned kind:** concept/reference · **Scope:** whole work; general prose.

- **Purpose:** Compare closure, resonance, implication, and deliberate openness to choose an ending consistent with the work's development.
- **Useful when:** A piece stops abruptly, mechanically summarizes itself, or asserts a resolution that its material has not earned.
- **Coverage and source leads:** Compare alternative endings for an original story and an essay; investigate narrative endings in [GS](#source-register), nonfiction structure in [JM](#source-register), and framing in [WB](#source-register).
- **Primary tools:** P35 for the central inquiry, P36 for payoff, P39 for changing motif meaning, P40 for an argumentative answer, and P44 for the limits of a supported conclusion.
- **Boundary and tradeoff:** Synthesize choices without becoming a second payoff or motif article. An open ending can sharpen a literary question; a factual argument still needs an inspectable answer or clear account of what remains unknown. Do not force a moral or complete closure onto every work.

## Foundations tip

### T01. State the Useful Positive

**Planned home:** a `state-the-useful-positive` section within `writing-foundations` · **Scope:** sentence–paragraph; explanatory and professional prose.

- **Principle:** When it helps the reader act or understand, say what is true or wanted rather than making them reconstruct it from a vague negation.
- **Useful when:** Avoidable double negatives or indirect instructions obscure a direct meaning, as in “do not fail to include” where “include” suffices.
- **Source leads:** [CL](#source-register) for sentence choices; [WB](#source-register) for clarity and concision.
- **Boundary and tradeoff:** This is a concise wording tip, not a separate field-guide article or a ban on negation. “Did not consent,” “not proven,” and “not uncommon” can convey distinctions that a positive restatement changes. Cross-link P02, P09, and P10 where appropriate, while keeping its main treatment in the foundations reference.

## Article standard and production order

Begin with P08, P11, and P36 to test sentence-level choice, the merged dramatization/pacing treatment, and whole-work technique. Draft the remaining patterns by family while retaining their cross-family links. Write G01–G03 once the tools they synthesize have sufficiently clear examples and boundaries. Include T01 when authoring the foundations reference.

A finished pattern should ordinarily contain a recognizable opening problem, a precise move, an explanation of its effects, original worked examples, a deliberate alternative or counterexample, meaningful limits, and useful neighboring links. Aim for approximately 800–1,200 words where warranted, but permit shorter treatments rather than stretching a narrow technique to a quota. Keep exercises separate from explanations. Add a practice only when a concrete comparison or procedure helps; choose diagrams for time, structure, perspective, and information relationships when they explain more clearly than prose.

**Opening and example standard:** The introduction should stand on its own: explain the recognizable writing problem, why this pattern can help, and when to apply it before developing an illustrative scene. An example-led opening is optional and must make its usefulness clear without requiring prior reading. Establish the people, relationships, and stakes needed for each example at the point of use. Recurring names or settings do not imply that the reader knows an earlier article. Carry that orientation through the article: explain what a choice accomplishes before introducing a developed passage, then examine what the revision gains or loses. Prefer consequential choices over isolated, mechanical wording drills; use tables only when they clarify a relationship better than connected prose. Changing examples should not silently carry characters, stakes, or events over from another article.

The three thematic guides have a different contract: compare tools and alternatives, show how choices interact, and link to the primary treatments. They need not prescribe one move or repeat the tools' exercises. T01 stays a compact foundations section with contrasting examples of avoidable and necessary negation.

Give each major worked transformation a primary article. P11 owns scene/summary selection, dramatization, and their pacing costs; P12 owns replacing stock description with revealing observation; P16 removes explanatory commentary only after the evidence is sufficient. A neighboring article should demonstrate a different failure and revision rather than repeat the same before/after passage. Compare the actual examples and practices after the first representative drafts and consolidate again if their teaching is interchangeable.

Before expanding an entry, verify the source passages needed, check titles and aliases for unsupported attribution, and check existing articles for the same intervention. Record edition-specific references only after inspection and cite supported claims where they occur. Write original explanations and teaching examples rather than reconstructing a book's distinctive examples or chapter sequence. This editorial revision does not establish passage-level source verification.

For repository integration, reuse existing article and nested-collection formats. Keep research annotations separate from content frontmatter; proposed metadata does not establish that new schema fields exist. The guides should use the existing concept/reference treatment, and T01 belongs within foundations. Draft content should use the repository's draft treatment until publication is requested. Later validate entity IDs, collection membership, aliases, internal links, rendering, and any authored practices using existing tools.

## Coverage and extension boundaries

The 54 patterns, three thematic guides, and foundations tip retain coverage of orientation, diction, description, sensory and conceptual grounding, rhythm, perspective, voice, dialogue, characterization through choices, plot movement, pacing, larger structure, argument, factual attribution, and revision. A pattern can appear in several reader routes while keeping one primary home.

Later extensions or supporting references can address full grammar and usage, sustained humor and comic timing, research interviewing and specialist fact-checking procedures, genre-specific plotting systems, long-form character-arc taxonomies, poetry and prosody, screenplay conventions, multilingual style, translation, and publishing strategy. Maintain explicit scope rather than describing this as every writing technique that exists.

A pattern is ready when its move is distinct, its examples make the effect inspectable, its source claims are verified, and its limits let a writer choose a different technique intelligently. A guide is ready when its comparisons help choose among tools without repeating their teaching. The collection is ready when the articles and companions form a coherent, navigable repertoire without dangling promises or unsupported universal rules.
