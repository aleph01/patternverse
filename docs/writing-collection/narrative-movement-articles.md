## Teaching boundaries and examples

### P29 — Immediate concern

Tessa, a former box-office worker at a closing cinema, visits the manager on the day staff return their keys. An initial observational passage establishes the emptied building but leaves her local purpose unstated. A second passage gives her a prepared list of seventeen colleagues and a request to retain the projector for one last staff screening. The writer’s reason for introducing the cinema differs from Tessa’s reason for visiting the office. Details from the atmospheric account can remain; the request changes their relation to her present concern.

The article distinguishes the immediate request from a broader wish to preserve belonging. Neither has to be declared immediately or concealed as a matter of principle. A want can emerge, change, or coexist with uncertainty about its personal significance. Other characters can have aims beyond the main plot, and the manager can agree without the scene requiring invented resistance.

A separate fictional scene gives Inez a quiet intention: to remain beside a tidal inlet until the water reaches a step after a week of hosting visitors. Wanting that interval alone can coexist with hoping for a safe-arrival message. The scene need not become a contest, and the article acknowledges observational, associative, and collective alternatives to organizing a passage around an individual want.

### P30 — Relevant resistance

Rina plans an oral-history gathering and a printed booklet. The affordable upstairs room cannot serve two participants; the available ground-floor hall consumes the printing budget. Alternatives alter the form or promises of the project. Further development can come from understanding the same difficulty rather than accumulating unrelated obstructions. The hall manager and participants can have legitimate concerns of their own, while the inaccessible arrangement remains the constraint.

A separate fictional scene gives Mika an opportunity to send an apology but no promise of a meeting or reply. The other person’s boundary remains a limit; persistence is not guaranteed to remove it. The article permits costly success, proportional difficulty, and scenes without opposition. It establishes what a pursuit must contend with rather than interpreting every response as a character verdict.

### P31 — Consequences connecting developments

Leah accepts an urgent atlas repair to fund a wedding journey. Her unannounced use of the shared bench sends Emre to another workshop, where an existing possibility of permanent space reopens. He chooses to move, and Leah uses her travel savings to cover rent alone. A short chronological outline leaves these relations unspecified; a developed account supplies the changed conditions and responses.

The article distinguishes conditions from decisions, a trigger from longer preparation, causal sequence from presentation order, and genuine relationships from connective wording. Alternative responses remain possible, chance can enter, and the story need not distribute punishment and reward. Associative and observational structures remain legitimate. Factual succession must not be presented as established causation without a basis.

### P32 — What a decision reveals

Iris directs a theatre company that can afford one performance. A reviewer can attend Friday, but the originally scheduled Saturday is the day leading actor Malik can perform. An understudy is ready. Keeping either date preserves something valuable and gives something up. The article develops keeping Saturday, the handling of a possible Friday decision, and a request for an alternative review arrangement that cannot be assumed to erase costs.

What Iris knows, why she acts, how she tells people, and what follows can change the interpretation. Keeping Saturday might express care or conceal fear of review. A separate gardener, Ed, asks advice after dismissing his neighbour’s planning; his later response can complicate an apparent change. Neither act establishes a permanent trait. Available alternatives, freedom, information, and the chosen viewpoint limit what readers can infer.

### P33 — The reader’s evolving need to know

An invented island uses mail boats. Sera brings a glassworks letter while her brother Eno prepares to leave after assuming his apprenticeship application was refused. An initially opaque envelope passage gives way to one that supplies the application and departure context while preserving uncertainty about the answer. Revealing acceptance can then change the question to whether he stays. Following Eno while readers know more illustrates dramatic irony without a further secret.

The article distinguishes curiosity, suspense, reader–character knowledge differences, and avoidable confusion. Answers need room to matter; open meaning differs from an omitted outcome. Viewpoint limits, readily available communication, service instructions, and factual attribution constrain useful withholding. No mandatory cliffhanger, repeated secret, or universal reader reaction is assumed.

### P34 — The situation a scene leaves behind

Mara is leaving a band after four years. Finn assumes a shorter set and changed rehearsals can keep her, while she has booked a room to hear her own songs independently. His change appears when he removes her from the summer bookings and notes that he should ask when she is playing. The turn changes an arrangement and his stance towards her work without establishing permanent harmony or her future success.

Alternative versions let Finn remain unconvinced while the reader newly understands his refusal to hear her. The article distinguishes the loudest moment from the consequential turn, different participants’ changes, reader-only recognition, and outcomes later scenes must inherit. Quiet changes and purposeful stability remain available. Nonfiction cannot invent an instantaneous realization or decisive gesture.

### Family boundaries

P29 owns the identification and presentation of an immediate concern. P30 addresses relevant constraints and costs; P31 connects developments through consequences and responses; P32 interprets priorities made visible by a choice. P33 shapes the reader’s changing uncertainties; P34 locates a consequential change in situation or understanding. P35, in structure and coherence, concerns an inquiry across the work. P26 concerns purposes acting through speech turns; P23 concerns what a perceiver notices. No future article is linked as if already authored.

All examples are original fiction. Factual narrative must distinguish a reported purpose, an evidenced plan, and an interpretation of conduct. The craft pattern does not establish a real person’s intention or justify simplifying a mixed account into a neat goal.
